# Thirty Marks in Old Silver

*A scene from Aurefold — House Stonebear, Oath-hold, year 396 A.U.*
*Ser Alba and Rurik Stonebear, alone in the counting room, with a contract on the table.*

---

The counting room at Oath-hold was the coldest room in the fortress, because Torvald's grandfather had ruled that ledgers keep better in cold and no one since had asked whether the same was true of the men who read them.

Ser Alba had laid the papers out before Rurik arrived. That was her way: the ground chosen, the figures already facing him. Two ledgers, open. Between them, in a leather sleeve gone soft as cloth from two generations of careful hands, the Pass Compact.

"Read it," she said. "You will anyway."

He would anyway. Rurik drew the old page toward him and read it aloud, the whole of it, word for word, the way he read every sworn thing — not because he doubted his memory but because oaths were made of their exact words, and paraphrase was the first solvent.

"*The road over the high pass shall be kept open and guarded by House Stonebear in every season, and for this keeping the combine of the valley towns shall render the pass-toll, thirty marks in old silver, at midsummer, for so long as stone stands on stone.*"

Two generations old. His great-grandmother's seal at the bottom, and beneath it the combine's — a merchant mark, seven towns' worth of careful men, none of them born when it was struck.

"Thirty marks in old silver," Alba said. "Say what it was worth the year it was sworn."

"A garrison. The way-houses. The snow-crews." He knew the figures; she knew he knew; the recitation was part of the ground she'd chosen. "A fair year's profit over the cost."

"And say what it's worth now."

He looked at the ledgers instead of answering. The left one held the pass accounts: forty men in rotation, the two way-houses, timber for the snow-sheds, four horses lost in the black month, a bone-setter's fees. The right one held thirty marks in old silver, converted at this year's rate, sitting at the top of a column of nothing. The old silver had rotted the way all fixed things rot, slowly and then entirely — an eighth of what it had been, while the mountain charged the same as always.

"The combine's factors know it to the copper," Alba said. "They've known for thirty years. Every midsummer they send a boy up with the strongbox and a letter praising our honor, and every midsummer the letter is a little warmer, because every year we're a little poorer and their carriage is a little cheaper than anyone else's in the realm. They are not our partners, Rurik. They are farmers, and the crop is us."

"They keep their word," he said. "Thirty marks, at midsummer, without fail."

"Yes," she said. "It's an excellent word. I'd keep it too." She let that sit, because she was Alba, and knew the weight of a true thing set down without comment.

Somewhere below them the fortress went about its evening. His father would be at the Wall about now — not at the rite, just walking it, palm out, the way other men checked fences. Rurik had asked him once, at twenty, what the compact was costing. Torvald had set his hand flat on the table between them, the grip that wasn't a grip, the touch of a man finding his foundation, and said: *the word stands.* Not angrily. The way you'd say the mountain was north. There was no argument in his father because there was no question in him; the question had been asked once, long ago, weighed once, and buried with full honors. You could not fight a settled man. You could only bleed beside him.

"Say it, then," Rurik said. "You didn't build this room to show me arithmetic I keep in my sleep."

Alba took the second paper from her coat. Not old, this one. Her own square hand.

"Two readings," she said. "The first is clean. I'd stake my sword on it in front of your father, Hild, and the Oath-Stone itself." She set it down. "The compact swears the *road*. Open, guarded, in every season — the road, Rurik, the stones of it. It says nothing of carriage. Nothing of our wagons, our stables, our way-house beds, our sledge-crews that haul the combine's goods when their axles crack at the summit. We have given all of that away for free for sixty years because it grew up around the road like moss and nobody ever asked what was sworn and what was merely *done*. Read the words. Charge for the moss. The road stays open and guarded to the last letter, and the house eats."

He read the words. He turned them over aloud, twice — *kept open and guarded* — hunting the way he always hunted, for the trap, for the shame folded into the clever reading. And couldn't find it. Carriage was not the road. Beds were not the road. His great-grandmother had sworn stone and passage, not stables. The reading was honest. It was so honest it embarrassed him — sixty years of a house quietly starving beside revenue it had never promised away, because asking the question at all *smelled* of fee-hunting, and Stonebear did not permit itself even the smell.

"That's the first reading," he said slowly. "Fair. Say the second."

"The second," Alba said, and for the first time that evening she did not sound like the ground was hers, "is for a bad winter. *Guarded in every season.* When the mountain closes the pass itself — the black month, the avalanche weeks, when nothing moves and no guarding guards anything — the closure is the mountain's, not ours. Stand the garrison down those weeks. A quarter of the year's cost, gone. The road is open whenever the road *can* be open. The letter holds."

And there it was. He turned those words over too — *in every season* — and felt the difference in his hands the way his father would have felt it in stone. The first reading found room the oath had genuinely left. The second manufactured room by deciding what the oath had *meant*, which was the one thing the swearer no longer had a vote in. In every season. His great-grandmother had watched the black month from these windows. She had known exactly what she was promising to stand in, and had promised.

"The first reading is honest," he said.

"Yes."

"And the second is the first one's child."

"Yes," Alba said again, and did not dress it. That was the thing about her that frightened the family more than any argument: she would not lie to him even about her own knife. "The first step is honest, Rurik. They always are. That's how the path gets walked. I've watched honorable houses go down that road one clean reading at a time, and I've watched honorable houses starve to death at the trailhead with their word intact, and I have buried friends from both kinds, and I am telling you I don't know which grave I'd rather dig. I only know I'm tired of digging ours a spoonful a year."

"Why not take this to my father?"

"Because your father is at peace," she said. "And peace can't be argued with. I've tried for ten years. He grips the table, and the table wins." She straightened the papers, squaring the edges — a tell of her own, he realized, a soldier's habit of making the field tidy before the charge. "He's not wrong, Rurik. That's the worst of it. When outsiders swear at us for fools, they miss what your father knows in his bones: the only thing this house owns is that it has never bent. Not the pass, not the silver — the record. Bend once, even honestly, even by a reading a court of saints would bless, and every debtor we have spends the next decade probing for the second bend. He's guarding the record. I'm guarding the house. And I no longer believe they're the same thing, and I would give my sword arm to be wrong."

"And your own accounts?" Rurik said. He hadn't meant to say it tonight, but the ledgers were open and one column in the left book had been bothering him for two winters. "The shortfall in the black month, the year the north way-house burned. It's covered in the book. It isn't covered by anything I can find."

Alba stood very still, and then she almost smiled, which on her was a kind of surrender. "My mother's farm," she said. "And the year before that, my share from the border commission. There's no oath against a sword-hand being a fool with her own silver." She shrugged, iron-casual. "Somebody was going to pay for your father's peace. I preferred it be someone who volunteered."

The lamp between them had burned low. Rurik looked at the two papers — the compact in its soft leather, older than everyone living who was bound by it, and Alba's square hand beside it, the honest reading and its child. He thought of his father's palm on the Wall. He thought: *I could take the first page to him. The first page might even win. And in ten years, in some bad winter, a man who looks like me takes him the second page, and it is easier, because the first one was taken.*

His hand went to the table — and he caught it midway, hovering, halfway to his father's grip, and knew that if he set it down flat and hard he would be Torvald, settled at thirty, and the question would be buried in him too with full honors, and the house could starve nobly around his peace.

He laid the hand down flat. Gently. Not gripping anything.

"I'll take the first reading to my father," he said. "The carriage, the stables, the moss. Nothing the words don't leave us. I'll argue it to the letter, and if he grips the table, I'll put the compact in front of him and make him read me the sentence that forbids it."

"And the second?"

"Stays on your side of the table."

Alba gathered the page back into her coat, unhurried, unoffended. At the door she paused, and what she said then she said kindly, which was how she said her worst things.

"You'll come back for it in a bad winter."

Then she was gone, and Rurik sat alone in the coldest room in the fortress with the compact and the arithmetic, reading the old sentence over again, word by word by word — *for so long as stone stands on stone* — listening for the room in it, and hearing, for the first time in his life, how much silence a word can hold and still be kept.

*[End of scene.]*
