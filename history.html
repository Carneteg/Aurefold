History

Four hundred years of record, as the archive keeps it.

The reckoning of years

Years in the Banner-lands are counted in A.U. — after the Unification. The present is 396 A.U. The 400-year jubilee of the founding stands four years off, and every house is already preparing what it will say about the last four centuries.

The War of the Unification

Before the war there were no Ten Houses — only fractured principalities, cities, and clans fighting over the same exhausted land. The war swept the entire known world, and what one calls it tells you which record one trusts. The Chronicle of Victory calls it the War of the Unification — solemnly, the Great Unification — and "unification" makes a war sound like a merger. The Ash Fragments, which few cite aloud, call it the Divided Fire, and describe not a defense against an outside enemy but a civil war. The sources disagree about who won. That is the record's oldest wound.

What is beyond dispute is how it ended: on the cooling ground of the Ash Fields, in the year that was then set to nought, the lords of the war swore the Tenfold Compact and divided the land, the duty, and the grief among them. The peace it sealed has held — by one measure or another — for four hundred years, save four.

The four records

Everything above rests on four kinds of source, and they do not agree.

  • The Chronicle of Victory — the official record, kept at Crown-watch by House Blackcrest. Complete, consistent, and written by the side that kept the pen.
  • The Ash Fragments — burned, partial pages that survive from before and during the war. They contradict the Chronicle where they overlap with it, which is why so few of them overlap with it.
  • The Songs — what the people remember in verse. Songs cannot be recalled and corrected; they are sung wrong, in the same wrong way, in every tavern in the Banner-lands.
  • The Marginal Notes — small anonymous hands in the margins of official copies, written by readers who noticed something. The archive does not cite them. The archive has not erased them either.

The archive records the disagreement; it does not decide it.

The Living Archive

The Chronicle is not the only thing the archive keeps. Beneath the official record lie the fragments the officials could not enter cleanly — a letter that says what a hearing could not, a confession bound inside a devotional cover, a tribunal transcript that stops after sentencing. Each was written by someone with a reason to shape it, and each keeps its conflict rather than resolving it. These are ten of the houses' own hardest histories, and one the whole realm shares.

The summaries below are distilled and public. The full fragments — in the voices of the people who lived them — are in the Living Archive, with the openings free to read and the complete accounts kept for members.

Blackthorn · Intellect · 394 A.U.

The Deathbed of Lord Corvane Marrenhold

A lord takes eleven days to die while his household discovers eleven uses for the time. A daughter's marriage is negotiated beside the bed; a letter that could ruin one cousin makes him suddenly cooperative. No page proves murder — and every party can defend a motive that never renders their conduct harmless. Blackthorn prefers its corruption with a reason.

Ashbourne · Courage · 318 A.U.

The Charge That Arrived Too Soon

A captain disobeys a hold order on a dispatch that proves false. The charge kills her lieutenant — and may also have saved the army from encirclement. Afterward some call her reckless and some call her visionary, and she refuses to let either name make his death mean less. Success is the great corrupter of military truth.

Whitehart · Faith · 394 A.U.

The Vigil of the Sealed Testimony

A servant brings a testimony against a patron's kinsman; by custom it should be entered in the White Ledger by sundown. Instead it is sealed "pending clarification of conscience" while an abbess lies dying and the accused family funds the hospital. The house built to test the truth learns how easily patronage can teach a record to wait.

Stormrider · Unity · 188 A.U.

The Winter the Roads Closed

The passes freeze and the council sends one instruction: keep a passage open at all costs. It does not say which passage, or whose cost. Four people die waiting for a way that shepherds had already cut — unrecorded, because the men who cut it had no official mark to sign. Unity held the realm; it did not save the girl beneath the river.

Ravenshade · Information · 393 A.U.

The House of Opened Letters

A factor holds a packet of names one day before it goes on — long enough for a physician to reach the sick, and long enough for those who could afford to leave to be gone before the rest are marked. The delay saves and dooms in the same motion. The finest intrusion, he writes, leaves nothing to find.

Ironvale · Innovation · 392 A.U.

The Furnace That Would Not Stop

A furnace is run past every measure with a single structural support left out. When it fails, the house that measures everything cannot agree whose seal authorized the risk, or whether the man who forced the evacuation prevented a catastrophe or caused the injuries. The one event at their own forge is the one that will not be measured.

Blackcrest · The victor's history · 287 A.U.

The Victory at Red Hollow

The chamber that writes victory argues for days over a single inscription. The wording it adopts preserves the settlement — and refuses, deliberately, to grant the victor innocence. The record is a kind of power, and here it is spent, once, to keep a door open rather than close it.

Stonebear · Honor · 341 A.U.

The Oath at Three Spears

A shield-captain breaks a direct command to keep faith with a signal her house had made. It is insubordination and the preservation of custom at once, mercy and a dangerous precedent in the same act. Stonebear keeps its word past the point the word costs it everything — and cannot always tell which word it is bound to.

Tidebreaker · Knowledge · 366 A.U.

The Map That Refused the Coast

A chart is settled that honours the consent of an island people and, by the same omission, leaves uninformed sailors to the rocks. The house that charts all of it and shares almost none learns that a map is a claim: every chart after it must name its date, its maker, its purpose, and what it chose to leave out.

Phoenix · The future · 381 A.U.

The Children of Tomorrow

A planner delivers real improvement — cleaner water, warmer winters, schooled children — by controlling shelter, standing, and patronage until refusal is no longer practical. The future arrives. No one can prove it needed to arrive that way. The house that builds on scorched ground refuses, on principle, to look down.

The realm entire · 229 A.U.

The Year the Ten Banners Lowered

Crop failure, spoiled stores, sick herds and empty nets overlap with panic and concealment until no count is current and no house can meet its own need. No single house caused it; no single house solved it. The Common Keeping — cooperation without a crown — held the realm together for one year, and the eleventh seat in the Hall of Eleven stayed empty, and unexplained.

These fragments are contested by every tradition that keeps them. That is the point of keeping them.
