History

Rawness & mortality — a development document.

10 July 2026. Keyed to Lore Bible v26.5. STATUS: gate material — nothing in this document is canon until each marked item passes the author’s gate. Scene samples become canon only when placed; all proposals are marked CANON CANDIDATE / SCENE OPTION / TOO EARLY FOR CANON.

1. Brutality without losing mystery — the method in twelve rules

  1. Violence is the consequence of a philosophical choice. Every hard scene must trace back to a house’s virtue applied completely. If the philosophy is removed and the scene still works, the scene belongs to another book.
  2. The mystery makes the choice unjudgeable. The reader must never be able to verify whether the choice was right — only what it cost.
  3. The body is real. The grief is real. The explanation stays uncertain. Mystery lives in cause, context, or meaning — never in whether the death occurred.
  4. No executioners. Emberwold’s institutions kill through procedure, consensus, weather, and stillness. The knife is rare; the ledger, the vigil, the withheld bread are common. A death with no one to blame is rawer than a death with a villain.
  5. The victim has a name, a context, and mourners — or the scene is not ready. No corpse exists to decorate another character’s arc.
  6. Let the house be right. Every brutal act carries a defense a good person could make, made well, on the page. The target feeling: the reader half-agrees and hates it.
  7. Every significant death does at least two kinds of work (ends/warps a relationship; changes a succession; shifts a house balance; creates a debt or oath; changes how history is told; destroys a believed-in future; makes a survivor worse; makes a survivor stronger; splits into contradictory versions). One kind of work = a plot device; zero = shock.
  8. The shock test: reduce the graphic detail by half. If the scene weakens, cut it. If it strengthens, keep it quiet.
  9. Sexuality changes power — inheritance, legitimacy, alliance, information — or it stays offstage. Never used as a maturity costume; sexual violence is not a shortcut to adult tone.
  10. The elevens stay frozen. No death, grave, count, or relic may add a new eleven-motif. (This kills several obvious grave designs; see §5.)
  11. The ratio discipline: ordinary death outnumbers political death; political outnumbers mythic. Mythic deaths per book: one, at most two.
  12. The sources carry the brutality. Every major death eventually exists in up to four versions — official, private, sung, marginal — plus one material detail no version explains. The disagreement is the horror.

2. House-by-house: the cost of philosophy

(Format: virtue → brutal form → who pays → institutional mechanism → intimate scene → political consequence. All examples are SCENE OPTIONS unless marked.)

BLACKTHORN — intellect. Virtue: the best available plan. Brutal form: people as load-bearing variables; the plan requires that some who serve it must not know they are its cost. Who pays: the small, the peripheral, the loyally uninformed. Mechanism: the sealed order — plans compartmentalized so that no single officer can see whom the plan spends. Intimate scene: Wren updates a garrison roster and moves one name from a column to another; the name is her childhood fencing partner; the move is correct. Political consequence: a village burned to make a feint credible buys a decade of peace on that border — and Blackthorn is thanked by people who will never learn what the thanks cost. The brutality is that the decision may genuinely have been the best one.

ASHBOURNE — courage. Virtue: the daring leap. Brutal form: the ritual manufacture of dead nineteen-year-olds. Who pays: the young, and worse, the maimed — the half-fallen, whom the house honors less than corpses. Mechanism: the Wall of Names, carved by next of kin — grief converted into pride at the chisel’s edge, making refusal unthinkable. Intimate scene: a mother carves her son’s name and does the serifs beautifully, because the carving is the last thing she can do well for him; Roderick watches from the colonnade and does not come down. Political consequence: Ashbourne’s casualty culture makes it the realm’s shock force and its demographic wound; other houses schedule their crises for Ashbourne’s mourning seasons. Make the ritual beautiful, seductive, and morally unbearable at once.

WHITEHART — faith. Virtue: the tested voice. Brutal form: the quiet lethality of judgment — canon already states the house “does what the house does” with the judged-false; concretization below. Who pays: the judged-false and their families, who must keep vigil. Mechanism (CANON CANDIDATE — the Giving-Back): the judged-false are “given back to the silence”: walked to the high line above the vigil grounds at first snow and left, unbound, unharmed, with bread for one day. No one kills them. The mountain decides — or they walk away into namelessness. And their name is removed from the prayers (a mundane erasure that rhymes with PART 4’s principle: some silences are ordinary, and this one has a liturgy). Intimate scene: §7, Scene A/B. Political consequence: no house dares a false prophet, and no house forgives Whitehart for the certainty of its doubt; the realm sends Whitehart its visionaries and its problems in the same wagon.

STORMRIDER — unity. Virtue: the thousand moving as one. Brutal form: collective cruelty without a single cruel actor — the shunning. Who pays: dissenters, and small peoples of the Expanses whose separate customs read as division. Mechanism: the Closed Circle — no one shares fire, food, watch, or answer with the shunned; the group sincerely believes it has killed no one; winter does the rest. Intimate scene: a shunned herdsman’s daughter leaves a bowl outside the circle at night; Katla sees her do it and looks away — the smallest possible mutiny, the largest she can afford. Political consequence: the Binder’s peace is genuinely the reason the north hasn’t burned in forty years — and it is enforced by a method that leaves no marks and no guilty. The oppressors must sincerely believe they are preventing a greater war, because they may be.

RAVENSHADE — information. Virtue: knowing first. Brutal form: intimacy as instrument; the deaths are downstream and deniable. Who pays: lovers, confessors, the trusted; and the people whose secrets travel. Mechanism: the cultivated closeness — marriages, friendships, bedside vigils entered as positions; a confession heard in love and filed by craft. Intimate scene: Lyra, holding someone as they sleep, composing the report in her head and hating that the composing is involuntary — then realizing she cannot locate the moment the affection stopped or started being work. Political consequence: a war averted because Ravenshade knew a lord’s despair before his council did — and a friend of the house dead because warning him would have exposed the source. The violence is rarely Ravenshade’s hand; it is Ravenshade’s silence.

IRONVALE — innovation. Virtue: the improved world. Brutal form: the first generation pays the test costs of every improvement. Who pays: workers, early users, the first fifty. Mechanism: the proving contract — labor hired onto new works at better wages, the risk priced in and signed for, which makes the deaths legal and the guilt structural. Intimate scene: Senna re-reads a signed proving contract after a scaffold death and finds her own improvement in the clause wording — she wrote the sentence that made it clean. Political consequence: Ironvale’s cheaper steel makes war affordable for houses that could not afford it before; the smiths who wanted to end sieges shortened the peace. (Note: ghost-free by canon — Ironvale’s tragedy needs no mystery, per v26.)

BLACKCREST — the victor’s history. Virtue: the story that holds the peace. Brutal form: administrative violence — lives changed by an entry. Who pays: those whose truth is inconvenient; widows defending necessary lies; heirs whose legitimacy lives in a book. Mechanism: the register — inheritance, legitimacy, guilt, and honor all resolve to what is entered; and the Clean Page burns the alternatives nightly (canon). Intimate scene: canon already holds it — Harrowick’s widow, comforted for forty years for the wrong grief. Political consequence: a massacre entered as a riot keeps two houses at peace and one family’s murderers respectable; the peace is real; so are the murderers. Blackcrest must keep winning arguments (canon guard). Their violence is the realm’s cheapest — and the only kind that compounds interest for four hundred years.

STONEBEAR — honor. Virtue: the kept word. Brutal form: the letter that kills — guest-right shielding a murderer; a garrison freezing to guard a road no one can use (the Pass Compact, canon, in a killing winter). Who pays: the house’s own poor and its own soldiers; occasionally a victim whose killer stands under Stonebear’s roof. Mechanism: witnessed oath and guest-right — absolute, public, and therefore exploitable by anyone shameless enough to invoke them. Intimate scene: Torvald serves the evening meal, with his own hands, to a guest he knows is guilty, because the roof was promised before the guilt was known; his hands do not shake, and that is the horror. Political consequence: Stonebear’s absolutism makes it the realm’s arbiter and its mark; every treaty in Emberwold is worth more because Stonebear exists, and Stonebear is dying of exactly that. Both letter and spirit must remain defensible.

TIDEBREAKER — knowledge. Virtue: the verified chart. Brutal form: lethal patience — the deaths of waiting for certainty. Who pays: those downstream of withheld knowledge; expeditions sailing on the maps politics released rather than the maps that were true. Mechanism: the Deep Archive’s release protocol — knowledge graded by what the realm “can bear,” which makes withholding a procedure rather than a choice. Intimate scene: Maren annotates a famine projection “insufficient corroboration; hold one season” in her habitual neat hand — and the hand pauses, because she has just noticed the projection’s villages include the one her mother came from, and she holds it anyway. Political consequence: the withheld true document avoids one war and, a generation later, causes another among people who negotiated on the false one. Knowledge is not automatically good; Tidebreaker is what that sentence costs.

PHOENIX — the future. Virtue: the world worth handing on. Brutal form: the erasure of the living present — homes, graves, trades cleared for a future their owners won’t inhabit. Who pays: the old, the rooted, the ones whose skills the reform obsoletes. Mechanism: the resettlement writ — compensation paid, procedure followed, grief unpriced; objectors entered as “friends of the ash” (enemies of the future) in Phoenix’s own registers. Intimate scene: an old lime-burner is paid out fairly, resettled kindly, and dies within the year of nothing the physicians can name; Idun attends the funeral and takes notes on how to do the next clearance better, and the notes are genuinely good. Political consequence: New-Ash’s reforms demonstrably save the next generation — the granary yields prove it — which is why no argument against the current price ever wins. Phoenix must be the most hopeful and the most ruthless house in the same gesture.

5. The grave that should not exist

Compliance first (binding): all count-based designs are REJECTED — eleven bodies, “documents say ten,” ten marked graves and one unmarked — every one of them would mint a new eleven-motif, and the elevens are frozen (5B). The grave must disturb by quality, not arithmetic.

Location: the high east shoulder above the Bell of Silence’s vigil grounds — precisely the direction canon already loaded (“east, toward a grave that was not there,” 7.4.1). The grave pays off an anchor instead of spending a new one.

Discovery: the wet-spring slide (the same slide of ch. 9’s ordinary death — one event, two bodies, two centuries apart). The snow-crew digging out their own dead opens older ground.

The body: one. Adult, old burial, high-status wool (fine victor’s weave, wrong for a shepherd’s slope), buried face-down, outside the consecrated line by a stride’s width — and the hands are absent. Not severed and buried alongside: absent from the grave entirely, the wrist-ends clean.

The most uncomfortable detail: the grave was maintained. Under the slide-debris, the stones over it lie in courses — re-laid, more than once, generations apart. Someone has been tending, in secret, across centuries, a grave dug to face away from the prayers of the house that tends graves for a living. The horror is not the corpse. It is the continuity of care.

Explanations (all must stay standing): (1) an oath-breaker buried by the old custom — hands taken, face turned from the oath (Stonebear lore knows the custom; nothing says whose oath); (2) a plague- or crime-corpse reburied hastily by frightened people, tended later out of guilt; (3) a penitent’s self-erasure under some extinct rite — the fine wool a burial gift, not a rank; (4) deliberate plant — a grave built generations later to be found, disinformation with soil on it; (5) simple mis-dating — the wool is old, the grave older, the pairing an accident of two events.

Who wants it hidden / shown: Alaine orders it resealed and a vigil set (the dead are listened to, not exhumed — doctrine and self-protection in one order, and no one can tell which). The jubilee Herald wants it entered — recorded, dated, closed — because an unentered grave four years before the retelling is a loose page. Stonebear, if word travels, will quietly ask about the hands. Tidebreaker would pay for the wool alone. The villagers have already decided it is whatever the Songs need it to be by autumn.

Why it cannot prove the eleventh: nothing counts, nothing is named, no seal, no writing; every explanation above is fully mundane; and the one witness who might have pointed — the voice — says nothing further, which is canon-typical. The grave raises the question. It answers nothing.

What it does to Book One: retroactively loads Sela’s east-turn (the house remembers what she said at the trial after the ground opens); splits Whitehart along its deepest seam (listen to the dead vs. seal the dead — movement II’s fuel); gives the Herald a reason to stay; and hands Alaine an order to give whose two motives cannot be told apart. — CANON CANDIDATE, gated as a package.

6. Book One — first ten chapters (spine-compatible plan)

(Honors the locked spine: Whitehart only; Sela primary, Tomas secondary, three Alaine interludes; trial = ch. 1; east belongs to the trial; Herald = stranger engine; Garron never on the page. Fields per chapter: focus / intimate scene / philosophical choice / cost / death / double explanation / political consequence / relationship shifted / later contradicting source.)

  1. The Trial of the Goatherd (exists). Sela & Tomas. The east-turn. The wind that drops. — Contradicted later by Alaine’s interlude (the lamp was still before).
  2. The Morning After. Sela. Intimate: the house treats her differently at breakfast — reverence is a kind of exile. Choice: she underplays the hearing. Cost: her last ordinary morning. No death. Consequence: word starts downhill to the villages. Relationship: Sela–novices (peer to icon). Source: the Songs will say she fasted; she was only afraid to sit down.
  3. INTERLUDE I — Alaine (exists in register: The Woman Against the Stone). The burn-rule; the half-breath. Contradicts ch. 1 on the wind.
  4. The Ledger (Scene A below). Tomas & Sela. The stakes made visible: the Giving-Back, the removal from the prayers. Choice: Tomas follows procedure to the letter because he doubts it. Cost: Sela learns what failing means; ordinary death offstage (the abscess novice) keeps the ratio honest.
  5. The Kept Vigil (Scene B below). Tomas and the mother of a judged one. No death on-page; a sixteen-year-old death everywhere in the room. Consequence: the family will petition the Herald when he comes — private rite, realm-visible.
  6. The Slide. The wet spring; a snow-crew man dies (ordinary death #4); recovery work opens the old ground (Scene C below). Double explanation begins at the grave’s edge. Consequence: Alaine’s reseal order; the house’s seam shows.
  7. The House Divides. Sela & Tomas. Choice: Sela is asked — by novices, by a village delegation — what the voice says about the grave; she says nothing, truthfully, and the nothing is heard as depth. Cost: her honesty becomes unusable. Relationship: Sela–Alaine (the old woman starts watching her the way one watches weather).
  8. The Herald (movement II begins). The Blackcrest herald arrives to collect the valley’s testimony for the jubilee — and finds an unentered grave and an untested saint. Intimate: the herald is likable, precise, and right about almost everything. Choice: Alaine grants the Chronicle everything except the two things that matter. Consequence: the realm is now in the valley.
  9. The Asking. A village crew, planning the summer route past the slide-slopes, asks Sela’s word. She gives the least she can — a hesitation, an honest “not that way” — and it is taken as command. Death: the crew that ignored her (or obeyed her — draft both) loses its leader in a second slide. Double explanation: wet spring vs. the voice; both fully sufficient. Consequence: her word becomes policy.
  10. INTERLUDE II — Alaine / The Sanctified. Alaine watches the valley kneel to the girl; Sela feels the guilt arrive with the reverence, each feeding the other. Ends movement I→II hinge: Sela is now more holy in the valley’s eyes and more guilty in her own — the book’s engine, running.

7. Three scene samples (English, canon-compatible, gate-pending)

Scene A — The Ledger

The second night of a testing has a procedure, like everything in the house, and the procedure was that the Tester and the tested ate together.

Tomas brought the bread up himself. It was the same bread the house ate, dark and dense, and he set it between them on the small table in the listening room, and cut it, and gave Sela the first piece, because the rite said the tested one eats first, and the rite did not say why. Sela ate. She had herded goats through eleven winters; she was not delicate about bread. But she watched him cut it, and he saw her watch, and he thought, as he thought every time: she is learning the room. Good.

“There is a book,” he said, when they had eaten a while in the quiet the room was cut for. “The rite requires that I show it to you. I would show it to you even if the rite did not.” He set it on the table. It was not large. That was the first thing everyone noticed, and the last thing they said aloud.

“These are the judged,” he said. “All of them, since the book began. The found-true are in black.” He turned pages; the black entries went by in their dozens, names and years, some with the small sign of the griffe beside them — hearers who had lived and died in the house’s honor. “The found-false are in red.”

There were not dozens of red. There were enough.

“What happens to them,” Sela said. It was not a question. She had grown up in the valley; the valley knew things it did not say at festivals.

“They are given back to the silence.” He said it the way the book said it, because the book’s words were the only ones he could say levelly. “At first snow. They walk to the high line above the vigil grounds. They are not bound. They are not harmed. They carry bread for one day.” He paused, and then gave her the part the book did not say, because he had decided, sixteen years ago, that the ones he tested deserved the whole of it: “No one has ever been struck, Sela. No one is pushed. The house does not kill. The mountain decides — or they walk east over the shoulder and are never spoken of. Some must have walked. I have told myself for years that some walked.”

“And their names?”

“Are taken out of the prayers.” He turned the book so she could see how a red entry ended: the name, the year, and then the small ruled line where the name had been unpicked from the daily office — a needle drawn back through cloth. “The house believes a false voice, prayed for, keeps speaking. So the prayers close over them. It is —” he looked for the word he used in his own head, and gave her that one too, because she had eaten his bread — “complete.”

Sela looked at the red names for a long time. There were sisters in the valley whose family names she knew. There was a name from her own village, old, from before her mother’s time, and she understood at last why that family planted their fences a stride wider every generation, as if width could answer something.

“You have written in this book,” she said.

“Yes.”

“In red.”

“Yes.”

She took another piece of bread — he watched her make herself do it — and ate it slowly, and when she had finished she folded her hands and said, in the flat true voice that had frightened him the first evening: “Then teach me the procedure properly. All of it. If the mountain is going to decide about me, I want to know exactly what men decided first.”

It was, Tomas thought, the best answer the book had ever gotten. He did not write it down. Nothing said in the listening room survived the lamp — the rule was old as the mountain — and for the first time in sixteen years, walking down the stair with the ledger under his arm, he found himself glad of the burning, because no page would ever have carried her voice as it deserved, and he did not want to read a lesser version of it, in red or in black, ever, for the rest of his office.

Scene B — The Kept Vigil

The woman came every year at first snow, and every year the house let her in, because there was no rule against it and no one had wanted to write one.

She was sixty now, or near it, straight-backed the way valley women were straight-backed — from carrying, not from pride. She kept the vigil at the griffes the whole first night of snow, at the far end, past the last of the honored dead, facing a niche that held nothing at all. In the morning she would eat what the kitchen gave her, thank the house correctly, and walk back down to the valley. She had done it for sixteen years. Her name was Merta. Her son’s name had been Col, and no one in the house was permitted to say so.

Tomas met her at the gate. He did this every year, and every year he understood less why he did it, and could no more have stopped than stopped his own pulse.

“Brother,” she said.

“Merta. There will be snow by dark. The kitchen has the east room warm.”

“The east room is kind,” she said, meaning: I will sit at the griffes, as we both know. They walked up together through the first thin flakes, and she asked after the house’s health and he answered, and she asked whether the harvest had reached the high pastures in time and he answered that too, and neither of them said the name, because the name was out of the prayers, and out of the prayers was out of the mouth, within these walls, forever.

At the door of the vigil ground she stopped, as she always stopped, and asked the question she had asked sixteen times, in the same words, the way the valley keeps anything it cannot afford to lose:

“Is there a way back in?”

She did not mean her son’s body. The mountain had never given that back, or he had walked — the house’s mercy was that no one would ever know which, and its cruelty was the same fact. She meant the prayers. She meant: can a name, once unpicked, be sewn in again? Can the office close back over him? If the house was wrong — if you were wrong, Brother, you personally, sixteen years ago, at the start of your red ink — is there a needle for that?

“The rite has no way back in,” Tomas said, as he had said sixteen times, because it was the truth and she deserved the truth from him of all men, having received everything else from him already.

“No,” she agreed, mildly, and went in to her empty niche.

He stood at the door while the snow thickened. Procedure did not require him to stay. He watched the old woman settle onto the stone where the honored dead ended and her portion of the dark began, and he thought about the boy — charismatic, beloved, twenty; found false, not from malice, but self-deception, his own verdict, his first — and about the two sentences he had lived between ever since: I proved he was false and I could not prove he was genuine. Sixteen years, and the gap between them had not closed a hair’s width. It had a woman sitting in it, every first snow, straight-backed, facing nothing.

That night, alone, in his own cell, with the door shut and no lamp burning, Brother Tomas committed the only transgression of his tenure. He said the name. Once, aloud, at ordinary volume, the way one says a name at table.

“Col.”

Nothing answered. He had known nothing would answer; he was the house’s instrument against the belief that things answer. But the room felt different with the name in it, or he did, and he sat a long while in the dark, sixty years old in a house that burned its knowing nightly, and let himself understand — carefully, like a man probing a cracked tooth — that if the rite had no way back in, then the rite believed its own verdicts more than he had ever managed to, and that between him and the rite, one of them was pretending.

He would test the girl in the morning. He would do it properly.

He did not light the lamp again that night.

Scene C — The Ground Opens

They dug for Jarn for two days — the slide had taken him at the throat of the east shoulder, where the wet spring snow let go all at once — and they found him on the second evening, and that was grief but not mystery: the mountain had done what the mountain does, and Jarn had known the slopes and been unlucky, and his brothers wept and swore and carried him down, and the house rang the small bell it rings for the valley’s ordinary dead.

It was on the third day, cutting a drainage line so the slide could not sit and rot into the vigil ground below, that Odd’s spade rang on laid stone where no stone should be laid.

They were valley men; they knew field-clearing heaps from walls and walls from graves. This was courses. Flat stones set in overlapping rows, a hand’s width under the slide-wash, on the high east shoulder a full stride outside the line of white boundary rocks that marked where the house’s consecrated ground ended — outside it, above it, facing away. Odd stopped digging. They sent the boy down for the house, and the house, after a silence, sent up Brother Tomas, and — because it was the east shoulder, and everyone remembered what had been said at a trial in the winter, though no one said it now — the house sent up the goatherd too, and watched her face the whole climb.

They lifted the courses with cold care. Under them the ground was dry, which meant the courses had been doing their work — shedding water off the grave for a long time. For a very long time. Grave-depth, then wool: fine wool, tight-woven, the kind the valley saw at festivals on other people’s lords, gone brown as bark but whole enough to read. Then the body.

It lay face-down.

No one spoke for a while. Valley men had opened old ground before — slides and road-cuts turn up the forgotten dead a few times in a lifetime — and there were customs for it, words, a direction to lay them. There was no custom for this. The dead of the house lay in the griffes, faces to the valley. The dead of the valley lay in the churchyards, faces to the sky. This one had been put down, deliberately, carefully, in good wool, with its face in the earth and its back to the prayers, a stride outside the line — and when Tomas knelt, with the slow gentleness he did everything with, and freed the near arm to lay it decently, the arm ended at the wrist.

Clean. Old. Not the slide’s work, not an animal’s. The other arm the same. The hands were not in the grave. They looked, carefully, the way you look when you are hoping. The hands had never been in the grave.

“An oath-breaker,” Odd said at last, because someone had to, and his grandmother had been from the northeast where they still told it: hands off, face down, the word turned away from the world it broke. “Old custom. Mountains custom.”

“Or a plague grave,” said his brother. “Hasty. They took — ” he did not finish the sentence about what frightened people had sometimes taken, in bad years, to be sure the dead stayed dead.

“In lord’s wool?” said Odd.

“Grave gift,” said the brother. “Guilt does that.”

Tomas said nothing. He was looking at the courses they had lifted — stacked now to the side, in order, because valley men keep order even frightened. Flat stones in overlapping rows. Some lichened dark, some paler, some nearly clean. He was the house’s instrument for exactly one thing, the telling of true from told, and the stones were telling him something none of the explanations reached: they had not been laid once. They had been re-laid. Different hands, different centuries, the courses renewed the way the house renewed the vigil candles — which meant that for as long as this grave had faced away from the prayers, someone, generation after generation, in secret, on the high cold shoulder, had been keeping it.

Not hiding it. Keeping it.

Beside him he heard Sela’s breathing change — nothing more, no turn, no word; he was watching for it and it was only breath — and below them, small with distance, he saw figures already climbing from the house, and knew the first one by her straight slow gait, and understood that within the hour Mother Alaine would stand where he stood, and look at what he was looking at, and give an order.

He knelt in the wet snow beside a body with no hands and no name, on the east shoulder, above the vigil grounds, in the direction a girl had listened at midwinter, and he began — because it was his office, and because no one else would dare — to say the words for the unknown dead.

He was three lines in when he realized the words for the unknown dead were a prayer, and that he did not know, and could never know, whether he was sewing something back in.

He finished the lines anyway.
