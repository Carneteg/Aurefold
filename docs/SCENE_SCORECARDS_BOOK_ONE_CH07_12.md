## Scene-level conclusions

### Chapter 7 - The East Shoulder

The accidental grave becomes a test of custody rather than a solved mystery. Incompatible counts refer to different objects; handling and changing drawings destroy original positions. Reclosure preserves dignity at the cost of further examination.

Protected remainder: the grave has damaged provenance. It establishes neither the occupants' identity nor an Eleventh House connection, and no count may be reconciled into objective truth.

### Chapter 8 - Nothing Happened

Sela's precise denial becomes a social proof because listeners already possess the interpretation they want. The chapter shows a belief system growing through accurate repetition and changed use rather than an originating lie.

Protected remainder: Sela supplies no authoritative interpretation, miracle or objective magical fact.

### Chapter 9 - The Man Who Writes It Down

Perrin separates claims carefully while using money, shelter and attention to determine who can speak and which account can travel. His relief work saves lives and simultaneously becomes narrative power.

Protected remainder: the collector does not own truth. Access and synthesis do not produce a reconciled grave count or total meaning.

### Chapter 10 - Nobody Ever Asked

Fen receives an hour of recognition and knowingly trades observations for it. The next day's seating proves that attention has not repaired his page, marriage history or standing.

Protected remainder: Perrin's attention and a temporary seat produce no simple restoration. Fen's pain does not make his use of others morally infallible.

### Chapter 11 - What the Grave No Longer Shows

Wren correctly bounds Tomas's testimony and refuses a false total. She also turns Sela's refusal into a route toward alternate witnesses and material reconstruction. The evidentiary conclusion is sound; the method is not harmless.

Protected remainder: the grave remains unresolved. The reconstruction proves limits, not a voice, magic, identity or total count.

### Chapter 12 - Fen Who?

The flour book recovers Fen Leren as biographical fact, but public naming immediately activates land, debt and kinship complications. Fen forces Perrin to distinguish identity from the rights and belonging the name cannot restore.

Protected remainder: identity is not equivalent to institutional recognition. A surname restores no lease, marriage validity, rent or Whitehart standing.
