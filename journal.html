15 August 2026

Book Two has a name: The Road Still Open

A quiet milestone worth marking. Book One, The Bell of Silence, is complete — approximately 104,000 words across 47 chapters and one interlude — and it stands on its own; you don't need a second book to finish the story it tells.

But there is a second book, and now it has a title: The Road Still Open. That's all I'll say for a while, and on purpose. No plot, no promises about when — the work of a first book is to earn a second, and I'd rather show you that road than describe it. What I can tell you is where the name comes from in spirit: the peace the ten houses swore has held for four hundred years, and every road that keeps it open costs someone something to walk.

Book Two is in development. When there's a real step to report — not hype, an actual step — it goes here first. Follow the Journal free, and you won't miss it.

11 August 2026

Which house would you follow? Why Aurefold has no villains

The question I get asked most about Aurefold is which house is the good one. It's the wrong question — and it's the exact question the whole series is built to refuse.

The realm is sworn to ten Great Houses, and each is a different wager about what people are for. Blackthorn bets on intellect; Ashbourne on courage; Whitehart on faith; Stormrider on unity; Ravenshade on information; Ironvale on innovation; Blackcrest on the writing of history; Stonebear on honour; Tidebreaker on knowledge; Phoenix on the future. Say those aloud and you can already feel yourself leaning toward one. That lean is the whole story.

Here's the rule I set myself. Every house has to hold a virtue a decent person could live by — and every virtue has to cost someone something. A house of courage protects the many and spends the few. A house that keeps information moving makes some people safer and leaves others exposed. A house that preserves knowledge can quietly become control over the people who produced it. None of them is lying about their good; none of them is innocent of its cost.

That's why there is no villain to root against. The tension in The Bell of Silence isn't good versus evil; it's your sympathy pulling two ways at once and refusing to settle. The book opens on that kind of collision — a goatherd who will not say more than she knows, and a house built to test anyone who claims to have heard something. Both sides are defensible. That is the problem, and I never let the reader off the hook of it.

I think that's what "morally grey" should actually mean. Not that everyone is a little bad, but that everyone is defensibly right from inside their own logic — and you're left holding the trade-off the characters can't. So I'll keep asking the wrong question on purpose: not which house is good, but which house would you follow? Take the six-question quiz and find out where your sympathy really lands — then argue with the result. The houses would. You can meet all ten on the Great Houses page.

8 August 2026

Ten philosophies, one map: how the Great Houses were built

People assume you build a fantasy world by drawing the map first. I built Aurefold the other way round. I started with ten arguments, and the map is only where they landed.

Each Great House began as a single question about how people should live together — not a colour, not a sigil, not a castle, a question. "What should the most capable mind be permitted to decide for everyone else?" became Blackthorn. "When does bravery protect others, and when does it demand that others pay?" became Ashbourne. Ten questions, ten houses. You can read all ten, each with the question its virtue forces on everyone else.

The test I gave myself was simple: a house is only real if one of its own members could state its philosophy sympathetically and mean it. The moment a house can only be described by its enemies, it has stopped being a culture and become a cartoon. So I wrote each one from the inside first — what it is proud of — and only then went looking for the cost that pride hides.

Geography came after that. A house that charts everything and shares almost none of it wants a coastline and a lighthouse. A house of unity wants roads, and the passes that hold the valleys together. A house built on the idea of the future wants scorched ground it can insist on rebuilding. The map is really a diagram of ten temperaments, drawn as terrain.

Over all of it sits the thing that makes the houses press against each other: the Tenfold Compact, the peace they swore on cooling ground after the war that made them, and have kept — by one measure or another — for four hundred years. That history holds. It just costs a little more every year to keep, and Book One is set in the one valley where the cost finally comes due for a single ordinary person.

If you want the method in a line: start with what people believe, make the belief cost something, and let the world grow around the argument. The houses came first. The map only tells you where they had to stand.

Building this one in the open

Most books arrive as a finished object, with none of the road that led there. Aurefold is going the other way. From here to publication, the work happens where readers can see it — reader testing, cover and interior design, print proofs, the campaign — and this journal is where each real step gets written down.

No hype, no invented milestones. When something genuinely moves, it goes here, and it goes to free members first. If you want to watch a book get made rather than just buy the result, you're in the right place.
