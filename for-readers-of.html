For readers of

A page for those who arrive from other shelves. No comparisons are made lightly here; what follows is an honest account of what you loved there, and what this archive keeps in the same spirit.

If you loved The Name of the Wind

What stays with you from Rothfuss is the weight of a name withheld, the sense that a story told in a quiet room can be more dangerous than any battle, and prose that treats every sentence as an instrument. Aurefold works in that register. The Bell of Silence opens not with a war but with a record under dispute, read aloud in a room built to keep voices from traveling. Its world holds ten great houses, a testimony the realm cannot settle, and a girl who will not explain what she heard. It is a slow book in the deliberate sense — one that trusts the reader to listen.

If you loved The Lies of Locke Lamora

Lynch gave you a city that was itself a character, schemes within schemes, and loyalty that costs more than coin. Aurefold has no gentleman thieves and no Camorr — but it shares the pleasure of watching institutions outmaneuver one another. Its ten houses keep their own archives, witness one another's witnesses, and fight their wars in sealed records and unanswered letters. The intrigue here is patient, and the debts are older than any living character.

If you loved The Wheel of Time

Jordan's readers know the long road: a world built to be lived in, factions with histories that reach back centuries, and the slow gathering of a story told at full scale. Aurefold is built the same way — a realm four hundred years into an uneasy peace, mapped, chronicled, and kept. The archive here is real: house records, maps, and histories that extend beyond the first book and wait for readers who like their worlds deep.

If you loved A Song of Ice and Fire

Martin taught readers that houses are philosophies before they are armies, and that what a family chooses to remember decides what it becomes. Aurefold's ten houses are drawn on that principle — each a promise and its cost, each keeping its own version of the realm's founding wound. What happened at the banner-field is a matter of record; which record is the true one is the question the series asks. No one here is safe from the archive.

If you loved The Priory of the Orange Tree

Shannon's readers came for an epic that takes its time, a world with its own theology and contested history, and people whose duties outgrow the lives they meant to have. Aurefold offers the same patience: a listening room instead of a battlefield, a tester of testimony instead of a chosen one, and a faith — the Bell of Silence itself — that the reader is invited to weigh rather than simply accept.

What Aurefold asks of you

Only time, and the willingness to read closely. Book One, The Bell of Silence, is out now as a free ebook — forty-seven chapters and an interlude, complete. Nothing on this page spoils it. Begin where the realm begins: with a record, and with the one person willing to say what it omits.
