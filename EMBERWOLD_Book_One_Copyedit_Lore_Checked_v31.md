# EMBERWOLD
## Book One: The Bell of Silence

## Prologue

**From the Chronicle of Victory, on the swearing of the Ash Oath, being the account entered and kept at Crown-watch these four hundred years:**

*In the last year of the war now called the Unification, the armies of the houseless enemy were broken upon the heartland plain. The plain burned from morning to the third day, and when the burning was done the land itself was grey, and is called the Ash Fields to this hour.*

*And eleven lords came together upon the cooling ground while the ash still moved in the wind. There they named the ten customs by which the peace would be kept: the planner and the rider, the listener and the smith, the keeper of words and the keeper of word, the reader of waves and the reader of men, the one who writes and the one who begins again.*

*And the ten Great Houses pressed their seals into the wax of one pact, which is the Ash Oath, and divided the land and the duty and the grief among them.*

*The peace they swore has held from that day to this, four hundred years save four, which no peace before it held. The wisdom of the ten is proven in every harvest gathered and every child grown old, and their seals endure upon the pact, ten seals, as the houses are ten, as they have always been ten.*

*It has been entered.*

---

So says the Chronicle.

It is read each year in halls from the Sleep Coast to the Edgelands. In four centuries no authorised hand has altered a word, which the house that keeps it offers as evidence of its truth.

One old copy carries a note in another hand beside the final sentence. The hand has never been identified.

*Count the seals.*

Nothing follows.

Those permitted to examine the Ash Oath report that the wax is cracked, crowded and darkened by age. Impressions overlap at the foot of the page; the outermost is broken against the edge. Ten seals, says the Chronicle, and the Chronicle is the record. Some say eleven. They are not the sort of people the Chronicle cites.

This is the realm the story enters: a peace four hundred years deep, a jubilee four years away, and a record no one has improved.

The story does not begin where the pact is kept or where its great retelling is being prepared. It begins in the high northwest, where the Silent Mountains bend along the top of the known world and the valleys are cold, steep and poor. There stands a house feared by people who have never seen it, because it claims to hear what no record can hold.

House Whitehart does not read the Chronicle aloud at year’s end. House Whitehart listens.

In 396 A.U., in the high pastures above the Bell of Silence, a goatherd stopped mid-word on a cold hillside and listened to something that was not there.

No account agrees on what she heard. None records a direction.

The house would test her. The house tests everyone who hears.

This book is assembled from the accounts that survived. Where they contradict one another, the contradiction has been kept.

*The map ends where the truth begins.*

---

*The Light Heights, 396 A.U.*

---

## Chapter One
### The Trial of the Goatherd

There was no throne in the Bell of Silence.

The listening room ran deep into the mountain, long and narrow, with the griffes cut into the far wall. The dead rested there in open niches, their faces turned towards the valley. The room had been made for echoes.

Tonight they meant to use it for judgment.

Tomas arrived early.

He set one lamp low beside the table. It lit the hands of anyone sitting opposite him and left the face uncertain. People watched their faces. They forgot their hands.

He laid out a sheet of paper and a stick of char, then shifted his chair until the draught from the valley would fall across the girl.

Mother Alaine entered while he was testing the lamp.

She took her usual place at the back, close against the stone and the niches. The novices called the choice humble. They said she left the centre of the room to those who needed it.

Tomas nodded.

Alaine answered with the smallest movement of her head.

Then they waited.

Sela came late.

She crossed the room without looking to either of them and sat near the open arch, facing the valley. The dark lay below her, along with the villages and the narrow road climbing towards the house. She put the dead at her back.

No one had told her this was improper. Tomas decided not to tell her now.

“Sit where you like,” he said.

She glanced at him. “I have.”

“I only want to talk.”

“You want to test me.”

There was no accusation in it.

“Everyone wants to test me,” she said. “You’re only the one they named for it.”

Something changed in Alaine’s stillness. Tomas saw it, though he could not have said what had moved.

“Then we understand each other,” he said.

He smiled. He knew what smiles did in rooms like this. People wanted to answer kindness. Often they answered too much.

He began with the herd.

Where had she grazed them? How many had survived the winter? Which pasture had failed first?

She answered plainly. No liturgical pauses. No *Icke jag*. She named the upper meadow, the broken wall, the three old ewes lost before the thaw.

Tomas left the paper untouched.

“Tell me about the winter at the pass.”

Her eyes went to him. “You know about that.”

“I know what people say.”

“They say enough.”

“I want what you say.”

Sela rubbed one thumb across the side of the other. There was a split in the skin near the nail, dark with dirt.

“The sickness started low,” she said. “In the old pasture. The animals stopped feeding. Then some of the older people took ill.”

She looked towards the arch.

“They prayed. Properly. I didn’t know the words.”

“And you?”

“I sat with a woman. She was nearly gone. There was—”

Sela stopped.

Her head turned.

Not towards Tomas. Not towards Alaine. Towards the arch and the dark beyond it.

She listened.

Tomas felt his own neck begin to turn before he caught himself. Across the room, Alaine had turned as well.

For a heartbeat the three of them faced the same empty place.

The wind dropped. The flame straightened.

Sela looked back at him.

“A direction,” she said. “That was all.”

“What direction?”

“North. Over the high pass.”

“In winter.”

“Yes.”

“With sick animals.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

She tucked her hands farther into her sleeves.

“It made no sense. We moved them anyway. The sickness didn’t follow.”

“You told them to go north.”

“I told my father. He told the others.”

“And they listened to you.”

“They listened to him.”

Tomas picked up the char.

“What did you hear?”

“I’ve told you.”

“You said a direction.”

“That is what it was.”

“A word?”

“No.”

“A voice?”

Her jaw tightened. “No.”

“A thought?”

“I don’t know.”

He waited.

Sela looked back towards the dark. “It wasn’t mine.”

Tomas wrote:

*She stops speaking and turns towards the open air. The room turns with her.*

He read the line once.

It described what had happened. Nothing more.

It might have been a gift. It might have been a habit she had discovered in frightened rooms. It might have been three tired people following a movement without thinking.

The paper did not choose.

“Do it again,” he said.

Sela stared at him.

“Call it back.”

“I can’t.”

“Try.”

“It doesn’t come because I try.”

“Then how does it come?”

“When it wants.”

“What wants?”

“I don’t know.”

Her voice had risen. She heard it and lowered it again.

“A direction,” she said. “That’s all. Not words. I never asked for it.”

Tomas said nothing.

“I never asked,” she repeated.

She looked at the lamp, then at the floor, then back at him.

“It was a direction. I’ve said that.”

Her answers came faster now, each one flatter than the last. Tomas had seen this before. Fear made some people vague. In Sela it did the opposite. She began to hammer every word into place.

He set down the char.

Sela turned past him.

“Mother.”

Alaine’s hands rested in her lap.

“You’ve heard them,” Sela said. “You’ve sat with the griffes for fifty years.”

Alaine did not answer at once.

“You know what it feels like,” Sela continued. “Tell me what I should listen for. Tell me how I know whether it’s real.”

The lamp remained still.

Tomas looked to Alaine. He had done so a hundred times and had never noticed how quickly the habit carried him there.

Alaine’s face did not tighten.

Her breathing slowed.

When she spoke, her voice was quieter than before.

“It is not something you listen for, child. It listens through you.”

Sela watched her.

“You will not find it by straining after it,” Alaine said. “You must learn not to stand in its way.”

She paused.

“You are further along than you fear. Do not crowd the silence.”

It was a good answer.

Too good, perhaps. The thought came to Tomas and was gone before he could take hold of it.

Sela lowered her shoulders.

“Thank you, Mother.”

Alaine inclined her head.

Tomas looked down at his paper.

He had spent sixteen years watching people under pressure. They swallowed. They spoke too quickly. They touched their faces or folded and unfolded their hands.

Alaine had gone quieter.

He had seen it before. He must have. He had sat across from her for thirty years.

The thought would not settle into any useful category.

“Enough,” he said.

Sela turned back to him. “You haven’t judged me.”

“No.”

“Will you?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“When I can.”

Her mouth moved as if she might argue. Instead she stood.

At the arch she stopped. The wind caught the edge of her cloak.

“I hope it’s real,” she said.

Neither Tomas nor Alaine answered.

“If it is, I shouldn’t be afraid of it.”

She looked into the valley.

“And if it isn’t—”

She left the sentence there and walked out.

The wind returned after her. The lamp flame bent towards the room.

Alaine rose.

“It has been a long night for the girl,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You will know when it is time.”

Tomas gathered the paper and char.

Alaine passed the nearest griffe and touched the stone with two fingers. She did it often. The novices spoke of the gesture as devotion.

Then she was gone.

Tomas remained at the table.

The room felt larger without them and less empty.

He looked at the arch. Sela had turned towards it before the wind died. Or perhaps the wind had begun to fall first. He could no longer put the two events in order.

A tired girl. An old draught. Three people watching one another too closely.

That explanation required nothing else.

He held the paper over the lamp.

The edge caught. Flame ran across the page and took the line with it.

For a moment he saw the words through the fire.

*The room turns with her.*

Then there was only ash.

Tomas sat back.

The direction in the winter had been north, over the pass. Tonight Sela had turned east, towards the Silent Mountains.

Alaine’s corner lay in the same line.

He looked at the empty chair.

After a while he put out the lamp.

## Chapter Two
### The Morning After

She woke before the bell, as she had woken every morning of her life, because goats do not care what has happened to you.

For a moment the morning was ordinary. Cold floor. Dead ash. The sour smell of a house shut through the night. She put her feet down, took the wooden bowl with the wire-bound split and went into the passage. It was Tuesday. Someone would complain about the bread.

She got as far as the doorway of the long hall before it stopped working.

The hall was full and quiet. Not prayer-quiet. Whitehart was noisy at prayer. This was the quiet of a byre when something has come in behind you.

They had left her place.

Yesterday two girls had crowded her on the third bench and the old man opposite had eaten with his mouth open. This morning the bench filled from the far end and stopped an arm’s length before her place.

If she stood there, someone would fetch her. Sela crossed the hall before they could.

She sat. A few people breathed again.

She put down her bowl and turned until she could see the open doorway: yard, wet stones, grey morning. Four heads followed the movement.

She left herself facing the door. Turning back would have given them another thing to watch.

The pots came out. Ansel followed with his sleeves pinned and his tally-stick under one arm. He was wide, cheerful and quick with a sum. The Bell had eaten from his stores for thirty years.

He served her first.

Sela knew the order: elders, Testers, workers, novices, then whoever had annoyed Ansel. Twice she had eaten fourth from last for laughing.

He filled her bowl, paused, and added half a ladle.

“Eat that,” said Ansel comfortably. “You’ve gone thin.”

Ansel had changed the order without asking anyone. He continued down the bench, giving the next bowl its usual measure.

The novice opposite watched the extra food. Her name was Ivet. She was sixteen and scrubbed so clean that the skin at her knuckles had split. Her face brightened. Not envy. She had found proof.

Sela took one spoonful. The stew was too hot and caught the roof of her mouth. She swallowed it anyway.

---

Ivet had been waiting all night for a chance.

“Sister,” she said, careful with every word. “Yesterday, on the stair. You said the wind was wrong.”

Sela remembered the wet boots. Nothing else.

“I said the wind had gone round,” she said. “It had. It’s coming off the ice now. It’ll rain by dark.”

“Yes.” Ivet leaned closer. “The wind has gone round. By evening it will be a different house from the one that went to sleep.”

Sela said, “It only means it’s going to rain.”

Ivet smiled and set *Icke jag* into the pause with practised ease. Three people farther down answered her.

They answered Ivet, not Sela.

---

Then the others began.

Bryn Corr came up with the Tuesday bread, swearing about the mud. She brought the smell of wet wool and the valley into the hall. Sela nearly stood. Bryn had known her since she was six and had once thrown a boot at her for leaving a gate open.

Bryn set down the bread and looked at Sela without throwing anything.

“I don’t know,” said Sela.

“Nine. On a Tuesday, in this mud.” Bryn smiled. Part of her was already counting beds. “Your mother will be pleased.”

“One had Blackcrest at his collar,” Bryn added. “Another wore a Stonebear oath-ring. The rest looked ordinary enough.”

Children in the Light Heights learned the Ten Great Houses by rote. Sela had not expected Blackcrest and Stonebear to arrive behind Bryn Corr’s bread cart.

Osric sat across from her and asked whether she had slept, whether the room had been cold, and whether the feeling came before Tomas set down the lamp or after. The third question arrived in the same easy voice as the first two.

Sela answered. Osric tore bread into four equal pieces while she spoke. Only when he aligned them beside his bowl did she see that one question had been a test.

He thanked her and asked Ansel for salt. He liked Tomas. He also knew the Tester’s office would one day be empty.

Wilda stopped beside the extra half-ladle.

“Eat it all,” she said. “They’ll want you strong for the next testing.”

She went on. The bowl was still warm between Sela’s hands. The next testing had not been mentioned at breakfast until Wilda named it.

Sela had faced a wolf at fourteen with a stick. She could not lift her spoon.

She looked for Tomas.

Tomas sat at the Testers’ end, looking at his hands. He had seen the ladle, Ivet, Osric and Wilda. He did not look up.

Sela looked back at her bread.

---

Then Fen sat down.

Fen came late with wax on his knuckles and the candle-box under his arm. He saw the empty stretch beside her and sat in it.

He put his elbows on the table. He looked into her bowl.

“You’ve been given the sludge off the bottom,” he said. “Congratulations. It’s the good part. Nobody knows that except me and Ansel and now unfortunately you.”

Sela laughed too loudly. Every head rose. For one breath she was only a girl at breakfast with a friend.

Ansel came down with the ladle. He put two fingers on Fen’s shoulder, friendly and firm.

“That’s not your place, lad.”

No rule was spoken. Fen stood. His knee caught the bench and made the bowls jump. Nobody laughed. Ansel went back to the pot.

Sela opened her mouth.

She could have said, *Let him stay.* The room would have obeyed.

She drew breath. If they obeyed because she spoke, it would not make the seat his.

Fen lifted the box. One candle rolled loose, struck the floor and went under the next bench. He crouched to fetch it while the hall watched, then left before Sela chose.

Ivet watched him go. Then she looked at Sela.

“She permitted it,” Ivet said softly, to no one, to everyone. “*Icke jag.*”

The meal ended in pieces. Benches scraped. Bowls went to the kitchen hatch. People who had spent the morning watching Sela discovered work that required them elsewhere.

Sela gathered the bowls nearest her. The first stack was too high. It tipped against her wrist and stew ran down her sleeve.

Ivet caught the top bowl before it fell.

“Let me,” she said.

“I have them.”

“Of course.”

Ivet released the bowl but stayed beside her. They carried the stacks to the scullery, where the washing water had already gone grey. A kitchen girl pushed a cloth towards Sela, then pulled it back and gave her the clean one.

Sela took the grey cloth instead.

The girl stared. Ivet smiled.

“She chooses the used thing,” Ivet whispered.

“It was closer,” said Sela.

The scullery girl nodded too quickly. By the time Sela had wiped the third bowl, two novices at the door had heard that the Called would not take clean linen while others worked.

Sela put the bowl down hard enough to splash them.

Tomas appeared at the hatch with an empty cup. He saw the water on her sleeve, the novices in the doorway and Ivet standing very straight. His mouth tightened.

“The cup belongs here,” he said.

It did not. He had carried it from the Testers’ table and could have left it with the others.

Sela took it from him.

“You could have looked at me,” she said quietly.

Tomas glanced towards the doorway. The two novices pretended to discuss soap.

“Not there,” he said.

“There was where I was.”

He had no answer ready. The scullery girl reached between them for the cup, and the moment went with it.

Sela wrung out the grey cloth. Tomas left before she finished.

---

She found him afterwards in the listening room, at the wall, doing the niches.

Fen worked along the niches with a rag and bone knife. He knew every name in the wall. He did not turn.

“Fen—”

“Don’t.”

He kept scraping.

“If you apologise, it happened. Then I have to be gracious and you have to be sorry.” He knocked wax into his palm. “Leave it alone and it was seating.”

Sela stood in the middle of the listening room with her hands at her sides.

“It was only seating,” she said.

“There,” said Fen. “You see how easy.”

He moved to the next niche. The knife slipped once on the cold wax and cut a pale line across his thumb. He put the thumb in his mouth, looked at the wall and began again.

Sela went out past the arch without stopping. People were in the yard. Behind her, the scraping continued.

---

## Chapter Three
### Interlude: The Woman Against the Stone

The chair had been moved once in fifty years.

A novice had dragged it nearer the lamp on a winter evening when the room was bitter with cold. Alaine had thanked her, waited until the girl left, then put it back against the wall herself.

No one moved it now.

They said Mother Alaine sat apart so others might be heard. They said she put her back to the griffes because the dead required no ceremony from her. The reasons had improved over time. In the first years they had only called it her chair.

She had never supplied a reason of her own.

That night Tomas came early, as expected. He lowered the lamp, tested the draught with the back of his hand, shifted the paper twice. The char lay parallel with the table edge. It always did.

He believed he concealed the work inside ordinary movements. Perhaps he did. Alaine had watched him too long.

When he nodded, she returned it. Enough to answer. Nothing that could be mistaken for invitation.

Then Sela entered and sat at the arch.

The girl did not look at the griffes. She put them behind her as if they were a wall in a byre. Alaine watched the back of her neck, bare where the cloak had slipped.

A person could sit that way only by forgetting what was there.

Or by having no cause to fear it.

Alaine placed her hands one over the other.

Tomas began his questions. Pasture. Sickness. Weather. The girl answered with no care for the proper pauses. *Icke jag* did not appear once.

Alaine had spoken those words so often that they had lost their edges. At dawn. At bedsides. Over the griffes. Into the hands of frightened children.

Not I.

The house heard humility in them.

Alaine kept her eyes on the lamp.

Sela spoke of the winter at the pass. The dying woman. The direction north.

Then her voice stopped.

The flame was standing straight.

Alaine saw that. She was certain she saw it.

Sela turned towards the arch.

Tomas turned after her.

Alaine’s own head moved.

Later, alone, she would try to put the moments in order. The flame. The broken word. The turn. There should have been an order. She had lived by orders, by what came before and what could therefore be explained by what came after.

In the room there was only the dark beyond the arch and the girl listening to it.

The wind returned. Or had not yet stopped.

No matter.

Tomas made her try again. The girl grew frightened and began repeating herself. Alaine recognised the change in her voice. Every Mother learned such things. Fear had its registers. Faith had others.

Then Sela looked past Tomas.

“Mother.”

Alaine did not move.

“You know how it feels.”

The girl’s trust was plain. It sat badly in the room.

“Tell me what I should listen for.”

For years Alaine had expected the question from someone. She had kept answers without admitting that she kept them.

Old Mother Vess had once told a weeping boy not to chase the silence. A shepherd dying in the red winter had said that something listened through him. A novice with blood on her sleeve had spoken of stepping aside.

The phrases came when needed. They always had.

“It is not something you listen for, child,” Alaine said. “It listens through you.”

Sela’s face changed at once. Hope was quick in the young.

“You must learn not to stand in its way.”

That part had no owner Alaine could remember.

Perhaps it was hers.

Tomas’s hand stopped above the paper.

Alaine saw it. She continued before he could look up.

“You are further along than you fear. Do not crowd the silence.”

Sela thanked her.

It was done.

The girl’s shoulders came down. The room accepted the answer. Tomas did not write.

Alaine rested one thumb over the other and kept them still.

The test ended soon after. Sela left. At the arch she spoke of fear and did not finish what came after it.

Tomas remained at the table.

“It has been a long night for the girl,” Alaine said.

The sentence contained no claim that could be tested.

“Yes,” he answered.

“You will know when it is time.”

He looked tired. There was grey in his beard now, more of it each winter. She remembered him at fifteen, angry because a candidate had lied about stealing apples. He had wanted dishonesty to be simple then.

Alaine rose.

Her fingers found the nearest griffe as she passed. The stone was cold. The same shallow crack crossed the lower edge, worn smooth by years of touch.

The novices thought she greeted the dead.

She did not know what she did.

At the stair she heard paper catch fire.

The rule required it.

No working note survived a trial. A judgment was entered, or it was not. The path to it went to ash.

The rule had been written in the first year after she was raised as Mother. She remembered the room where she wrote it more clearly than the day of her raising: a narrow table, rain at the shutter, two old trial pages left beside one another by mistake.

On one page, a woman had described the silence as warmth beneath the tongue. On the other, Alaine had used the same words.

She had folded both pages before anyone returned.

By winter, the new discipline was law.

It had been praised for its purity.

In her cell she sat on the edge of the bed. The tinderbox was by her hand. She did not light the candle.

The girl had turned first.

The flame had gone still first.

Alaine pressed her palms together until the joints hurt.

There had been a draught. The room was cut for draughts. Tomas had set the lamp badly, or the wick had burned low. Memory changed under hunger. Everyone knew that.

It was said often in the house.

After a time she lay down without undressing.

Somewhere in the wall water moved through the stone. She listened until it became only water.

The chair remained where she had left it.

## Chapter Four
### The Road Down

Brother Tomas gave her the day.

He did it badly. He came to find her in the yard, and he stood there working out how to say a simple thing, and what he said in the end was, “You have people below. Go down and see them. Be back before the light goes.”

Two months earlier she would have gone without telling him. Now Tomas had to release her. He heard it after he spoke and looked away.

“Thank you,” Sela said. Tomas winced once she had turned away.

By the third bend the road was hers again. She knew the stone that rose through the mud, the alder that still leafed lying down, the gap goats always tried. She came above the village singing badly under her breath.

Then she came round the shoulder and the village saw her.

At first, nothing happened.

A man approached with a barrow. Thirty paces away he set it down and began examining a wheel he had built himself. He kept at it until she passed.

He had carried her home with a broken ankle when she was seven. She had been sick down his back. He had never mentioned it.

After that she saw the gaps. A woman took a child indoors. Two men at a wall lowered their voices. A dog ran to her and was called back twice.

No one was unkind.

---

She heard Harl before she saw him. Harl talked while he worked and did not require an audience.

He stood in her mother’s field with his coat off, driving a post. Brem held it with both arms and his eyes shut.

“Open your eyes,” said Harl.

“You’ll hit my hands.”

“I’ve never hit anybody’s hands in my life. Open your eyes and look at the post, or I *will* hit your hands, and it’ll be an education for both of us.”

Brem opened his eyes. Harl swung. The post dropped a hand’s width and the mountain gave the blow back.

“There,” said Harl. “Now you’ve seen it done, so you’ve no excuse. Come round this side.”

Harl saw Sela at the wall. Her stomach tightened. She waited for the barrow, the lowered voice, the called-back dog.

Harl said: “Well, don’t just stand there like a saint. Get the mallet.”

---

“Take the mallet. Knock the wedges in. Properly this time, not like the byre gate you ruined two winters ago.”

“I was twelve,” said Sela.

“You were seventeen,” said Harl. “Don’t add years on to get out of things. It’s a low habit and I’ve seen it ruin men.”

She laughed. It hurt under her ribs.

They set posts for an hour. The ground was harder near the wall and Sela had to drive each wedge twice. Harl rejected one post because its base had begun to rot, though Brem said it looked sound. Harl made him smell it.

Brem drove a splinter into his thumb and swore with words Harl had certainly taught him. Harl called him a disgrace, then took the boy’s hand and worked the splinter free with the point of his knife. He wiped the blade on his trouser leg before putting it away.

“There,” said Harl. “Don’t cry.”

“I’m not crying.”

“I didn’t say you were. I said don’t.” He gave the hand back. “Right. Next one. And this time hold it like you mean to keep it.”

Brem copied Harl’s grip. Wrong first. Then right. Harl corrected him without stopping his own work.

Sela looked at the mountain until her eyes stopped stinging.

The next post leaned.

Harl noticed after three blows. He stood back, shut one eye and swore at the post as if it had chosen the angle.

“Pull it,” he told Brem.

“It is in.”

“That is the difficulty.”

They dug it out again. The lower end had struck a buried stone and skated sideways. Harl made Brem reach into the hole and feel the stone before he let him fetch the bar.

“You cannot fix what you refuse to put your hand on,” he said.

Then the iron bar slipped, struck his shin and made him sit down in the mud.

Brem looked at Sela. Sela looked at the post.

Harl breathed through his teeth.

“Not a word.”

“I didn’t say anything,” said Brem.

“Your face did. It has always been loose with information.”

Sela took the bar and worked the stone free while Harl rubbed his leg. It was larger than it had seemed from above. The three of them rolled it to the wall. Harl tried to stand, failed once, and accepted Sela’s arm on the second attempt without thanking her.

Brem reset the post. This time he checked the hole first.

“Tam checks,” Harl said. “That boy checks a thing until it grows old under him. You don’t check at all. Between you there might be one useful person.”

“Tam isn’t here,” Brem said.

Harl’s hand paused on the maul.

“No.”

He inspected the strap at his wrist, though nothing was wrong with it. Then he gave Brem the maul and made him drive the post himself. The first blow glanced. The second landed square.

Harl nodded once and turned away before the boy saw.

---

Bryn Corr came up the lane at noon with an empty basket and a full face.

“Harl,” she said.

“Bryn,” said Harl, and went on working.

“You’re on my ground.”

“I’m on Sela’s mother’s ground, which is not your ground, and has not been your ground since before either of us was weaned, whatever your father told you in his cups.”

“The boundary—”

“The boundary,” said Harl, “is where the wall is. Walls are honest. It’s people who move.”

Bryn laughed. Harl went on with the post. They had been having this argument longer than Sela had been alive.

Then Bryn looked at Sela.

“Sela. You should know. I have eleven—” Bryn counted on her fingers. “Nine. Nine in the barn since Tuesday. Paying to eat my bread and be cold.”

“They’re paying to look at the mountain,” said Harl.

“They’re paying to look at *her*,” said Bryn, cheerfully, and jerked her chin at Sela. “And they’ll pay more by summer. There’s talk down the valley. There’ll be a hundred by the thaw.”

Harl set down the maul. He looked at the field, then at Sela. His mouth opened once before the words came.

“A hundred,” he said.

“By the thaw,” said Bryn. “They’ll need feeding. They’ll need beds. They’ll need somebody to walk them up the mountain who knows the road, because half of them will kill themselves on it otherwise, and I’ll tell you now, Harl, I’d rather it wasn’t you.”

“Because you’d rather it was you.”

“Because I’d rather it was *anyone* who could be relied on to bring them back down again.”

Bryn left laughing, then came back for the basket she had forgotten beside the wall.

“The flour’s grey again,” she told Harl, as if this were his fault too.

“Then stop buying grey flour.”

“There isn’t any other.”

She went a second time. Harl watched until the hedge hid her.

---

They ate on the wall. Harl was quiet long enough for Brem to notice.

Then he said: “Your father’s ram.”

Sela stopped chewing.

“Your father’s ram,” said Harl again, to the mountain, not to her. “The year of the wet spring. When he was ill.”

“I remember.”

“I paid twelve marks. It was worth more. Your mother did not know. I did.”

The wind came across the field.

“I told myself I was owed. Water. The top field. Something your grandfather did before either of us was born.” He rubbed his jaw. “It held up for six years.”

Harl sat with his hands between his knees. Sela waited. He did not reach the word.

“It was a good ram,” he said at last.

Sela put away the answer she had been holding for him.

Brem scraped mud from one boot with a stick and pretended not to listen.

Harl got up, went to his coat and returned with something in his hand.

It was his small knife, the one he used for splinters.

“You have gone two months without a knife. Take it. Do not make it a thing.” Harl picked up the maul.

---

The light went long. Harl kept working and talked about summer.

He would guide the high road over the east shoulder. One day instead of two. A hundred visitors would need meat, beds and someone who knew the ground.

He had counted the water, the grazing and the stopping places aloud while mending harness.

“And I’ll have the roof off the byre before the first snow,” he said. “That roof has been a scandal since before you were born. And I’ll get the boy a horse.”

“Tam doesn’t want a horse.”

“Tam wants a horse so badly he has decided he does not. I did that at his age.” Harl drove the post. “He can have it and be ungrateful. I will say nothing.”

Sela turned the knife in her hand. She wanted to see Tam get the horse. She wanted to see Harl keep quiet.

---

Her mother fed her and asked nothing. Passing with the pot, she touched Sela’s hair and snatched her hand back.

They ate at the low table. Sela set her bowl down and turned herself, without thinking, until she could see the door.

No one looked when she turned towards the door. Her mother looked hardest at the pot. The stew had caught at the bottom and she scraped the burnt part loose though no one had asked for more.

Brem had followed her all day, showing the hole in his thumb and asking about the mountain. Halfway through her sentence he interrupted, as he always had.

“—but that’s not what Harl said, he said the—”

And he stopped.

He covered his mouth with one hand. Then he lowered it and stared at his bowl.

“Sorry. Go on.”

The room was very quiet.

“Go on,” said her mother gently, from the fire. “Go on, Sela.”

Harl’s knife lay under the table in Sela’s lap. Her family waited for her to continue.

She had lost the sentence. Brem’s hand over his mouth was all that remained.

“It doesn’t matter,” she said. “It was nothing.”

They nodded and returned to their food.

She climbed in the dark without a lamp.

At the wind-bend she stopped with the village behind her.

Her left boot had taken water since the ford. She shifted her weight and felt the loose seam press her foot. It needed mending. She had no awl and no one above she wanted to ask.

She went on.

---

## Chapter Five
### The Ledger

She asked him on the stair without warning.

“What happens to the ones you say no to?”

In sixteen years as a Tester, no candidate had asked. A novice who imagined failure was already suspect. The house had no reason to ask. The Called had reasons not to.

Tomas had filed their silence as fear. The filing had been useful.

Sela stood two steps below him with her hood down and her hands in her sleeves and waited.

“Come to the Ledger room,” said Tomas, “tomorrow, after the bell.”

The office required refusal. Twice he had refused frightened candidates and neither had asked again.

He could not find an honest ground for refusing her. That night another reason presented itself and he put it aside: he wanted a witness to what he might do.

The Ledger room was not a shrine. It was a cold cell off the north passage: table, stool, shutter and chest. It smelled of iron gall, mildew and stone.

The Ledger’s boards shone at the corners. Generations of hands had lifted it there rather than touch the middle.

Tomas caught himself doing the same.

“Black,” he said, “for the true.”

He turned the pages. Sela stood with her hands behind her back and did not touch them.

The pages were plain. Browned ink, hurried ruling, entries no longer than a butcher’s account.

“That’s all?” said Sela.

“It says she was true and what she did. More would be opinion.” He turned two courses. “Two hundred years. Most of these names are never spoken outside this room.”

Her lips moved over one of them.

And then he opened it at the other place, and turned the book, and stepped back.

---

The red entry stopped her.

Same hand. Same ruled line. Red ink instead of black. A thumbprint had dried in the margin, no darker than the rest.

Sela stood very still.

“Given Back,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Say what it means.”

Tomas had decided not to soften the answer. The decision had felt like honesty while he walked to the room.

“At first snow they are walked to the high line. Unbound. No one strikes them. They receive bread, a good cloak and the full office all the way up. I have heard men weep at it.”

His voice stayed level.

“And then everyone comes down,” he said, “and they do not.”

The wind moved the shutter.

“No one kills them. The mountain does. Or the cold. Some say they walk east and survive. None has returned.”

“How many?” said Sela.

“In red? Since the book began?” He did not have to look. “Twenty-six.”

“That’s not many.”

“One in a generation. The house treats it as it treats a bad winter: rare, terrible, nobody’s fault.” He touched the board. “That is why the red is kept in an ordinary room.”

Sela looked at the red for a long moment.

A drop of water came through the shutter and struck the margin below the entry.

Tomas moved the Ledger before the second drop fell. He placed a chipped cup under the leak, found that it rocked, and folded a scrap of old cloth beneath it.

Sela watched him do it.

“Does the book always live in a room that leaks?”

“Only when it rains.”

“It rains often.”

“The north roof is on Ansel’s list.”

The cup ticked as the water fell. Tomas dried the damp margin with his sleeve, careful not to touch the ink.

Sela had imagined the Ledger guarded by iron, perhaps by a person with a sword. Instead it sat in a room with mildew at the corners and a cup under the shutter.

“Who rules the lines?” she asked.

“Novices.”

“Who mixes the red?”

“The kitchen clerk, when there is need.”

“And if she mixes it wrong?”

“It dries brown. The judgment remains the same.”

Tomas turned the page. His fingers hesitated at a repair in the binding, two rough stitches through the leather.

“I made those,” he said. “Badly.”

Sela bent closer. One stitch had pulled too tight and puckered the skin.

“It holds.”

“That is not the same as well.”

The cup ticked again. Neither spoke until the drop came a third time.

Then she said: “What if you can’t tell?”

---

Tomas did not answer at once.

The pause lasted long enough for Sela to notice.

“Then there is a third thing,” he said.

“Show me.”

“I can’t.” He opened the book again, at a place he did not have to search for, and turned it towards her. “Because there’s nothing to show you.”

A date stood at the head of the page. Beneath it, the entry began without a name.

The ink ended mid-word. The ruled space below had remained empty for two hundred years.

“When the Tester can write neither *true* nor *false*, the entry stops. The page remains open.”

“And what happens to them?”

“Nothing,” said Tomas. “They live.”

Warmth entered his voice on the word *live*. He heard it. His left thumb pressed the edge of the table until the nail whitened.

“They are Unwritten. They stay if the house has work, and it always has work. They eat. They grow old.”

Sela was frowning at the empty half-page.

“Is that a kindness?” she said.

“Roche chose life when certainty failed. Before him, doubt went up the mountain. We say his name in the winter rite.”

Outside, in the passage, someone went by.

A bad boot scuffed past. A wooden box knocked once against a hip. Wax gave off its faint warm smell in the cold passage.

Tomas kept his eyes on the empty page.

“So they stay,” Sela said. “And live.”

“Yes.”

“And what are they *called*?”

“I told you. The Unwritten.”

“No,” said Sela. “I mean their names. What are their names?”

---

Tomas had not anticipated the question. He disliked that enough to note it.

“They keep their names,” said Tomas.

“Do they? Who says them?”

“In law, rite, inheritance and testimony an Unwritten is not addressed. There is no entered person for the words to attach to.”

“So nobody says their name.”

“In the house’s own voice, no.”

“Then how many are there?”

Tomas put his hand flat on the closed board.

“Forty-one,” he said.

Sela looked up.

“Forty-one. The pages can be counted.” Wind lifted two leaves. “I counted them when I was forty.”

“So you know how many.”

“I know how many.”

“But you don’t know *who*.”

Tomas did not answer.

“You don’t know who,” said Sela.

“No.”

“It’s your *book*—”

“The name belongs to the verdict. No verdict, no name. The working paper is burned that night.” His voice changed and he could not correct it. “The house knows forty-one people entered the room and came out in neither colour.”

“It cannot give you one of their names.”

Sela looked at the empty ruling. Her finger followed a line without touching it.

“Then it isn’t mercy,” she said.

“It is mercy. It is life instead of the mountain—”

“He would not kill them,” Sela said. “So he kept them alive and took away the part people could say.” She looked up. “Was Roche clever?”

“He was a good man.” Tomas spoke too loudly.

“I believe you,” said Sela.

“Brother Tomas,” she said. “Who decided the third thing was a mercy?”

“Roche.”

“Did he ask them?”

Tomas said nothing.

“Did anyone ever ask them,” said Sela, “whether they’d rather have been written down?”

---

He dismissed her.

Her steps stopped once in the passage, then continued.

He sat down on the stool.

Paper and char lay beside the Ledger. It had not been a testing. There should have been nothing to record.

He wrote three words. They were not about Sela. Then he burned them under the rule he had repeated for sixteen years: no one may read how a judgment came to be, including the man who made it.

The paper vanished in seconds.

He opened the Ledger at the unfinished page.

There were, in the working half of the book, a number of pages like it.

He knew the other blank places. One was five years old and in his hand. He could have found it with the room dark. He did not turn to it.

The shutter banged until he rose and fastened it. Below, Fen carried candles past three hundred names, stopping at each cup to trim the wick.

---

## Chapter Six
### First Snow

The first snow fell straight down from a white sky.

No one in the Bell remarked on it. Wood came in. Shutters closed. Ansel complained about a swollen hinge. Two novices laughed in the kitchen passage. The date remained in every room.

Tomas went out at dusk with his cloak on and told nobody where he was going, and nobody asked, because for sixteen years nobody had asked.

Merta was already at the turn of the path.

She came up early. She always came up early, and she never came onto house ground, and she never once in sixteen years had given them the smallest excuse.

The path from the valley climbed to a shoulder below the Bell and turned there, sharply, at a slab of rock the shepherds called the elbow, and it was at the elbow that the house’s ground began — not by any wall, but by a line in an old grant that four hundred years of Whitehart had agreed was the line. Merta stood below the elbow. She stood on the valley’s side of a boundary that existed on a page in a chest in a cold room.

She was fifty-two years old and she had walked up in the snow with a bundle under her arm and she stood there and did not sing, did not pray, did not weep.

She unwrapped the bundle. It was a coat.

It was a boy’s coat, and it had been let out twice, and there was a patch on the elbow of it done in the wrong colour thread because sixteen years ago a woman had not been able to get the right colour and had been in a hurry.

Merta shook it out once, folded it over her arm, and faced the mountain as though someone might yet come in from the cold.

That was the vigil. She would stand until the light was entirely gone and then she would fold the coat and walk back down.

Tomas stopped at the top of the rise, where the rock threw a shadow even in that grey light, and stood in it, and did not go on.

For sixteen winters Tomas had watched from the same rock. He had never let Merta see him.

---

Sister Wilda came down the path a little before full dark.

Tomas heard Wilda’s unhurried tread and moved deeper into the rock’s shadow.

Wilda stopped at the elbow. She stood on the house’s side of it. She folded her hands.

“Merta,” she said.

“Sister.”

“You know I have to.”

“I know you like to.”

“That’s not true,” said Wilda, “and you’ve never believed it, and you say it every year, and I’d think better of you if you’d stop.” She said it without heat. She said everything without heat. “You are below the line. You may stand there as long as you please. It is the valley’s ground and the valley may do as it likes on it.”

“I know where the line is.”

“Then you know the rest of it.” Wilda’s voice did not rise at all. “You may not say the name on this mountain. Not on our ground, not from below it, not in our hearing. Not the name of one who was Given Back.”

Merta did not answer.

“It is not a punishment of *you*,” said Wilda. “It has never been a punishment of you, and I have told you this in every one of these years, and I will tell you again, because it is true and because it is owed you. It is not to spite a mother. It is because a name spoken over that ground is a claim, and a claim on the Given Back is a claim that the testing was wrong, and if the testing can be wrong then every soul in that house is standing on nothing.”

“He was seventeen,” said Merta.

“I know what he was.”

“You walked him up.”

“I did,” said Wilda.

And the snow came down between them, straight, without wind.

“So you may stand there,” said Wilda, “and you may hold the coat, and I will not have you moved, and if any novice of mine so much as looks at you sideways they will answer to me for it. And you may not say his name. And I am sorry.”

“No, you’re not.”

“No,” Wilda said. “Not as you would have me be.”

She stood there a while longer. Then she turned and went back up the path, and passed within four feet of the rock where Tomas stood in the shadow, and did not see him, or did, and said nothing.

---

Sela came down fast, badly dressed for snow, and stopped at the elbow.

Tomas shifted forward, then stopped. There was no explanation for the rock that did not make him smaller.

Sela reached the elbow and stopped. She looked at Merta. Merta looked back up at her.

“You’re the new one,” said Merta.

“I’m Sela.”

“I know what you are.”

Sela came down two steps — down, past the elbow, onto the valley’s ground, and Tomas felt something in his chest turn cold, because she had crossed a line she did not know was there and had done it as naturally as walking.

“Is that a coat?” said Sela.

“It’s his coat.”

“He’ll be cold, if he’s out.”

Merta’s mouth opened. Tomas shut his eyes.

“Sixteen years,” said Merta, “I have stood on this ground with this coat, and every one of them has come down that path to tell me I may not say his name, and not one of them — *not one*, girl — has ever said the thing you just said.”

“What thing?”

“*If he’s out.*” Merta’s hands closed on the coat. “As if he were a person who might be somewhere. As if he were out in the weather and not — ” she stopped. “They talk about him like a page. Like a decision. They’ve talked about him like a decision for sixteen years and I have gone half out of my mind for want of one person to talk about him like a *boy*.”

Sela stood in the snow with her hands at her sides.

“What was he like?” she said.

---

Tomas heard about a boy who would not get out of bed, sang the rite wrong to make his sister laugh and feared goats at fourteen. Col kept tending them because it was his work.

Merta laughed once. Tomas remembered the two red words he had written at twenty-nine. The hand had been the same one he used now.

“And now,” said Merta, “you.”

Sela said nothing.

“They’ll put you in black and keep you, or put you in red and send you up. Either way, the page gets you.”

“That isn’t fair.”

“No,” said Merta. “None of it is. That’s the whole of what I’ve learned.”

And then she said, quite calmly, as though asking for a light:

“Say his name.”

---

The snow came down.

“They won’t let me say it,” said Merta. “Not here. Sixteen years. But you’re the Called. You’re the one who’s supposed to hear the dead — so you can *say* them, can’t you? If you’re real. If you’re real you can say a dead boy’s name on your own mountain and nobody can touch you for it.”

Sela stood there.

Tomas did not breathe. Sela was not asking what the rule allowed. He knew that much.

She looked at the coat, then at Merta. Snow gathered in her hair.

“I don’t know if I hear anything,” said Sela.

“I didn’t ask you to hear him,” said Merta. “I asked you to *say* him.”

And Sela’s face changed.

“Col,” she said.

The name was quiet. A neighbour’s name, spoken on a path.

“Col,” she said again. “Merta’s son. Col.”

Merta made a noise like a woman going into cold water.

Merta shook once. She folded the coat, made the first fold crooked, and did it again.

“Go up, girl. It’s cold.”

And Sela went up.

She passed within twenty feet of Tomas without seeing him. Snow had melted in her hair and run down both cheeks.

---

Tomas began with the rule. A Called under testing had named one Given Back on house ground. There were witnesses. The house would know by morning.

Then the other fact remained.

Sela had said the name in an ordinary voice.

Merta went down with the coat. Tomas watched until the snow covered her.

He stepped onto the boundary. One boot stood on house ground and one on the valley’s.

Snow filled Merta’s prints.

“Col,” Tomas said. The word caught. He breathed and tried again.

“Col,” he said again. “Merta’s son. I’m sorry.”

No one answered. Tomas stayed until cold passed through his boots. Then he went up, burned the day’s notes and ate supper with the others.

---

## Chapter Seven
### The East Shoulder

The snow brought it down.

The snow lay wet for three days. On the fourth morning a shepherd followed a stray ewe and found part of the east shoulder gone.

He told Ansel. Ansel told Osric. Before Tomas heard, half the house was already climbing. Sela went because otherwise they would have fetched her.

She had not been up the east shoulder in two months.

She had kept away and called the grazing poor.

The turf had split from the rock.

At the head of the slip, soil had peeled from worked stone.

Somebody had cut a hollow in the rock and put a lid of stone over it.

The hollow was smaller than a byre door. Broken lid-slabs lay down the slope. No one could say when it had last been open.

There was one person in it.

Sela’s first thought was that the body had been placed carefully.

The body lay face down.

It lay face down with both arms drawn under the chest. Someone had turned it, set the limbs and roofed the cut.

The close-woven wool had survived when it should not have. It was fine, tightly woven and unlike anything made in the Light Heights. Beneath a tear at the shoulder lay a newer binding, pale where the outer layer was dark. Osric reached towards it and stopped before touching.

And the hands were gone.

Both hands had been removed cleanly at the wrists. They were not in the grave or on the slope.

No griffe. No niche. No inscription. No mark cut anywhere on any stone.

“There’s no name,” said somebody, behind her.

Sixty people looked down. No one answered.

---

The grave had been maintained. A narrow channel carried meltwater away from the lid; no grass grew in its scraped bed.

The lid-stones were of different ages. Two bore old tool marks. One had been cut with a tool still used in the valley and set by someone who meant it to hold.

Someone had returned after the burial. Growth had been cut back. Stones had been reset.

No one in the Bell of Silence had known. No surviving account identifies the keeper.

Sela, standing at the lip, looked at the place where the hands were not.

The missing hands held her attention. She could not say why.

---

“They were not prayed for.”

Osric said it first. He was quick enough to see the problem and willing enough to name it.

“No griffe,” said Osric. “No niche. No name cut anywhere. If the house had put that person there, the house would have named them, and there is nothing. So either they were put there by people who were not us—”

“—or they were put there by us, and left out on purpose,” said Tomas.

“Yes.”

The wind came off the ice.

“And if that is one of ours,” said Osric, and now he was careful, and now he was watching Tomas’s face, “then this house has a dead man it does not pray for, outside its own line, with his hands off and his face in the stone — and every soul standing on this hill is going to work that out by supper.”

---

Ivet reached a different answer before supper.

“East,” she was saying, ten paces off, to a knot of novices with their hoods up against the wind. “*East.* She said east at the trial. Everyone heard it. And now the ground has opened in the east and there is a soul in it who was never prayed for, and who else was going to find him? Who else was ever going to *find* him?”

“I didn’t say east,” said Sela.

She said it loudly. Several people turned.

“I said north. The winter herd went north.” Sela heard her own voice harden. “I did not know this was here. I have never been up here.”

Ivet looked at her with patient love.

“No,” she agreed. “Of course you didn’t.”

She turned back to the novices.

“*She doesn’t know. That’s how you can tell.*”

---

It was Tomas who went down into the cut.

Tomas handed off his cloak and climbed into the cut. Loose soil filled his right boot. He stopped once to empty it, sitting on a broken lid-stone while sixty people watched. Then he climbed down again.

The crowd shifted when his boots touched the grave bed. No rite covered what he was doing.

He looked down at the body for a while.

Then he knelt.

He knelt, removed his cap and set one hand on the rock beside the body.

The common office required a name. Everyone on the ridge knew the place where it belonged.

Tomas came to the place where the name went.

And he stopped.

Tomas stopped there, kneeling in the wind.

Then he said:

“—*and thy name is not known to us, and we say it anyway.*”

He did not sing it. He spoke to the dead in the plain voice used for the living.

He completed the office without looking up. Sela saw Osric watching.

Beside her, an old woman was weeping. Two novices had gone down on their knees in the snow.

Osric’s lips parted. He was counting consequences.

---

Wilda arrived too fast.

She stopped at the lip and put one hand on the rock.

She stood there a long time.

“How old?” said Wilda.

“Old,” said Osric. “The bone’s clean. It’s not years, Sister. It’s a long way past years.”

“You’re certain.”

“I’m not certain of anything. But nobody in that hole died in my lifetime, or my father’s.”

Wilda closed her eyes.

Two heartbeats later her face was controlled again.

“Then he is not ours to name, and not ours to move, and the house has no rite for him, and we are standing on him. Get everyone back from the edge.”

She moved sixty frightened people back from the slip without one fall. She put novices at the soft edge, sent the oldest down first and made Osric count them twice. When the last child was clear, she found that she was still gripping the same piece of rock.

Sela watched Wilda’s hands. They shook once when no one needed directing.

---

Merta came up the mountain in the afternoon.

Novices were turning valley people back at the elbow. Merta came with mud to both knees and the old coat under one arm. The first novice stepped into her path, recognised her and stepped out again. No one stopped her.

She passed Wilda and stood over the grave.

The whole mountain went quiet.

Sixteen years earlier Col had gone east over the same shoulder with bread for one day. Everyone on the ridge knew it.

Merta looked into the hole for a long time.

“It’s old,” she said.

“Very old,” Tomas said. “It is not him.”

“No,” said Merta.

She kept looking.

Merta went down without speaking. Sela followed.

---

Mother Alaine came at the end of the day, when the light was almost gone.

The crowd opened for Alaine. She looked into the cut briefly.

“Close it,” she said.

There was a silence.

“Mother,” said Osric carefully, “there may be a great deal to learn from—”

“Close it. Use stone from the fall. Set turf over it. Keep vigil until the last stone is laid.” Alaine looked once at the body. “Someone laid him down and someone kept this place. We will not pull him apart to learn about ourselves.”

“And the office?” said Tomas.

Alaine looked at him.

For one breath Alaine’s face changed. Sela saw it and did not know what it was.

“You have already said it,” said Alaine.

Then she walked back down the path.

They closed the grave over two days. The house remembered Alaine’s mercy and certainty.

The high-valley songs remember five breaths before the order.

Neither account records what she feared.

---

Sela caught Merta halfway down, at the elbow, where the boundary was.

Merta had stopped on the boundary.

“Merta.”

“Go up, girl.”

“It was not him. He had been there for hundreds of years.”

“I know.”

“So he could still—”

“I *know*,” said Merta.

Merta’s shoulders folded.

“I wanted it to be.”

Sela did not move.

“I looked at that poor body and wanted it to be Col.” Her breath caught. “Then I could put the coat away.”

She wiped her face with her palm.

“Sixteen years I’ve stood on this stone,” she said. “And the worst thing that ever happened to me today was that it wasn’t him.”

Then she went down.

Sela remained until cold reached through her boots. Above, men argued over which stone would lie flat. One dropped a slab and swore. The sound came down the shoulder in short hard blows.

She kept her eyes on the path and her hands clenched. Nothing came, or she would not let it. She could not tell which.

---

## Chapter Eight
### Nothing Happened

By the end of the week there were fires on the road.

You could see them from the upper gallery after dark: little smudges of orange strung out down the valley where people had stopped for the night on their way up, because the inns below were full and Bryn Corr’s barn was full and the byres of three separate families were full, and there was nowhere left except the road itself.

They were not pilgrims. Not yet. Nobody had used that word out loud.

They were people who had heard that the ground had opened on a mountain, and that a girl had been told about it beforehand by someone who was dead, and who had put on their boots and come to see, in the cold, in the dark, sleeping in ditches, on the strength of a story that had crossed forty miles of bad weather in nine days.

Sela stood at the gallery and counted the fires and stopped counting.

“Don’t,” said Fen, behind her. “It’s bad for you.”

He was doing the niches, as he always was, working along the lower row with the rag and the bone knife and the box at his feet.

She sat down on the flags beside him and picked up a stub of candle and began scraping the old wax out of it with her thumbnail, badly, and he let her do it wrong for a while before he said anything.

“You’re pushing,” he said. “Don’t push, pull. It comes out in one piece if you pull.” He did not look up. “You’ve the hands for it. You’ve a goatherd’s hands, which is the same as a candle-tender’s hands, which is a thing I would rather you didn’t repeat to anyone, since it’s the only professional dignity I’ve got.”

“What do they say about me? In the house.”

“Ah,” said Fen. “Straight in. No weather, no *how are you, Fen*, no *have you eaten, Fen*—”

“Have you eaten?”

“No. Ansel’s forgotten me twice this week, which is what he does when there’s something on and he doesn’t want to be seen making decisions.” He knocked wax into his palm. “They say the mountain opened for you. They say you told Brother Tomas the direction at your trial and the ground obeyed it. There’s a novice from the low pasture telling people you said *east* out loud in the listening room, and that the whole house heard it, and that three of them turned to look.”

“I never said east.”

“I know.”

“I said *north*. I said it clearly. It’s written down—”

“I *know*,” said Fen. “I was in the passage. I heard the whole thing through the arch like everyone else in this house who was pretending to be asleep.” He set the candle in its cup and moved down. “It doesn’t matter what you said. That’s what I’ve been trying to work out how to tell you for four days without sounding like I’ve something to gain from it.”

Sela put the ruined candle down.

“They’re going to ask you,” said Fen. “Publicly. In the hall, with everybody there. Osric wants it, because he wants a ruling on the grave and he can’t get one out of Alaine and he can’t get one out of Wilda, and he’s worked out that he can get one out of *you* without either of them being able to stop it. And your friend Ivet wants it, because she loves you, and because she’s been telling half the novices for a week that you’ll speak when the time comes, and now she needs you to speak or she’s a liar.”

“Ivet’s not—”

“Ivet is the most dangerous person in this house and she has not had a single unkind thought in her life,” said Fen. “Those two facts are not in conflict. They’re the same fact.”

---

“I’ll just tell them the truth,” said Sela.

Fen stopped working.

He sat back on his heels and looked at her with the rag in one hand and the wax in the other, and for once there was nothing funny in his face at all.

“Yes,” he said. “That’s what I’m afraid of.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means—” He stopped. He started again. “Look. You think a lie is the dangerous thing. Everybody does. A lie can be caught, a lie can be argued with, a lie has an *edge* on it that somebody can get a fingernail under.” He turned the rag over in his hands. “The truth hasn’t. The truth from a person who’s got no reason to be telling it is the most useful material in the world, and there’s a room full of people up there who need something to build with, and you are about to hand them a load of dressed stone and tell them it’s nothing.”

“But it *is* nothing. Nothing happened. I didn’t know the grave was there.”

“I believe you,” said Fen. “*They will too.*” He picked up the box. “That’s the problem.”

Ansel found them before Sela could answer.

“The lower camp has no dry blankets,” he said.

He spoke to Fen, because Fen was holding the box, and to Sela, because she was there. His eyes rested on neither.

“There are twelve in the west store,” Fen said.

“There are eight.”

“There were twelve yesterday.”

“Four are under pilgrims who lied about fever.”

Ansel handed Sela a bundle of cord. “Tie the kitchen cloths in pairs. They can sleep under two layers. Do not call them blankets. If they think we have blankets, there will be fifty more by morning.”

They worked in the passage. The cloths were meant for covering flour and had old white streaks ground into them. Fen tied quickly. Sela’s first knot slipped.

“A goat would leave you,” he said.

“A goat has more sense than a pilgrim.”

“That is nearly funny. Be careful.”

His own next knot caught the edge of his sleeve and had to be cut loose. Sela looked at him.

“Nothing happened,” Fen said.

They carried the bundles down to the gate. Rain had turned the road into black paste. A woman from the coast stood barefoot under the wall, holding her boots so the leather would not rot. Beside her, a child slept sitting up.

When Sela gave them the cloths, the woman touched two fingers to her forehead.

“I heard what you said in the hall,” she whispered.

“I haven’t said it yet.”

The woman smiled through cracked lips. “You will.”

Sela opened her mouth, then saw Ansel counting heads by the gate and a novice watching from the stairs.

She tucked the loose corner around the sleeping child instead.

By supper, someone had told Ivet that the Called had gone among the road people before speaking and covered a child with her own hands. The flour cloth had become a cloak in the telling.

---

They did it after the evening office, which meant it had been arranged, because nothing happened after the evening office by accident.

The long hall was full. Not just the house — there were valley people at the back, standing, who had no business being inside the walls after dark and whom nobody had turned out. Steward Ansel had put extra benches along the north wall and had done it without being asked, which was how you knew what the steward thought was about to happen.

Sela sat where they put her, which was near the front, which was new.

Brother Osric stood up.

He was very good. She could never afterwards get anyone to understand it: he was *good*, and he was not lying, and every single thing he said was reasonable.

He said that the house had found dead on its own mountain who had never been prayed for. He said that Brother Tomas had done a merciful and irregular thing in the moment, and that no one blamed him, and that the house had been grateful — and there was a murmur at that, which was warm, and Tomas did not lift his head. He said that Mother Alaine had closed the grave, wisely, to prevent it becoming a spectacle. And he said that the house was now in the position of having a mystery in the ground under its feet and no teaching about it, and that a house which has no teaching about a thing will invent one, badly, in the kitchens.

“So I would ask the Called,” said Osric, and he turned, and the whole hall turned with him, “who was the only one among us who ever spoke of the east before the ground opened: what does it mean?”

---

Sela stood up.

For one cold moment Sela saw how little was required.

She could say, *I felt something.* That much was true. Then she could stop. The hall would finish it for her.

Alaine was in the far corner, against the stone, with her back to the dead.

Sela did not look at her.

“Nothing happened,” she said.

The hall was very quiet.

“I didn’t know the grave was there,” said Sela. “I’ve never been up the east shoulder. I didn’t say east at my trial — I said *north*, and it was north, and it was two years ago, and it was about goats.” Her voice was going flat and hard and she let it. “I stood on that hill with the rest of you and I looked into that hole and I felt sick, and I looked at a man lying with his face in the stone, and I thought about the person who turned him over. That’s all I’ve got. I didn’t hear anything about that grave. Not words. Nothing I could prove came from the dead. I don’t know what it means.”

She stopped.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I know that’s not any use.”

And she sat down.

---

Afterwards, she could not have told you what she expected. Anger, perhaps. Disappointment. The particular silence of a room that has been let down.

What she got was Ivet, three benches back, beginning to cry.

And then the sound.

A murmur rose behind her. Sela kept both hands in her lap.

“*She refused it,*” someone said, quite clearly, behind her.

“She could have said anything,” said someone else. “She could have said *anything* and we’d have believed her. And she said *nothing happened.*”

And an old woman from the low pasture, who had walked up in the snow to be there, said the thing that got out of the hall and down the road and into the valley and never afterwards came back:

“*That’s how you know.*”

---

Brother Osric sat down slowly.

He had not got his ruling. He had got something else, and he was quick enough to see it before anyone else in the room, and what was on his face was not triumph and was not defeat.

He was watching the girl who had just become the most powerful person in the Bell of Silence by declining to be, and he was doing arithmetic, and he was not enjoying it.

Wilda stood against the side wall with her arms folded. She could object to no word Sela had spoken.

She looked, for a moment, at Mother Alaine.

And Sela — turning, because everyone was turning, because the hall was moving and she wanted to get out of it — saw Alaine’s face.

Alaine’s face was still, but the look she gave Sela was new and unreadable. Sela carried it with her.

Mother Alaine, who had said the right holy thing for fifty years, was watching a nineteen-year-old goatherd get more from the truth in one sentence than Alaine had ever got from a lifetime of saying the right thing.

And she could not tell — she genuinely could not tell, and it went into her like a splinter and stayed — whether she was looking at a fraud who was better at it than she was, or at the real thing.

---

They kept her in the hall a long time.

People wanted to be near her. Not to touch her — nobody touched her; that had stopped weeks ago — but to *be near*, to stand at a small respectful distance and look, and to say afterwards that they had been in the room.

And when at last they let her go, and she came down the length of the third bench with her hands in her sleeves, she did the thing she had been dreading all evening without ever putting it into words.

There was a bench by the arch. It was where she always sat. It faced the doorway, and the dark, and the drop, and the long open air of the valley, because that was where a person sat, and she had sat like that in every room she had ever been in.

She stopped.

There were forty people watching her. There were forty people who would see her sit down and turn herself, without thinking, towards the open door — and who would say, tomorrow, in the kitchens, in the valley, on the road, at the fires: *she faces the opening. Have you not seen? She sits facing the dark. She is listening even now.*

Sela walked to the bench.

And she sat down with her back to the arch.

It took everything she had. It was like holding her breath. Her whole body was wrong, every part of her wanted to turn, the draught was on her neck and she could not see the weather and there was open ground behind her that she could not watch — and she sat there, straight, with her face to the wall of the dead, and she made herself breathe.

Nobody noticed.

Except one person.

Fen was leaning in the passage with the empty candle-box under his arm, where he had been standing all evening, where nobody had thought to move him because nobody ever thought about him at all, and he watched her sit down facing the wrong way, and his face did something complicated.

He did not come over. He did not say anything.

He looked at her for a moment longer than a man looks at a room. Then he went away down the passage with his box.

---

She went out much later, when the house was asleep, in the coldest part of the night.

The listening room was empty. The griffes were dark. The wind came in through the arch at the mouth of the hall, off the valley, off the ice, carrying the smell of the fires on the road below.

Sela sat in the dark mouth of the arch with the house behind her.

She faced out.

She faced the open ground and the cold and the long drop and the black shoulder of the mountain, and she put her arms around her knees and sat there like a goatherd, like a girl, like nobody, and it was the first easy breath she had drawn in fourteen hours.

Nobody could see her.

Her shoulders came down. No one could see her here.

A fire went out far below. Another light moved steadily up the road. Sela watched it come.

She did not listen. She had stopped trusting anything she heard enough to give it a name.

She sat in the arch with her back to the dead until the cold drove her in, and the lantern came on up the valley behind her, and she did not know it, and it did not matter, because it was coming whether she knew it or not.

---

## Chapter Nine
### The Man Who Writes It Down

He came up at the pace of a man who had been on the road for a month and knew exactly how much of himself he had left, which is to say slowly, and without stopping, and he arrived at the gate at the worst possible hour on the worst possible night, soaked to the shoulder, leading a mule that plainly despised him.

Tomas was in the yard when they let him in.

The man handed off the mule, pulled back a hood, and looked around at the wet stone and the sleet and the four suspicious faces of House Whitehart with an expression of enormous and entirely sincere pleasure.

“Oh, that’s *beautiful*,” he said.

Nobody answered him. It was the middle of a freezing night and he had woken the gate.

“No, look at it. The valley narrows into your door. You hear weather, bells, carts. You heard me for hours.” He wiped his face with a wet sleeve and made it worse.

“We heard you,” said Tomas, “for three.”

The stranger laughed — a real one, brief and delighted — and put out his hand.

“Perrin,” he said. “Blackcrest. I’m here to write down everything you remember, and I am extremely sorry.”

Tomas noted the apology as he might have noted a good coat: chosen, useful, well fitted.

Tomas had spent sixteen years across a table from people who wanted something, and thirty in a house full of them.

He liked Perrin before the stranger had been inside a minute. Knowing the method did not prevent it.

---

They gave him the small guest cell and he was up before the house the next morning, and by midday he had learned the name of every person in the Bell of Silence.

Every person. Not the seniors. *Everyone*: the kitchen girls, the two old men who kept the ropes, the boy who did the fires. He asked, and he listened to the answer, and then the next time he passed them in a passage he used it.

“That is a trick,” said Sister Wilda, at the noon meal, watching him do it.

“Yes,” Tomas agreed.

“You seem untroubled by that.”

“It’s a trick that costs him something to keep up and costs them nothing,” said Tomas. “I’ve seen worse tricks played for less.” He watched the Herald stop in the doorway and say something to Steward Ansel that made Ansel — Ansel, who had not been surprised by anything since the reign of the last Mother — throw back his head and laugh out loud. “And I would like to know, Sister, at what point in the last forty years we became a house where a stranger learning our names is regarded as an attack.”

Wilda did not answer that.

She looked at him for a moment with something in her face that was almost, but not quite, respect.

---

He explained himself in the hall, and he explained himself well.

Four years, he said, to the four hundredth year. Four years to the Jubilee, and Blackcrest was making the great retelling of the Chronicle — the whole realm’s memory, gathered and set down and bound, and it would be read at the Jubilee and it would stand for the next four hundred years, and after that it would simply *be* what happened.

“Which is why I am not sitting in Crown-watch copying out the old one,” said Perrin. “The old one was written by men in a room. This one is going to have you in it.”

He let that sit.

“I want the valley’s memory,” he said. “Not your house’s. I have the house’s — I have four hundred years of the house’s, I could recite it to you and bore you insensible. I want what the woman who milks knows about the winter of the wolves. I want what your steward remembers about the year the pass closed. I want the songs nobody sings any more because they were only ever sung in three houses in one valley and two of those houses have fallen down.” He spread his hands. “And I will get almost none of it. You know that, and I know that. People don’t tell a stranger what they know; they tell him what they think he wants. So I will be here a long time, and I will be a nuisance, and I will ask you the same question four different ways, and I am going to eat a great deal of your food.”

Somebody laughed.

“But when I am gone,” said Perrin, “there will be a book in Crown-watch with the name of this valley in it. And in four hundred years, when everyone in this room is a line in it, that will be the only reason anyone knows you were here at all.”

---

Tomas watched the hall.

He watched a hundred people who had spent their whole lives being the sort of people that nothing is written about, sitting up straighter.

---

The road was the thing that decided it, and it decided it on the third day.

There were four hundred people on that road by then — the count came from Ansel and Ansel did not exaggerate about anything that could be eaten — camped along the verges in the wet, with the sleet coming, and no fuel, and the house feeding them out of its winter stores because the alternative was to watch them starve at the gate.

Wilda wanted them turned back. Osric wanted them regulated. Alaine had said nothing at all for two days, which Tomas had begun to recognise as her most active state.

Perrin listened to the argument for an hour without saying a word.

Then he asked whether he might go down and look.

He was gone all afternoon. He came back at dusk, filthy, with his boots ruined and his hands blue, and he had done the following: he had found nine families in the valley with an empty byre, a barn, or a roof; he had gone to Bryn Corr and got a price out of her — a hard one, and he paid it out of a purse Tomas had not known he was carrying; he had put the sickest people under cover first and made a list of who was where; he had told the rest, plainly, that there was not room for everyone and that some of them would have to go home, and he had told them so directly and so cheerfully that most of them agreed with him; and he had arranged for the house’s bread to be carried down the road rather than fought over at the gate, which meant that within a day the crowd at the gate was gone.

Nobody died on the road that week.

Perrin returned after dark with one boot split at the heel.

Tomas found him in the kitchen passage trying to force a strip of rawhide through the sole with a meat skewer. The mule stood outside under the eaves and struck the door with its nose every few breaths.

“That is a kitchen tool,” Tomas said.

“It is also a narrow piece of iron. Blackcrest believes in flexible institutions.”

The skewer bent.

Perrin looked at it. “The institution has objected.”

Tomas brought a proper awl from the repair chest. They sat on overturned buckets while the kitchen women worked around them. No one gave up a table for a herald. One woman stepped over Perrin’s legs three times before telling him to move.

“You knew where to send the sick first,” Tomas said.

Perrin pulled the rawhide tight with his teeth. “I asked who coughed blood.”

“You paid Corr before you knew whether Crown-watch would repay you.”

“Crown-watch will repay me. It will complain for six months and repay me in the seventh.”

The mule struck the door again.

“Does it always do that?” Tomas asked.

“Only when ignored. We have much in common.”

The line was ready before the question. Tomas heard that. Perrin saw him hear it and looked down at the boot.

“She bites anyone who calls her pretty,” he said.

“Is she pretty?”

“No. That is why she resents the lie.”

The heel held when he stood. It was an ugly repair.

“Good enough for the road,” Perrin said.

Tomas nearly answered that good enough was not a category he trusted. Instead he handed back the bent skewer.

Perrin set it beside the washing basin for the cooks to find in the morning.

They would have. Tomas knew it, and Ansel knew it, and Wilda knew it, and none of them said so.

“You paid Corr,” said Tomas that night, in the passage, when they were alone.

“I did.”

“Out of your own purse.”

“Out of Blackcrest’s purse. I’ll enter it, and Crown-watch will pay it, and in twenty years someone will read the entry and think what a generous house we are.” Perrin was scraping the mud off his boots with a knife and doing a bad job of it. “It’s the cheapest thing I’ve done all year, Brother. Four hundred people freeze on a road below a holy house at the exact moment that house acquires a saint, and *that* is what the Chronicle says about this valley for four hundred years. Nobody would ever hear of anything else that happened here.”

Tomas said, “So it was for the book.”

“It was for the book,” said Perrin. “And they’re not dead. Both of those are true and I’ve never worked out how to say them in an order that doesn’t make one of them a lie.”

And he looked up, and he grinned, and it was the most likeable thing Tomas had seen in a decade, and Tomas — who had made his life’s work out of the space between what a person does and why they say they did it — found that he could not get his knife into that sentence anywhere.

---

They came to it two nights later, over the last of the wine, and Tomas walked into it himself.

“You burn them,” said Perrin.

“Every one. The same night.”

“Why?”

“Because no one may read how a judgment came to be.” Tomas had said it a thousand times. It came out smooth. “Not the house, not the tested, not the Tester. A judgment must stand on itself or not at all. If a man knows his working will be read, he writes for the reader.”

Perrin turned his cup around on the table.

“That’s a good argument,” he said. “That’s a *very* good argument. I want you to know I’m not being clever with you when I say what I’m about to say.”

“Say it.”

“Who checks you?”

Tomas opened his mouth.

“You test a child,” said Perrin, “and you write down what you saw, and then you burn it, and then you tell the house what you concluded. And the house believes you, because you are a good man and you have never once been wrong that anybody could prove.” He was not smiling now. He was not unkind either. He was, Tomas realised with a slow cold feeling in his chest, *being careful with him.* “And that is the entire system. There is no other part to it. You have built a rite where the only evidence is destroyed by the only man with an interest in destroying it, and you did it for the purest reason anybody has ever had, and I have read a great many chronicles, Brother, and I am telling you that this is what every one of them looks like from the inside for the first four hundred years.”

“That is not—” said Tomas.

He stopped.

He sat in the small warm room with his hands flat on the table and he could not find the end of the sentence.

He had said the words for the unknown dead in a hole on the east shoulder eight days ago because a name he could not supply had stopped his mouth in the middle of a rite. He had a page in the Ledger, five years old, that stopped after four words, in his own hand. He had a boy’s name in his chest that he had said out loud into the snow, once, in sixteen years, when there was nobody to hear it.

“I don’t know,” said Tomas.

Perrin nodded slowly, and did not press, and refilled his cup, and began — with impeccable timing, with genuine kindness, and quite deliberately — to talk about something else.

---

Later, in the passage, Tomas came round the corner and stopped.

Perrin was standing at the mouth of the listening room with his hands behind his back, looking at the wall of the dead in the dark, and the candle-tender was working along the lower row with a rag, and they were talking.

“—no, but *how*,” Perrin was saying. “That’s what I don’t understand. You’ve what, three hundred of them? Four? And they’re not marked in any order I can see. It’s not by house rank, it’s not by year—”

“It’s by year,” said Fen. “It’s just not by *our* year. The old ones counted from the founding of the seat, not from the Unification. Everyone forgot. So it looks like nothing.” He did not look up. He was scraping wax. “You have to know where the join is. There’s a gap of about forty years in the middle of the third row where they changed over and nobody bothered writing anything down.”

There was a silence.

Tomas, in the dark of the passage, saw the Herald turn his whole body towards the young man with the rag.

“*How do you know that?*”

“Because I clean them,” said Fen. “Nobody else has looked at that wall in fifty years.”

“How many people in this house know what you just told me?”

Fen shrugged. “None of them. They know the names. They don’t know the *wall*.” He knocked the wax out. “It’s not a secret. Nobody’s ever asked.”

Perrin stood there a moment longer.

Then he said: “Thank you, Fen.”

And he went off down the passage to bed.

---

Tomas stood in the dark and thought nothing at all about it.

Years later Tomas would remember hearing every word and registering only this: Perrin had used Fen’s name and waited for the answer. Tomas thought it decent.

He thought: *that was decent of him.*

He thought: *someone should have asked that boy about the wall years ago.*

And he went to bed.

---

He tested a novice the next morning — a small routine thing, a boy of fifteen who was frightened and honest and no more Called than the mule — and he wrote his line on his paper, as he always did, and he came back to his cell that evening with the paper in his sleeve and the iron dish on the table and the flint beside it.

And he sat down.

And he did not burn it.

He told himself it was because he was tired. He told himself the boy’s case was clear and would keep. He put the paper under the dish, where it could not blow, and he lay down in his clothes, and he did not sleep.

He lay in the dark for a long time with a single sheet of paper on the table three feet away and the whole cold weight of House Whitehart on top of him, and the thing was not that he doubted his judgments.

The thing was that he had never, in sixteen years, been able to look at one afterwards.

He burned it in the morning.

He got up at first light and struck the flint and held the paper over the dish and watched it go, exactly as he had done every day for sixteen years, and it took the same three seconds it always took, and nothing whatever was different about it.

Except that between the writing and the burning there had been twelve hours.

And in all his life — in every winter, every trial, every child he had walked up that mountain in his mind and left there — there had never once been twelve hours before.

## Chapter Ten
### Nobody Ever Asked

It isn’t a secret.

Fen told himself that while he worked the lower row.

The wall stood in a public room. Three hundred and some names, cut into stone, with candles under them. Anyone could count from the founding of the seat instead of the Unification. Anyone could find the forty-year break in the third row where the numbers changed and no one left a note.

The cup beneath Old Merra’s griffe had cracked. Fen turned it in his hand, checked the split with his thumbnail and set it aside. He would need clay from the kitchen stores. Ansel would complain. Ansel always complained before giving him what he needed.

Nobody had asked about the break in the years.

That was all.

He scraped the old wax from the next cup. The bone knife caught and jumped. A strip of wax landed on his sleeve.

“Excellent,” he said to the dead woman above him. “Now I look employed.”

No one laughed. The joke had been better in his head.

Footsteps came through the passage. Sister Wilda stopped behind him.

“The fourth lamp on the north wall is smoking,” she said.

“It has been doing that for twenty years. I thought it was tradition.”

“It is soot.”

“A younger tradition.”

Wilda looked at the lamp, not at him. “See to it before the evening office.”

She set a folded cloth on the stone beside his box. Clean linen. The old cloth in his hand had gone stiff at the edges and left grey threads on the cups.

“This is not mine,” Fen said.

“No,” said Wilda. “The linen has been provided.”

She went on.

At twenty, when the lung-fever took him, Wilda had sat beside his bed for two nights. She changed the water when it warmed and held the basin when he coughed blood. On the third morning, once he could breathe without help, she told him the south row had gone dark and asked when he meant to return to work.

Fen had loved her for both things and disliked her for neither. It was inconvenient.

Ansel came later with the midday bread. He left a heel on the edge of the box and began counting candles.

“There are thirty-six,” Fen said.

“There were forty yesterday.”

“Four became saints and rose bodily into the roof.”

Ansel counted again.

Fen waited.

“Thirty-six,” Ansel said.

“A miracle in the other direction.”

Ansel pushed the bread closer without looking at him. “The candles have been seen to poorly this week.”

“The candles object to criticism from men who cannot count them.”

Ansel grunted and left.

At the formal meal that evening he would read the labour aloud. Wood carried. Roof patched. Two goats returned from the lower slope. The candles had been seen to.

No name would follow.

At the winter rite the Tester asked who stood witness before the griffes. A hundred mouths answered. Fen kept his closed. His answer had nowhere in the book to land.

He had tried once, in his second year. Not loudly. Just enough to feel the word in his own throat.

The woman beside him had turned, frightened, and missed the next response.

He had not tried again.

It isn’t a secret.

He changed the cracked cup.

---

At the autumn market his cousin sold onions beside the ford.

Fen had gone down for lamp oil. He stood three stalls away while a trader from the west asked whether she was kin to the young man who kept the Bell’s candles.

His cousin looked across the crowd. She saw Fen. He knew she saw him because her hand stopped on the scale.

“No,” she said.

Then she added another onion to the trader’s measure.

Four children depended on her portion. The youngest had been ill. A declared tie to an Unwritten man could bring questions about inheritance, and questions cost coin even when the answer was obvious.

Fen understood every part of it before she finished weighing the onions.

Later he told a kitchen girl that his family had disowned him because he cheated at bowls. The girl laughed. Fen improved the story until he had stolen a priest’s trousers and hidden them in a well.

He used it twice more that winter.

By spring it had become a good story.

His cousin never heard it.

---

Nessa brings the flour on Thursdays.

She comes with a sack on one hip and mill dust along both forearms. In the yard, where the kitchen girls and novices can hear, she says, “The flour is here.”

Fen takes it.

He does not say her name. She does not say his.

At nineteen they planned to live in the little north house with the bad roof. They argued all summer about whether the roof could be saved. Fen said yes. Nessa said no and smiled whenever he became too certain.

She knew the scar on his shoulder. When she first found it in the dark, he told her a long story about a wolf.

She waited until he finished.

“Goats,” she said.

“Several wolves shaped like goats.”

Her brother struck the wall because they laughed too loudly.

Fen was tested that autumn. The page did not close.

A marriage had to be entered. So did the children born under it. There was no line for him. Nobody held a council. Nessa’s father stopped repairing the north house. The roof went in during the next hard rain.

She waited through one winter. Perhaps two. Fen stopped counting when she married a man from the low pasture with four fields and a name that held ink.

Her eldest son is called Fen. Great-uncle Fen, everyone says. There was a great-uncle. The name is common.

On Thursdays, after the sack is stacked and the yard is empty, Nessa sometimes meets him in the passage.

“Mind your back, Fen,” she says.

Then she leaves.

It has been five years.

---

One spring the sack shifted.

The rope had loosened on the road. When Fen took the weight, the flour rolled sideways. Nessa caught the lower corner and he caught the top, and for a few breaths they stood close in the dark passage, holding it between them.

Neither let go.

The mill was in her hair. Smoke too, from the hearth in the low-pasture house.

She had asked him that winter to stand at the naming of her youngest.

“Not as witness,” she said quickly. “Only in the room.”

Fen said no before she finished.

Nessa answered, “Yes. Of course.”

At a naming, a witness spoke the child’s name and the clerk entered who had heard it. Fen could have stood at the back with the candle-box. Nobody would have driven him out.

She had not asked him to stand at the back.

The flour sack slipped another inch.

“Fen,” she said.

Not as warning. Not towards the air beside him.

He leaned forward before he had decided to.

Nessa put her forehead against his. Hard. Warm. One breath.

Then she stepped away, took the sack from him and set it on the flags.

“The tie is rotten,” she said. “Put a new one on before next week.”

She left.

Fen retied it twice.

The first knot would have held.

---

Perrin kept coming to the listening room.

On the first evening he sat on the wet flags in a Blackcrest coat and asked what the candles cost.

“Too much,” Fen said.

Perrin waited.

Fen gave him the number.

On the second evening Perrin asked why the north niches burned faster.

“The dead on that side were more sinful.”

Perrin looked towards the arch. “The draught?”

“You ruin everything.”

The joke did not make him laugh. He wrote down *draught* and asked where the wax came from.

On the third evening he brought two cups of hot ale. He gave one to Fen, then held the other between both hands until the heat stopped hurting him.

“The forty-year gap,” he said. “Where does it begin?”

Fen showed him.

Perrin counted the old marks, lost his place and started again. He did not pretend he understood before he did.

That was new enough to be suspicious.

When he had finished, he turned the cup on the stone.

“Who decides what goes in the Ledger?”

“Brother Tomas.”

“He tests and enters the judgment?”

“Yes.”

“Where are the working notes kept?”

“They aren’t.”

Perrin’s thumb stopped against the cup.

“He burns them,” Fen said. “Same night. One lamp, one sheet. He sits out of the draught and puts the tested in it. Hands in the light, face in shadow. He writes what he sees and burns it before the lamp cools.”

He scraped at a cup that was already clean.

“No one may read how a judgment came to be,” Fen continued. “Not the house. Not the tested. Not Tomas later, when he has forgotten why he was certain.”

Perrin did not move.

Fen heard the list after it was out. The lamp. The draught. The paper. He had given the man the whole room.

He picked at the same bit of stone.

“That’s the most frightening thing I’ve heard this year,” Perrin said.

Fen laughed. It came out too loud and ended too soon.

“He’s a good man.”

“I know.”

Perrin rubbed both eyes. “That is not helping.”

Fen looked down at the bone knife.

“The north row needs doing,” he said.

“You’ve done it.”

“It may have changed.”

Perrin studied him for a moment, then stood.

“Come and eat at my table tomorrow.”

Fen kept the knife against the cup.

“I have a table.”

“You have a wall.”

“It is a very good wall.”

“Tomorrow,” Perrin said. “At the second bench. I will save the end.”

He left his empty cup on the floor. Fen stared at it until the fourth lamp on the north wall began to smoke.

He had forgotten Wilda’s order.

---

The next evening Perrin called his name across the hall.

“Fen. Here.”

The room did not fall silent. It slowed.

Fen stood in the doorway with no box in his hands. He had left it in the passage and now regretted it. Empty hands were too visible.

Perrin had kept the end of the guest bench.

Ansel stopped with the ladle halfway over a bowl.

An Unwritten man could not sit at the head of a bench. A guest of Blackcrest had invited him. No rule covered both facts.

Ansel looked at Fen for one breath, then poured the stew and moved on.

Fen walked.

Ivet watched with her mouth open. Sister Wilda did not look away. A man Fen had known since childhood shifted his cup to make room and then seemed embarrassed by the kindness.

Fen sat.

Perrin poured wine.

“Tell Osric what you told me about the rope stores,” he said.

Fen had not expected the rope stores.

He began badly. The first joke ran too long. Osric waited with the polite expression used for children and drunk uncles.

Fen stopped.

“The hemp comes damp,” he said. “Ansel pays dry weight. Half the first coil is water.”

Osric looked down the table towards the steward.

“How long?”

“Three years. Maybe four.”

“Why has no one said?”

Fen looked at him.

Osric understood before Fen could make a joke of it.

“Show me tomorrow,” he said.

Later the better jokes came. A story about a novice, a goat and a funeral cloth. Someone asked for the ending twice. Another man repeated Fen’s opinion about the old wall and began it with *Fen says*.

He kept his left hand under the table, gripping the bench.

Across the hall, Sela saw him.

Her face opened. She grinned and lifted one hand: *look at you*.

Fen smiled back.

He had told Perrin where her fear lived. Not at the table and not that night, but the knowledge sat there with him. Sela knew none of it. She was pleased for him.

His fingers tightened until the wood hurt.

Perrin asked another question. Fen answered and made the end funny.

This time everyone laughed.

---

After the meal Ansel found him in the passage.

“You ate?”

“A scandalous amount. Blackcrest may never recover.”

Ansel held out a small clay cup.

“For Merra’s niche,” he said.

Fen took it.

“I was coming to ask.”

“No, you weren’t.”

Ansel walked away.

Fen stood with the cup in both hands.

It fitted the cracked place exactly.

---

He did not sleep.

He counted what he knew because counting was easier than thinking about the bench.

The join in the wall. Ansel’s missing bread. Osric asking twice for the Ledger. Wilda coming home with mud to her knees. Alaine touching the same griffe. Sela saying north and turning east.

He had no field, no entered name and no place that remained his once a guest stood up.

He had these.

In the morning the north row would need doing. He had left it two days and the wax would be a devil.

Fen turned onto his side.

It isn’t a secret.

He said it once more. The words were getting thin.

---


## Chapter Eleven
### The Corrected Entry

The trouble, Perrin said, was that the house’s version of Sela was useless.

He said it cheerfully, sitting on the end of Fen’s cot with his boots off, in the small cell Fen had never in his life had a visitor in, eating an apple he had got from somewhere.

“It’s not that it’s false,” he said. “It’s that it’s *finished*. They’ve had two months and they’ve already sanded her. She hears the dead. She refused the claim. She’s humble. She’s east.” He waved the apple. “That’s not a person, that’s a coat of arms. I could have written it in Crown-watch without leaving my chair, and in four hundred years someone will read it and feel absolutely nothing, and that will be my fault.”

“So what do you want?”

“I want the girl before.” Perrin leaned back against the wall. “Somebody who knew her when she was nine and had a cold and was a nuisance. One human detail — one — and the whole entry comes alive and stays alive for four centuries. Without it I’ve got a saint, and nobody has ever loved a saint. They’ve only ever obeyed one.”

Fen thought about it.

He thought about it while he laced his boots, and he was aware — he was entirely, pleasantly aware — that he was being *consulted*, and that a herald of Blackcrest was sitting on his cot waiting to hear what he thought, and that this was the third such evening, and that he had begun to be able to sleep at night by promising himself a fourth.

“There’s a woman,” he said.

Her name was Merta, he told him. She was fifty-two. She lived in the valley, in the third house past the mill.

She had known Sela since the girl was born, because the valley was small and everyone had known everyone since they were born. She was hard and clean and she did not lie, ever, about anything, and she had the reputation of a woman who would tell you the truth about your own child at a funeral.

And she had a son.

“He was Called,” said Fen. “Sixteen years ago. He was seventeen. Brother Tomas tested him and found him false, and they walked him up to the high line at the first snow, and he did not come back, and nobody kills them — the mountain does it — and every year on the night of the first snow she comes up the path with his coat and stands below the boundary and holds it, and Sister Wilda comes down and tells her she may not say his name on our ground, and she doesn’t, and then she goes home.”

Perrin had stopped eating the apple.

“Sixteen years,” he said.

“Sixteen.” Perrin had gone still. Fen kept talking. “She is not mad. That is what frightens them. She says what everyone agreed not to say.”

“And what does she want?”

“His name back.” Fen shrugged. “Not his name — he’s got his name, it’s in the red ink. She wants him *out* of the red. She wants somebody, anybody, to say the testing was wrong.” He picked up his box. “She’s not going to get it. The house can’t give it to her. If a testing can be wrong, then every soul on this mountain is standing on nothing, and they know it, and so does she.”

He turned in the doorway.

Perrin was looking at him with an expression Fen had never seen on him.

“*I* can give it to her,” said Perrin.

---

And that — Fen would think, afterwards, over and over, in the cold, going along the lower row with the rag — *that* was the moment. Not the wall. Not the Ledger. Not the Herald’s table, or the bench, or the wine.

He did not slow down. He thought he was helping.

He went down that mountain on a wet grey morning, walking beside a herald of House Blackcrest, to knock on the door of a woman he had never once spoken to.

He whistled on the road down and stopped when Merta’s roof came into view.

He had spent five years being a man who could not give anyone anything.

And he was about to give a mother her son.

---

The house past the mill was clean and cold and very poor.

The house was cold and clean. One cushioned chair stood by the fire. Merta did not use it.

She gave them tea because that was what you did.

Merta listened. Fen sat by the door with his cap in both hands and watched Perrin choose each word.

Because Perrin did not lie to her. Not once. He did not soften it and he did not dress it.

He told her exactly what he was, and exactly what the Chronicle was, and exactly what it would mean.

“I cannot undo your son’s testing,” he said. “No one can. The house will not reopen it and I would not ask them to; they’d refuse, and then it would be worse for you, because a refusal gets written down too.”

“Then what are you offering me?”

“An entry,” said Perrin.

He put his hands flat on his knees.

“In four years the Chronicle is read at the Jubilee, and after that it stops being a book and becomes what *happened*. Everything in it is true, in the only sense of true that survives four hundred years — which is that it is what people will say. And I write the entry for this valley.” He looked at her steadily. “I can write: *In the ninth year of Alaine, a young man of the Light Heights was found false and Given Back. His mother maintained to her death that the testing was in error, and kept the vigil sixteen winters, and was never once permitted to say his name upon the house’s ground.*”

The fire moved.

“And his name in it,” said Perrin. “Written out. In the black.”

---

Fen watched her hands.

He had learned that from Brother Tomas, of all people, secondhand, from a passage overheard: *people lie with their faces and tell the truth with their hands.* And Merta’s hands were on the table, quite still, and they did not move at all.

“And what do you want,” she said.

“Sela.”

“She’s a child.”

“She’s the biggest event in this valley in four hundred years, and I am going to write her down whether you help me or not, and the version I have got from the house is a stone saint and it will be believed forever.” Perrin leaned forward, and now — Fen saw it — now he was not performing; now he wanted something, badly, and he had stopped hiding that he wanted it. “Give me the girl. Give me one true thing about her from before. That she was greedy at feasts. That she was frightened of the dark. That she cried when a goat died, or didn’t, or laughed at the wrong moment at a burial. *One human thing*, and she survives as a person and not a relic, and I am the only man alive who can do that for her, and I will do it, and I will do it kindly.”

“And my son’s name.”

“And your son’s name,” said Perrin. “In the black. For four hundred years.”

---

Merta sat and looked at her hands.

Fen, by the door, found that he had stopped breathing.

“No,” said Merta.

---

Perrin did not move.

“May I ask why,” he said.

“You may.”

She got up.

She went to the window and she stood with her back to them and she was quiet for so long that Fen thought she had forgotten they were there.

“Because I’ve watched them do it,” she said.

“Do what?”

“*What you’re doing.*” She turned round. “You’ve come into my house and you’ve offered me the one thing I want in the world, and all you want back is a true story about a girl. That’s all. Just a true thing. Just one.” Her voice was perfectly level. “That’s what they did to Col.”

“I don’t—”

“They came and they were kind and they asked him true things about himself,” said Merta. “That’s what a testing *is*. Nobody hurt him. Nobody lied to him. They asked him true questions and he gave true answers, and they wrote one line, and they burned the paper, and then it was written and then it was done, and now he’s a boy in a book in red ink and he’s been that for sixteen years and he will be that when I’m dead.”

She came back to the table.

“You want a true thing about Sela,” she said. “I’ve got one. She was frightened of thunder till she was twelve and she used to come to my kitchen and sit under that table, that one, and my Col used to sit under it with her and tell her it was carts.”

Perrin’s hands went very still.

“That’s beautiful,” he said quietly. “That’s — Merta, that’s the entry. That’s four hundred years.”

“I know it is,” said Merta.

“Then—”

“And what do they do with it?” said Merta.

---

The fire cracked. Nobody had put wood on it.

“You’ll write it down kindly,” said Merta. “I believe you. You’ll write it down better than any of them could. And it’ll be true, and it’ll be lovely, and everyone who reads it will love her.”

“Yes.”

“And they’ll know she’s ordinary.”

Perrin said nothing.

“She’s a girl who hid under a table from the thunder,” said Merta. “That’s what I’d be giving you. And in a year, when they’ve stopped being frightened of her, someone in that house is going to want her tested again — properly, this time, now that the fuss has died — and they’ll bring out your lovely true story about the little girl under the table, and they’ll say: *look. She was always only that.*”

She sat down.

“And I’ll be standing on that stone in another sixteen years,” said Merta, “holding a coat for *her*.”

---

Fen heard, very distinctly, the sound of his own blood.

“That’s not—” he began.

Both of them looked at him.

He had not been going to speak. He had no standing to speak. He was not in the book; his word carried no witness; he was a young man by a door with a cap in his hands.

“—that’s not what he’d do,” said Fen.

“No,” Merta agreed. “It isn’t. He’d do exactly what he says. And then he’d go away.” She looked back at Perrin. “And the book would stay.”

---

Perrin was quiet for a long moment.

Then he stood, and he bowed to her — properly, the full bow, the one he had not given Mother Alaine — and he said:

“I have been at this for twenty years, and you are the first person who has ever explained to me what I do.”

“You knew what you do.”

“Yes,” said Perrin. “But nobody had ever said it to my face.” He picked up his hat. “The offer stands. It will stand until the Jubilee, and I will not raise it, and I will not lower it, and I will not come back and ask you again, because you would find that insulting and you would be right.”

“Get out of my house,” said Merta, without heat.

“Yes,” said Perrin. “Thank you for the tea.”

---

Fen was at the door when she said it.

“Boy.”

He turned.

She was standing in the middle of her cold clean kitchen with her hands at her sides, and she looked at him — really looked, the first time anyone had done it in five years, which was a thing he had wanted so badly and for so long that he had built an entire personality around pretending he had not.

“You brought him here,” said Merta.

“I thought—” His throat closed. He got it open. “I thought I was giving you something.”

“I know you did.” She nodded slowly. “That’s the worst part of you.”

Merta looked at him for a long time. Then she said:

“They didn’t leave you out of the book because they were cruel to you.

“They left you out because you’d have said yes.”

---

He did not answer her.

He walked out into the wet and up the road with the Herald and he did not say a word for a mile.

Her sentence followed him up the road. By the elbow it was louder than the rain.

---

At the elbow, where the boundary was, Perrin stopped and looked back down at the valley for a while, at the smoke going up from the poor cold houses.

“Well,” he said. “That’s that.”

“I’m sorry,” said Fen. “I thought she’d—”

“Do not be sorry. I mispriced her.” Perrin rubbed warmth into his hands. “I know what to offer people who still own something they can trade.”

“So what do we do?”

“Nothing so tidy,” said Perrin. “We’ll do it the other way.”

“What’s the other way?”

Perrin started up the path.

“Ask what happened and people close up,” Perrin said as he climbed. “Give them a wrong version and they correct you. Then listen.”

Fen followed him up the mountain.

He thought it was a clever thing to say.

At the next bend Perrin’s repaired heel came loose.

He stopped, leaned against the wall and looked down at it.

“The mountain has reviewed my workmanship.”

Fen waited for himself to answer. Nothing came.

Perrin sat on a wet stone and pulled off the boot. The rawhide had torn through the old leather. He took out a cord, then remembered he had used it to tie the mule’s pack.

Fen gave him the spare wick-cord from the candle-box.

“This is waxed,” Perrin said.

“You can repay the house in four hundred years.”

The joke arrived late and sounded tired. Perrin did not laugh. He wrapped the cord around the heel and tied it badly.

“Merta was right,” Fen said.

Perrin kept his head bent. “About what?”

Fen could not tell whether the question was mercy or craft.

“You know.”

“I know several things. Most are wrong by morning.”

Fen picked mud from the edge of the box with his thumbnail.

“She said I would say yes.”

Perrin pulled the knot tight. “Would you?”

Fen looked up sharply.

Perrin raised one hand. “No. That was a question. I said I would stop asking them when I shouldn’t. I have not learned quickly enough.”

They continued up the path. Perrin limped. Fen carried the candle-box under the other arm to keep it away from the rain.

Near the elbow they passed two pilgrims arguing over a place beneath a leaning tree. Perrin started towards them, then stopped.

“Not every quarrel needs a herald,” he said.

“That must hurt.”

This time Perrin laughed, but Fen had already turned away.

---

## Chapter Twelve
### Not That Way

The thaw came early and it came wrong.

It came in like a warm hand laid on the mountain, four weeks before it had any business arriving, and the snow went soft and grey and began to move, and the streams came up brown and loud, and the ground under the pasture turned to something that was not soil and was not water and would hold nothing.

The valley, which had spent two hundred years learning what a wet spring meant, went out and looked at it, and said very little.

The valley had other things on its mind. There was money in the road now.

You could see it from the upper gallery. Bryn Corr had taken the roof off her barn and put a new one on with the takings, and there were two families building on the flat below the mill, and the mill itself was running six days a week for the first time in Sela’s life, and a man had come up from the coast with a cart of pilgrim-goods — little bone tokens, cheap and neatly made, on a cord.

They were meant to be hers. The bone toggle at her throat. He was selling copies of her at four for a mark and he was selling out.

Sela watched the valley get rich on her and could not for the life of her decide what she felt about it, except that when she looked at the new roof on Bryn Corr’s barn she thought: *those people are not sleeping in a ditch this winter.*

She was learning, by then, that most things were like that.

---

Harl came up on a Tuesday.

He came into the yard leading a mule with a load of the house’s ironwork on it, because that was work and he was paid for it, and he did not go to find her, and he did not ask after her, because that would have been ridiculous.

Sela went and found him.

“You’ve gone thin,” said Harl, without looking up from the strap.

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Because you’ve gone thin. Do they not feed you?” He hauled the buckle. “Don’t answer. I’ll tell you what it is, it’s that you’re not *working*. A person who doesn’t work eats less and sleeps worse and thinks too much. It’s the great secret of the world and nobody believes it.”

“Harl.”

“Mm.”

“Tam.”

Harl stopped.

He straightened up, and he turned round, and his enormous cracked face did something Sela had never in her life seen it do.

“He said yes,” said Harl.

“He never.”

“He *said yes*.” Harl was trying not to look pleased and failing so completely that a passing novice slowed down to watch. “I put it to him plain, over the fence, no fuss — I said there’d be a horse in the summer and he could take it or leave it and I’d not mention it again. And he looked at the ground for about a year and a half.” He shook his head slowly. “And he said *all right.*”

“That’s not yes.”

“From that boy,” said Harl, “that is a *hymn*.”

And he laughed, loud, in the yard, and went back to his strap, and Sela stood there in the wet with her hands in her sleeves and thought, with the sudden violence of a thing you did not know was in you: *I want to be there. I want to see it. I want to see Tam get that horse and be ungrateful about it, and I want Harl to not say a word, and I want to be standing at the fence.*

---

“You’ll be doing the route, then,” she said. “For the money.”

“I will take the route.” Harl checked each hoof. “Forty a week by midsummer. Corr needs a guide, and her cousin gets lost between his bed and the privy.”

“Which road?”

“The high one. East shoulder, past the slide-slopes, down onto the pass.” He dropped the hoof. “One day up instead of two. That’s the whole business. That’s the roof and the horse and the boy’s future in one summer, and I’ve walked it since I was nine years old.”

The yard was full of people.

It was a Tuesday afternoon in a working yard, with novices going by with wood, and the kitchen girls at the water, and Steward Ansel at the door doing his sums, and Novice Ivet — who had begun, in the last few weeks, to be wherever Sela was, out of love, without ever being asked, and Sela had not once found a way to say anything about it — standing four feet away with a basket on her hip.

And Sela said:

“Not that way.”

---

She did not mean anything by it.

She would say that a great many times afterwards, to a great many people, in a voice that got flatter and harder every time, and she would never once be believed, because by then it had stopped being a thing anyone could believe or disbelieve.

She meant: *the ground is wet.*

She meant: *I have watched that slope for nine winters and it goes in a wet spring, it went in my father’s time and it went in his father’s, and the thaw came four weeks early and the snow on the top of that shoulder is sitting on grease.*

She meant only what a herder means when she warns someone from bad footing.

“Not that way,” she said.

Harl had been checking the mule’s breast strap. His hands stopped on the buckle.

The leather had cracked beside the tongue. Sela saw it at once.

“That will go before the shoulder,” she said.

“I know.”

He took a spare strip from his pocket, threaded it through and tested the pull against his knee. The mule tried to bite his sleeve.

“And this beast will go before the strap,” Harl said. “Probably into a ditch.”

A novice laughed. Harl did not know her and looked annoyed that she had been listening.

Sela crouched and pressed her fingers into the yard mud. Water rose around the knuckles. Under it, the ground gave with no firm layer beneath.

“It is worse above,” she said.

Harl kept working the buckle. “Everything is worse above. That is why the view costs money.”

Ivet had come close. She watched Sela’s muddy hand as if it were part of an office.

“She touched the ground before she spoke,” Ivet said to the novice beside her.

Sela wiped her hand on her skirt.

“I touched mud.”

“Yes,” said Ivet.

The answer was full of agreement and no understanding.

Harl pulled the repaired strap once more. “There. It holds.”

“For how long?”

“Long enough.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Neither do you.”

The mule shifted between them. Harl put one hand on its neck, not gently but with familiarity. Sela could feel the yard listening now: the kitchen girls at the water, Ansel at the store door, Ivet with the basket, three novices pretending to sort wood.

The private warning had already gone. What remained belonged to the yard. “Not this year. The slopes’ll go. It’s come in too warm and there’s nothing under the snow up there, it’s all — ” she made a helpless motion with one hand, the motion of a goatherd describing ground to another goatherd “ — it’s *sliding*, Harl, it’s been sliding for a week, you can hear it at night.”

The yard had gone quiet.

The yard quieted in pieces. The girls at the water stopped last.

---

Harl laughed.

He laughed, and it was the last piece of pure kindness anyone did for her in that house, and neither of them knew it.

“Sela,” he said, “I’ve been going up that shoulder since before your mother met your father. It slides every spring. It slid last spring, and the spring before, and I went up it both times and here I am, being told my business in a yard by a girl who couldn’t hang a gate.”

“Harl—”

“You worry too much. You always did. When you were nine you cried for a week because a goat looked at you sideways.” He heaved the pack up. “I’ll go round the top of it if it’s bad. I’ve eyes in my head.”

And he clicked his tongue at the mule, and went out of the yard, and lifted one hand without turning round, which was how he said goodbye to people he liked.

---

By the evening office, it was law.

Sela did not know how. She has never known how, and she went over it a hundred times, and the honest answer is that it took about ninety minutes and involved no malice at any point.

Ivet told the kitchen as news and left out the goats.

And a kitchen girl told the woman who brought the milk. And the woman who brought the milk told her sister on the road, and her sister was one of the four hundred, and the four hundred had been sitting in a wet camp for three weeks with nothing whatever to talk about.

And by nightfall there were people on the road below saying that the Called had *forbidden the east road*, and that the ground there was cursed, and that it had opened once already, and had anyone not heard about the grave —

---

She tried to take it back.

That is the part that nobody has ever put in any account of it, and it is the only part that matters.

She went to Brother Osric, because Osric was the one who wrote things and would know how to unwrite them, and she stood in front of him and said: “I need to say something to the house. Tonight.”

“Of course.”

“I need to tell them I didn’t mean it as a — as an *instruction*. I meant the ground is wet. That’s all I meant. It’s just wet ground and a warm thaw and I’ve seen it before and Harl should go round the top of it, that’s *all*, that is the entire thing I meant.”

Brother Osric looked at her for a long moment.

And then he said, gently, and he was not being cruel, and Sela would think about it for years:

“Sela. If I take you into that hall tonight and you tell a hundred people that you did not mean it — what do you think they will hear?”

“The *truth*.”

“They will hear the Called of House Whitehart standing up in front of the house and saying, *I am nobody, my words are nothing, pay me no mind.*” He set his pen down. “And they will weep. And they will love you more than they did this morning. And tomorrow there will be twice as many on the road.”

Sela stood in the middle of the room with her hands at her sides.

“Then what do I do,” she said.

“You could lie,” said Osric.

She looked up.

He was not smiling. He was watching her with the frank, quick, unsentimental attention of a man who has just discovered something and is turning it over.

“You could stand up and say the voice spoke and told you the east shoulder was safe,” he said. “They’d believe that. They’d believe it instantly. It’s the only sentence in the world that would get your friend up that road with the house’s blessing, and it would cost you nothing but a lie, and no one alive could ever prove it was one.”

The fire spat.

“Is that what you want me to do?” said Sela.

“No,” said Osric. “I want to know if you *can*.”

---

She did not sleep.

She lay in the dark in her cell and listened to the mountain, which was making the sound it made now, all night, every night — a long slow settling somewhere above, water and rock and snow finding new places to be, the sound of a slope taking its time.

She would go down before breakfast. Harl would be at Corr’s yard or in the lower field. She would tell him to take the neck, and she would tell him where no one else could hear.

But thirty people had heard already. She could still see them in the yard, all pretending not to look while Harl laughed. If he changed his road now, they would know why.

Her left boot had dried crooked beside the bed. She pushed her foot into it.

---

Before dawn she went out to the gallery.

The light was grey and there was rain coming and the fires on the road below were smoking in the wet, hundreds of them now, more than she could have counted if she had tried, which she no longer did.

Down in the yard, two figures were standing together by the gate.

She knew both figures by their stance. The Herald, in his good coat, with his hands behind his back. And Fen beside him, in his shirtsleeves in the cold, with no candle-box, talking.

Fen talked with both hands. Perrin listened without moving. Sela leaned on the rail and lifted one hand.

Fen glanced up.

She was sure of it. She would go over that morning many times in the years that followed, and she was always sure of it, and she was always aware that being sure of a thing is not the same as it being true: he looked up, and he saw her at the rail with her hand raised, and his face did something.

And then he turned back to the Herald and went on talking.

---

## Chapter Thirteen
### Wet Ground

By Thursday the valley had two accounts of Sela.

In one, she had closed the east road with a warning from the dead. In the other, she was a frightened girl who had said something about mud and been misunderstood.

Both reached her. A woman at the well thanked her for saving lives. That afternoon the same woman’s brother told her it was a shame what the house had done to a child.

Perrin heard both versions too. For six days he wrote down neither. He sat in the yard, asked small questions and watched which story people corrected.

Bryn Corr came up angry.

“I have forty a week booked for summer.”

Sela stood with a basket of split wood against her hip.

“I did not close the road.”

“I know.”

“Then tell them.”

“I have. They think I’m protecting my money.” Bryn kicked a stick out of her way. “I’ve taken deposits in three towns. Spent some on the roof. The low road takes two days. Two days means fewer beds, less bread, fewer guides. Harl loses his summer. Half the valley has done its sums on people coming over that shoulder.”

“I told him the ground was bad.”

“Yes.” Bryn rubbed at the side of her nose. There was flour on her sleeve. “And now everyone has to decide whether weather came out of your mouth.”

She turned away, then stopped.

“You’re like frost now. No one blames frost. It still kills the lambs.”

“What am I meant to do?”

Bryn looked back at her. “I don’t know. I sell beds.”

She left. A moment later she came back for the bread list she had dropped by the pump, swore at the mud and left again.

Sela carried the wood to the shed. Two pieces slid out. She went back for them.

That night the mountain shifted above the house. Water moved under snow and stone. She could hear the long scrape of it when the wind fell.

She thought about Bryn’s roof. The two new houses below the mill. The man at the ford selling little bone copies of the toggle at her throat.

She had made the valley poorer by opening her mouth about wet ground.

Her left boot had split again at the toe. She pushed a rag into it and went to bed.

---

On Monday Ivet found her near the lower passage.

“Harl has gone up.”

Sela dropped the cloth she was carrying.

“Nine people. From the camp. They paid him.” Ivet caught at her sleeve. “He left before dawn so nobody would stop him. Sister, he is defying you.”

Sela pulled free and ran.

She took the east path without a cloak. Rain soaked her before the first rise. The ground took her boots at the heel and let go with a sucking sound. She passed two pilgrims resting beside the wall. One called her name. She did not turn.

Harl’s party was on the long traverse below the shoulder.

Nine travellers straggled behind him. Their boots were wrong for the slope. One man had tied cloth around both ankles. Another sang until he saw Sela coming.

Harl stopped.

A pilgrim dropped to his knees in the mud.

“Get up,” Harl said. “You’ll want those later.”

Sela reached them breathless.

“Come down.”

“No.”

“The neck is bad too.”

“You haven’t seen the neck.”

“I know what the water is doing.”

Harl shifted the rope across his shoulder. “They paid Corr four marks each. She paid me twelve. Six are spent. The rest is the horse.”

“Give it back.”

“With what?”

Sela looked at the nine people. No one met her eyes except the kneeling man, who still had not risen.

“Harl.”

“There were thirty people in that yard.” He kept his voice low. “You told me my road was bad in front of all of them.”

“I told you because it was bad.”

“They don’t ask me now. They ask you.”

“That isn’t my fault.”

“I know.” He wiped rain from his beard. “Doesn’t improve it.”

“The shelf is moving.”

“I hear it.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Because I’ve walked it since I was nine. Because I took their money. Because Tam has been told about the horse.”

“You told him.”

“Yes. Stupid of me.”

Someone behind him slipped and swore. Harl glanced back until the man found his footing.

Then he looked at Sela.

“Did you hear something?”

Rain ran into her mouth.

She had the answer ready. Six words. They weighed nothing.

Harl would turn around. He would curse her all the way down. By the end of the week he would be telling anyone who listened that he had never believed her in the first place. He would lose the summer and live.

“No.”

His face changed a little.

“I didn’t hear words,” she said. “I don’t know if I hear anything at all. The ground is wet. The thaw came early. I have watched that slope since I was ten and I think it will go.”

“You think.”

“Yes.”

Harl looked up towards the neck.

“I’ll take them over rock.”

“The rock sits on the same mountain.”

“That is a useful thing to say after running four miles.”

“Please.”

He put one hand on her shoulder. His thumb caught in the wet cloth and he had to free it.

“If you had said the dead told you, I would go down.”

“I know.”

“But you didn’t.”

“No.”

He squeezed once, then let go.

“You did right,” he said. “For whatever that buys.”

He turned to the travellers.

“Up. We go round the neck. Anyone who complains can carry the rope.”

The kneeling man scrambled to his feet.

Sela watched them climb to the right, away from the shoulder and towards the rock route.

Harl did what he promised.

She saw him take the neck.

---

For two days she waited.

At first she stood in the yard. Then on the gallery. By the second night she had gone down to the gate and would not come in.

The rain stopped. The mountain made no sound at all.

Her boot was full of water. She took her weight off it, then put it back. There was nothing else to do.

On the third morning a boy ran up from the valley.

People gathered at the elbow and remained there too long.

They began climbing towards the house in a close group. Behind them, a long way back, Tam walked alone in the middle of the road.

He was seventeen years old.

He had no horse.

---

## Chapter Fourteen
### Four Marks a Head

They brought the four bodies up on hurdles, and it took most of the day. No rule named Fen for the work. The bearers carried the dead into the cold room, set down a bucket, cloth and lamp, and left him there.

Harl was the second.

Fen shut the door after the bearers.

The bucket held cold water. He opened the door again and called into the passage. No one answered. He carried the bucket to the kitchen, emptied it and waited while a girl filled it from the kettle.

“For the cold room?” she asked.

Fen nodded.

She added another dipper without being told. The water would still be lukewarm by the time he returned.

Ansel stood at the flour chest with his tally-stick. He saw the cloth over Fen’s shoulder.

“Four?”

“Four.”

Ansel made a mark. His hand did not pause at Harl’s name because Fen had not given it.

“More soap,” Fen said.

Ansel opened the locked cupboard and handed him a piece from the winter store. It was the good soap, pale and hard.

“This is for the seniors.”

“The seniors are not dirty enough.”

Ansel closed the cupboard. “Bring back what remains.”

Fen almost said that soap did not return from the dead. He had used the line before, on a different day. It would have been funny enough.

He put the soap in his pocket.

Back in the cold room, steam had stopped rising from the bucket. Fen set the lamp closer and began with the first body. Mud had dried inside one ear. He worked it loose with the corner of the cloth.

Harl waited under the second sheet.

Fen finished the first man before he lifted it.

Harl had not been crushed. Fen had prepared three jokes on the way to the cold room. None survived the door.

The slope had not fallen *on* him. It had gone out from under the ground he was standing on — from the neck, from the safe way, from the rock, half a mile from where anyone said it would go — and taken the whole shelf with it, and Harl had gone down with a hundred feet of mountain and stopped when the mountain stopped.

He looked, from the front, like a man who had gone to sleep in his clothes.

Fen stood over him for a while with the cloth in his hand.

*Four marks a head*, he thought.

He did not know why. It was the only thing in his head. It stayed there for two hours.

---

The survivors disagreed at once, and they disagreed in good faith, and by evening the house had three accounts and could not choose between them.

Two of them swore he had taken the party straight across the shoulder — the forbidden ground, the ground she had spoken of — and that the slope had gone under them exactly where the Called had said it would.

Three of them swore the opposite: that he had walked them the long way round, three extra hours, over the neck, over rock, grumbling the whole time, and that the ground had gone in the one place on that mountain nobody had ever thought to fear.

The sixth left for the coast before dark. No later account gives his version.

Sela said Harl had gone over the neck. She drew the turn in spilled flour for Osric and showed him where the party vanished from sight.

People listened. Then they repeated the other road.

---

Fen washed Harl slowly. The work had an order: eyes, mouth, hair, hands. It did not change because the man on the table had known him.

He wrung out the cloth. The water had gone pink and cold.

*Four marks a head,* he thought, and began on the hands.

---

They gathered in the hall that night to say what it had meant, and they were quick about it, and every one of them was sincere.

**Novice Ivet** had it first, because Ivet always had it first, and she had been crying for a day and a half and her face was raw and shining.

Ivet took Sela’s hand in the passage and spoke too quickly for Sela to remember later. She looked up at her, waiting.

Sela let her do it.

Sister Wilda had stood in the yard that morning and said, loudly, that the girl had not been confirmed and that a house which lets an unconfirmed word become a prohibition has stopped doing its work. She had been right. Half the seniors had looked at their hands.

Sela thought about Wilda’s face.

She thought about it while Ivet held her hand and looked up at her, and what came up in her — for the space of one breath, warm, and she did not push it away — was that she wanted Sister Wilda to be standing in that passage.

Then she took her hand back and went in.

**Sister Wilda** had it second.

Half the room hated Wilda. She was right. Her folded hands shook against one another.

**Brother Osric** had it third, and he had it correctly, and he said nothing at all.

He sat at the Testers’ end with his fingers laced, and he looked at Sela, and Fen — who had spent his life watching people who never noticed they were being watched — could see the arithmetic going through him like water through a mill.

---

Tam came up on the third day for the burial, and he did not cry.

Tam stood in Harl’s coat. The sleeves covered his knuckles. He kept both fists closed and said nothing.

By evening people were saying the boy had borne it well.

Fen watched him from the back, where he always stood.

And he watched Novice Ivet detach herself from the front of the crowd, quietly, without any fuss, and go and stand beside the boy.

She did not say anything at first. She just stood there.

And then, after a long while, Fen saw her lean in and say something short — five words, six — into Tam’s ear.

And Tam turned his head and looked at her.

And something in that boy’s face *unlocked*.

Fen’s stomach turned. Ivet kept speaking to Tam, too quietly for him to hear. When the boy looked up, Fen looked away.

---

Bryn Corr came for the ground on the fourth day, and she came with the paper, and she was correct.

Harl had borrowed on the top field when the roof failed. Corr held the paper. Everyone in the valley knew.

She took the field. She took the four beasts. She took the cart.

Corr transferred the debt in front of Tam. Fen watched her read the figures twice. She swallowed before signing and complained about the ink.

And the horse was at the ford.

Harl had put four marks down on it in the spring, and the man at the ford had kept it, and it was a good horse and he had been offered better than four marks for it twice. There was nothing on paper. There was a handshake and a deposit and a dead man.

The man at the ford sold the horse.

He sent the four marks back up the valley, honestly, in a purse, to Bryn Corr, because Harl had owed her — which was correct, and lawful, and what any man in the Light Heights would have done.

Tam was standing at the fence when it went by.

He did not say anything. He watched the horse go down the road behind another man’s cart, and he watched it for as long as it was in sight, and Novice Ivet was standing at his shoulder and did not say anything either, and did not need to.

---

Perrin wrote the entry on the fourth day.

He did it in the guest cell with the door open, because he did everything with the door open, and Fen — passing with the box, because he was always passing with the box — stopped without meaning to.

“Come in,” said Perrin, without looking up. “Tell me if it’s wrong.”

And he read it out.

It was short. It was beautiful. It said that in the year three hundred and ninety-six after the Unification, in the valley of the Light Heights, a drover named Harl of the low pasture had taken nine pilgrims up the east shoulder against the counsel of the Called, and that the mountain had come down, and that he had died at his work.

The entry stopped there. It did not say she had a voice, or that she was proved, or that any miracle had occurred.

“That’s true,” said Fen slowly. “Every word of that is true.”

“Yes.”

“He went round the neck. She saw him. He *never went on the shoulder*—”

“He went up the east shoulder,” said Perrin. “That’s the name of the whole mountain, Fen. It’s what everyone calls the road.” He set the pen down and rubbed his eyes. “There isn’t a word in it that isn’t true. There isn’t a word in it that could be argued with in a court. I could read it out to Sister Wilda and she would not be able to lay a finger on it.”

“But it isn’t—”

“No,” Perrin agreed. “It isn’t.”

He looked up.

And Fen, standing in the doorway with the candle-box under his arm, saw — for the first and only time in his life — Herald Perrin of House Blackcrest look tired.

“Four hundred years from now,” said Perrin, “there will be no one alive who knows about the neck. There will be a line in a book. And the line will say that a man went up a mountain against her word and the mountain took him, and every child in the Banner-lands will know that story, and I will not have written one false thing.” He picked the pen back up. “It’s the kindest version, Fen. Believe me. I’ve had four days to think of a kinder one. Every other version I can write gets her killed, or gets Sister Wilda the house, or gets four hundred people on that road burning the mill.”

“Then don’t write anything.”

Perrin looked at him with something almost like affection.

“That’s not one of the choices,” he said. “Somebody is going to write it. It’s going to be me or it’s going to be the songs, and the songs are going to say she cursed him.”

---

Fen went back to the niches.

He worked until his hands stopped, which took a long time, and then he sat down on the flags with the wall of the dead in front of him and put his head against the cold stone.

He could name every link. None would hold the whole weight by itself.

---

“Fen.”

He was on his feet before he knew it, and he had the rag behind his back like a boy caught with something, and he had not heard the man come in at all.

Brother Tomas stood in the mouth of the listening room with a lamp.

He looked old. Fen had never once thought of him as old.

“Sit down,” said Tomas.

“I’ve the north row to—”

“*Sit down.*”

Fen sat down.

Tomas set the lamp low between them. Fen knew the arrangement. He had described it to Perrin three weeks earlier.

“The Herald knows how I take a testing,” said Tomas.

Fen said nothing.

“He knows the lamp, the draught, the line I write and the burning.” Tomas kept both hands flat. “He repeated them to me.”

The wind came in through the arch.

“There are four people alive,” said Tomas, “who could have told him that. Mother Alaine. Brother Osric. Myself.” He looked up. “And the young man who cleans this room, and has stood in that passage during more testings than any other soul in this house, and whom I have never once — not *once*, in five years — thought to wonder about.”

Fen looked at his own hands.

They were shaking. He watched with the distant attention he usually gave weather.

“I’m not going to ask you,” said Tomas.

---

“What?”

“I said I’m not going to ask you.” Tomas leaned back against the stone. He suddenly seemed unbearably tired. “I’ve spent sixteen years asking people questions in this room and getting true answers out of them and then deciding what to do with the truth, and I have never once, in all that time, considered that I might be the last person in the Banner-lands anyone should tell the truth to.” He rubbed his face. “So I’m not going to ask you. I’m going to offer you something instead, and you may take it or not, and I will not raise it again.”

He put his hands flat on his knees.

“I will test you again,” said Brother Tomas.

The room went absolutely still.

“You were nineteen,” said Tomas. “You were frightened and I was tired and it was a bad winter and I could not conclude. So I wrote nothing, and I burned my working, and a year and a day later the page sealed itself, and I let it. That is what Roche taught, and Roche was a good man, and I have said his name in the winter rite for thirty years with my whole heart.” His voice did not change at all. “And I have had five years to watch what my mercy did to you, and I would like to close the page.”

Fen could not speak.

“I will do it properly,” said Tomas. “I will do it in front of Osric and I will do it in front of the Mother, and whatever comes out of it will be entered. In the black or in the red. And I will not lie for you, and I will not lean towards you, and I want you to understand exactly what that means.”

He picked up the lamp.

“It means you might be Given Back,” said Tomas. “At the first snow. Up the high line. With bread for one day.”

He stood.

“And it means you might not,” he said. “And either way, Fen, either way — there would be a page in that book with your name written at the top of it, and it would be *finished*, and nobody in this house would ever again be able to talk about you as though you were the weather.”

He went to the door.

“Think about it,” said Brother Tomas. “Take as long as you need. I’m not going anywhere, and neither, I am sorry to say, are you.”

And he went out with the lamp, and left the young man sitting on the flags in the dark, in front of the wall of the dead, every name of which he knew.

---

## Chapter Fifteen
### The Page He Did Not Close

He said yes about forty times.

None of the yeses reached Tomas.

On the first morning Fen carried the candle-box to the Tester’s passage and found a novice scrubbing the floor outside the door. She asked him to lift his feet. He did. By the time the stones were dry, Tomas had gone to the morning office.

On the second day Fen stopped to repair the strap on the box. It did not need repair. He cut away the old stitching, regretted it and spent an hour replacing what he had destroyed.

Ansel came past while Fen held the leather between his teeth.

“That strap was sound.”

Fen released it. “I improve sound things. It is my gift.”

Ansel looked at the loose stitches. “No.”

He went on.

On the third day Fen reached Tomas’s door. He raised his hand. Someone inside dropped a pen and swore softly. Fen knew Tomas’s swearing: brief, precise, usually directed at objects.

Fen smiled despite himself.

Then Tomas asked whoever was with him to begin again from the first answer.

Fen lowered his hand.

The north wall had three cups with long wicks. He trimmed all three, then trimmed the rest to match. When he finished, the room was darker than before.

He stood with the shears in his hand and said, “Yes.”

The dead kept their opinions.

He said it lying in the dark in his cell with his hands behind his head. He said it going along the lower row with the rag, out loud, once, to a niche. He said it in the passage with the box under his arm and his heart going like a hare’s, walking towards Brother Tomas’s door — and got to within four feet of it, twice, on two separate days, and both times turned round and went to see to the north wall, which did not need seeing to.

He wanted it so much that it frightened him.

He did not care what verdict the house reached. That surprised him.

He wanted *the page*.

He wanted a sheet of ruled paper in a cold room with his name written at the top of it, in ink, in a hand, by a man whose job it was, and he wanted the line under it *finished* — any line, any ink, black or red, true or false, holy or damned — he did not care, he had *never* cared, he would have taken the red with both hands and thanked them for it.

He wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, to be *concluded*.

He knew what red meant.

He had washed four bodies and watched Merta carry the coat to the boundary. He knew what the mountain did. He also knew what it meant when someone used his name at a table.

His mother was seventy-one and four hours away. At market she called him *the boy who helps up at the house*.

Fen told himself she would not survive the walk.

Nobody would come.

For eight days he called that the whole argument. It did not hold.

---

Because here is the other thing, and he did not find it until the seventh night, and when he found it he lay very still in the dark for a long time.

He sat up.

He sat on the edge of the cot in his cold cell with his feet on the flags, and he turned it over, and it did not go away, and every time he came back to it, it was still true.

Perrin wanted what Fen had heard while holding the candle-box.

Five years of rooms. Five years of people speaking across him. Most of it had entered his head because no one had thought to lower a voice.

What Fen owned could be carried in his head. Most of it had entered there because no one noticed him standing nearby.

Close the page, and he was — what? A man of the house. A candle-tender with a name and a portion and a seat and a right to bear witness. Somebody who would be *seen* coming down a passage. Somebody who would have to be told things on purpose, by people who had decided to tell him.

Nobody tells a man with a name anything.

---

In the dark he tried three jokes. The third was the worst. He stopped.

He lay back down.

*It isn’t a secret,* he thought, out of long habit, and heard how thin it had got, and how far away, and how badly it had worn.

---

He went to Brother Tomas on the eighth morning, and he lied to him.

The Tester was in the Ledger room with the shutter open and the cold coming in, and he did not look surprised to see him, and he did not offer him the stool.

“I’ll not do it,” said Fen.

“All right.”

“I’m — ” He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it for two days and it had been better than this. “I’m grateful. I want you to know that. I know what it cost you to offer it.”

“It cost me nothing,” said Tomas. “It would have cost you everything. That’s rather the point of it.”

The wind moved the shutter.

“And the other thing,” said Tomas.

Here it was.

“I told him about the wall,” said Fen.

He heard his own voice do it. It came out level and easy and just faintly rueful, with the smallest apologetic shrug in it, the voice of a young man admitting to a small foolishness — and it was, he realised with a distant chill, a *good* lie, a really very good one, and he had not known until that moment that he could do this.

“He asked me about the niches and the count and I told him. I told him about the forty-year gap. It was three evenings and he was the first person in five years who’d — ” and this part was true, and he *used* it, he reached down and took the truest thing in him and put it in the mouth of the lie because it would make the lie stand up “ — who’d asked me anything at all.”

He stopped.

“And that’s all,” said Fen.

---

Brother Tomas looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, and he did not look at his face.

He looked at his hands.

Fen had known he would. He had known it in his bones — he had described this exact thing to a herald of House Blackcrest in the listening room three weeks ago, in exchange for the sound of his own name — and he had spent two days preparing his face, and he had never once thought about his hands, and now they were hanging at the ends of his arms like two pieces of evidence.

He left the papers where they were. His hands stayed at his sides.

The room was very quiet.

And then Brother Tomas — the Tester of House Whitehart, who had read frightened people for thirty years and had never once been wrong that anybody could prove, who had a page in the book in front of him that stopped after four words, who had said a dead boy’s name into the snow once in sixteen years —

— looked away.

“Very well,” he said.

---

Fen stood in the doorway with his mouth open.

“That’s — ” He could not get it out. “That’s *it*?”

“What would you have me do?” Tomas turned a page of the Ledger without reading it. “Shall I take you into the listening room and set the lamp low and ask you the same question in four different shapes until your hands give you away? Shall I do it *properly*?” He looked up, and his face was perfectly calm, and there was something behind it that Fen would not understand for another year. “I could. It would take me about an hour. And I would know, and you would know I knew, and then I would have to do something about it, and what I would have to do is take it to the Mother, and what the Mother would do—”

He stopped.

“I am not going to test you against your will,” said Tomas, “in order to convict you. That is not what the rite is for. That is what it *becomes*, in a house that has forgotten what it’s for, and I have spent sixteen years making very sure that this house did not forget.”

He closed the Ledger.

“Go and do the north wall,” he said. “It wants doing. And Fen—”

Fen stopped.

“The offer stands,” said Brother Tomas. “Until the first snow. I said I would not raise it again and I am not raising it. I am telling you where it is.”

---

Fen got out of that room and walked the length of the north passage and put his back against the cold stone and stood there for a while with his eyes shut.

He had seen it happen. He had watched the best reader of human beings in the Banner-lands look at his hands and find the answer and put it down.

Relief came first. Shame followed late enough for him to recognise the relief.

It was contempt.

He stood in the passage and despised the only man in Emberwold who had ever tried to save him, and he did it fluently, and at length, and with a good deal of wit.

And then he went and found the Herald.

---

“You look terrible,” said Perrin.

“I’ve been washing corpses and cleaning a wall. I look *professional*.” Fen sat down on the guest-cell floor without being asked, which he had never done, and which he did not notice himself doing. “You’re going soon.”

“In a month or so.”

“And then what happens to me?”

Perrin, who was packing something, stopped packing it.

He turned round and sat down on the cot, and he looked at Fen for a moment, and then he said, quite gently:

“You are the most useful person I have met in nine years of doing this, and I am not going to insult you by pretending I don’t know what that means, or what it has cost you, or what it is going to cost you when I ride down that road.”

Fen looked at the floor.

“I could take you,” said Perrin.

“You can’t. I can’t leave. An Unwritten can’t be given a portion or a place or a licence to travel and no house can employ me, because there’s nobody to employ. That’s the whole—”

“I know what Unwritten means.” Perrin’s voice sharpened, though not at Fen. “I have spent six weeks learning how often this house uses a person without using his name.”

He rubbed his eyes.

“I can’t take you,” he said. “Not yet. Not as anything. If I put you on a horse and rode out of that gate tomorrow they would have a boy on the road behind us within a day and there is not a magistrate in the Banner-lands who would hear a word from either of us, because you cannot be robbed of what you do not legally have.” He leaned forward. “But there is a Jubilee in four years. And I am the man who writes it. And there is a very great deal that can be done in four years by somebody who is *in the book.*”

The candle went sideways in the draught.

“You’d put me in it,” said Fen.

“I’d put you in it.”

---

And Fen — sitting on a stone floor with his back against a cot in a cold cell, twenty-four years old, with wax under his fingernails and a dead man’s face still in his hands —

— gave him Mother Alaine.

Fen did not call it theft while he was doing it.

Fen kept talking. He heard himself stretch one story past its ending and went straight into the next.

Perrin listened. That was enough to keep him going.

“You want to know what nobody knows about this house,” he said. “Here’s what nobody knows about this house. The Mother touches the griffes.”

Perrin did not move.

“Everyone touches the griffes,” he said.

“They bow. They say the office. No one touches the stone. It is not forbidden. People simply do not.” Fen rubbed wax from his thumb.

He grinned.

“Everyone thinks it’s devotion,” he said. “It looks like devotion. The novices copy it and get it wrong.”

And Perrin said, very quietly:

“And what do you think it is?”

Fen shrugged.

“I don’t know,” he said. “It’s just odd. I noticed it. That’s all I ever do — I notice things and there’s nobody to tell.”

---

Perrin got up and went and stood at the window with his back to the room for a long moment.

“There’s somebody to tell,” he said.

And he took out his book, and he opened it, and he wrote it down.

---

## Chapter Sixteen
### The Only Voice Permitted

Osric took the matter to Alaine before Tomas.

That morning Wilda had supervised two novices cutting burial cloth.

The younger girl measured each length twice and still cut the first one short. She stared at the missing handspan as if the cloth might grow under accusation.

“It is spoiled,” she whispered.

“No,” said Wilda. “It is short. Those are different conditions.”

The other novice had been crying without sound since Harl was carried in. Her scissors shook against the linen.

“May I leave?”

“You may finish the edge and then leave.”

“Sister—”

“If you stop now, someone else begins at the torn place. Finish the edge.”

Wilda threaded a needle for her because the girl could not see through the tears. She did not mention the tears. When the seam was done, Wilda sent both novices to the kitchen and recut the short cloth herself.

Osric found her folding the offcut.

“Mother Alaine has agreed to hear the charge,” he said.

“It is not a charge until it is entered.”

“The matter, then.”

“It is not a matter to the dead.”

Osric waited.

Wilda put the offcut in the repair basket. It would become bandages. Nothing in the Bell was discarded merely because its first purpose had failed.

“Tell me the words he used,” she said.

Osric did.

Wilda corrected him twice before they went to Alaine. By supper the order of those two names had become part of the charge.

He spoke quietly and without triumph. Tomas listened with his hands folded.

He laid out what the Herald knew.

Perrin knew about the low lamp, the draught and the paper burned before it cooled. He knew where the old count on the griffe-wall changed. He also knew Osric had twice asked for the Ledger and been refused.

Osric had told no one that last part.

Perrin had learned it while sitting on the listening-room flags with Fen.

“I make no accusation,” said Osric. “There is nothing to accuse. He is not in the book. He cannot be charged, he cannot be tried, he cannot swear, and he cannot be believed. There is no procedure.”

He let that hang for exactly the right length of time.

“But I would ask,” he said, “how it came about that the only person in this house with unlimited access to every room and no legal existence whatsoever was left, for five years, entirely unconsidered. And I would ask it of the Tester who left the page open.”

Sela was not in that room. Nobody was in that room but the two of them.

Ivet brought Sela the news, breathless. Two novices and a crying kitchen girl arrived before Ivet finished. By then everyone spoke as if judgment had already been given.

There was nothing to decide, and she could not make anyone see it.

You cannot punish a man who is not in the book. You cannot fine him — he has no portion. You cannot exile him — he has no place. You cannot flog him, because he has done nothing that is written down as a crime, because nothing he could do is written down at all.

So the house did the only thing available to it.

The house **protected itself.**

---

Alaine gave the order after evening office. It took less than a minute.

Sela said so whenever she was asked. The surviving accounts preferred another answer.

Alaine did not raise her voice. She did not name a crime. She stood in front of a hundred people with her hands folded and she said that the young man who kept the niches would continue in his work, that he would be fed and housed as he had always been, that no hand would be laid on him and no word said against him.

And that he would not be spoken to.

“Not in the hall,” said Alaine. “Not in the passages. Not at the wall. Not by name, and not at all.”

She let the silence sit.

“This is not a punishment,” she said. “He is not in the book, and what is not in the book cannot be punished. It is a *precaution*. Our house has learned this month that its private business travels, and that it travels through the one soul among us that nobody thinks about.” She looked down the hall, and her face was gentle. “That is our fault and not his. We made a room with no door in it and we put a man inside and forgot him, and now we are surprised that the walls have ears.”

And she said, in the passive of the house, the shield she had carried so long she no longer felt its weight:

“It has been decided that it is better this way.”

---

Sela stood up.

Sela was standing before she knew she had risen.

“No,” she said.

---

Nobody had contradicted Mother Alaine in that hall in thirty years. Not Wilda, who wanted to. Not Tomas, who could have. Nobody.

And a nineteen-year-old goatherd stood up in the middle of a bench and said *no*, in a flat hard voice with the mountain still in it, and a hundred people turned to look at her, and not one of them told her to sit down.

The room stopped when she spoke. Sela felt that stop in her knees.

She felt it go through her like cold water. She could have said anything. She could have said *tear down the wall of the dead*, and there would have been men on ladders by morning with hammers, weeping, doing it.

And it did not make her feel powerful.

It made her feel like someone standing in a river with her arms out.

“He’s my friend,” said Sela.

---

The hall made a sound.

She heard it and did not understand it, and she went on, too fast, in the over-plain insistent voice she used when she was frightened, arguing with something in her own chest.

“He sat beside me when the bench was empty. He does the work no one sees. He knows every name on the wall.” Sela looked at Fen. “He knew mine before the rest of you decided what it meant.”

She was shaking.

“I won’t do it,” she said. “I won’t. You can decide what you like. I’ll talk to him.”

---

Mother Alaine looked at her for a long moment.

Something shifted in Alaine’s face. A crack no wider than a hair, gone at once.

“Of course you will,” said Alaine.

She turned to the hall.

“The Called may speak to him,” she said.

---

Four words were enough.

By morning Ansel had altered the work list. By noon the camps below were repeating the permission as a new mercy of Whitehart.

Within a week it had a formal name.

---

The next morning the kitchen girl met Fen at the flour chest. She had told him the same poor joke every Thursday for three years, changing only the name of the goat.

She opened her mouth. Then she saw Sela at the far table and closed it again.

Fen waited with the candle-box against his hip.

“The flour has come,” the girl said to Ansel.

Ansel stood between them. He knew the joke. He had complained about it often.

“Put it in the west bin,” he said.

The girl carried the sack away.

Later a novice came to ask which wax belonged in the north cups. He stopped six feet from Fen and asked Sister Wilda instead. Wilda gave the wrong answer. Fen corrected her. The novice thanked Wilda.

At dinner Ansel served Fen last. His eyes stayed on the pot.

No order had forbidden the old conversations. No one had needed one. The house had been told that Sela might speak to him, and every smaller permission disappeared around it.

When Sela entered the hall, the kitchen girl looked down at her hands.

---

She went to find him on the second evening, and it was the worst hour of her life so far, and there had already been several to choose from.

He was at the wall, of course. Doing the north row.

His hands had split. They split every year at the turn of the cold — the wax dried them and the stone took what the wax left, and by the second month of it the skin went at the knuckles and along the pads of the fingers, and it bled a little, and he worked anyway, because the alternative was to stop, and there was nobody to stop for.

“Fen,” she said.

And the whole listening room heard it.

Two novices were lighting the evening candles at the far end. One lowered a taper until wax ran across his fingers. He did not notice.

Fen kept his back to them.

“Go away,” he said.

---

“Fen—”

“Every time you say that,” said Fen, “you make it heavier.” He kept working. His hands were perfectly steady. “There’s a girl in Duskport by now telling her sister that the Called says the nameless man’s name out loud. There’s a man on that road who has walked forty miles to see you and he is going to go home and tell his village that you spoke to me. Twice.” He scraped. “Say it a third time and it’s a sacrament.”

“I was trying to *help* you.”

“I *know* you were.”

He said it so quietly that she almost did not hear it, and there was nothing in it — no anger, no accusation, nothing she could take hold of and argue with.

“That’s the worst part of you,” he said.

---

She stood in the middle of the listening room with her hands at her sides.

“They’ve stopped,” she said. “Haven’t they. The others.”

“Yes.”

“All of them?”

“Sinnet in the kitchen used to swear at me every morning,” said Fen. “Every morning for five years. She’d call me a lazy article and put a heel of bread in my hand and go on shouting at somebody else.” He set the candle in its cup. “I’ve had my bread this week. She puts it on the table now. She looks at the table when she does it.”

He moved down.

“Now they lower their voices when I come in,” he said. “Three days, Sela.”

---

“Then let me undo it.” She was crying and she did not care. “I’ll go and stand up in the hall and I’ll tell them—”

“*What?*”

Fen turned round.

Sela stepped back. Fen’s face had gone plain.

“What will you tell them? That you were wrong? That the kitchen girls may swear at me again?” He did not raise his voice. “They will call that humility and bow harder.”

“That’s not—”

“You said *not that way* about a wet hill,” said Fen, “and a man is in the ground.”

---

The room was very quiet.

The two novices at the far end had not moved. Later accounts show that between them they remembered nearly every word, though not in the same order.

Sela stood there with her face wet and her hands open.

“Anything you say will make it worse,” said Fen. “They use your words before you’ve finished them.”

He picked up the box and put it under his arm.

“So don’t give them mine. Don’t say my name again.”

He reached the arch before he added, “It was never written anywhere.”

And he walked past her, out of the listening room, and he did not touch her and he did not look at her, and Sela stood in the middle of that cold hall in front of the wall of the dead with two novices staring at their own hands.

---

No later account records her saying it again.

The two novices at the far end of the listening room finished the candles.

They took a very long time over it. They did it very thoroughly. And when they had done it they went out past her without looking up, and one of them said good night to her, and she said good night back, and then she was alone in a cold room with three hundred dead people whose names she did not know.

---

## Chapter Seventeen
### Interlude: What the Notes Said

Four words had been enough.

*The Called may speak to him.*

They had come when needed. Alaine had not searched for them. The hall had been waiting, Sela had been standing in the third bench with tears on her face, and there they were.

Afterwards Alaine returned to her room and left the lamp unlit.

From the gallery came the low noise of the road. More fires than the night before. The people below had cut the last trees near the mill and fouled one of the streams. A child had been buried above the ford. Alaine knew this because Ansel had brought her the figures.

They had sold tools to come. Seed had been eaten. Shelters had been put where shelters should not stand.

There was work in every valley of the Banner-lands. People survived by doing it.

The thought came whole and hard.

Alaine sat with it.

She had never slept under a cart. She had never carried a sick child through rain. Food had always been present when the bell rang for meals.

In the morning she ordered grain sent down the road.

The order went before breakfast.

Alaine went to the small kitchen afterwards and waited for water to heat. The main kitchen was already loud with bread, lists and complaints from the lower gate. Here there was one kettle, a cracked stool and a shelf holding cups that did not match.

The novice tending the fire had fallen asleep against the wall.

Alaine did not wake her. She moved the kettle closer to the coals and sat on the stool. Smoke caught in the flue and came back into the room. Her eyes watered.

When the novice woke, she stood so quickly that her head struck the shelf. Two cups fell. One broke. The other rolled beneath Alaine’s chair.

“Mother, I am sorry.”

“The cup is broken.”

“Yes, Mother.”

The girl waited for more. None was required.

Alaine bent and retrieved the unbroken cup. Dust had gathered underneath the shelf, along with a shrivelled pea and a twist of blue thread. She set the cup on the table.

“The grain order—” the novice began.

“Has gone.”

“They say there will not be enough for winter.”

Alaine watched the kettle. It had not begun to sing.

“There may not be.”

The novice’s hands closed around one another. She wanted assurance. Alaine knew several forms of it and used none.

“Sweep under the shelf,” she said.

The girl fetched the broom. By the time the water boiled, the pea and thread were gone. Alaine drank standing because the stool had ash on it.

---

Perrin asked the same question more than once.

That was what troubled her.

He did not repeat himself. He changed the person.

He asked Alaine about the Winter of the Three Voices, then asked Ansel. He spoke to the linen keeper, who was nearly ninety and remembered the old Mother’s hands. He found two shepherds who knew the valley version. He went down to a man whose grandmother had once been a novice.

Every answer was thanked.

Then he put them beside one another.

Alaine saw the pages once, when he had left them open in the guest cell. The same story ran down the sheet in several narrow columns. Her own account stood at the top. The others had been written beneath it in the same calm hand.

She did not read far enough to find the differences.

There had been no need.

---

The house did not compare its Mothers.

A sentence spoken at a deathbed belonged to the dying. A sentence spoken at the winter rite belonged to winter. Tomas had never asked whether the words Alaine gave one frightened novice matched those she had given another thirty years before.

Why would he?

The rites were sound. The house had endured.

Perrin’s ink made occasions sit together that had never met in life.

That evening Alaine passed the griffes and laid her palm against the nearest stone.

She had gone three steps beyond it before she stopped.

Her hand was cold.

She looked back. The passage was empty. A wick guttered in one of the lower cups and righted itself.

The stone had been touched every night for years. It was expected now. The novices watched for it. Once, long ago, one of them had copied her. Alaine had corrected the girl.

She could not remember what reason she had given.

She went on.

At the turn of the passage she stopped again.

No prayer had been said. None was required.

---

The next evening she found Tomas in the Ledger room.

The shutter stood open. His fingers were red from the cold. Nothing lay on the table except the book and the iron dish where he burned his papers.

“Tomas.”

“Mother.”

She took the stool.

He waited.

“What did the notes say?”

His brow drew in. “Which notes?”

“The trial notes.”

“They are burned.”

“Yes.”

He waited again. The shutter knocked once in its frame.

Alaine folded her hands more tightly.

“Before they were burned.”

Tomas looked at her for a long time.

“They said what I saw.”

“Give me an example.”

He looked down at the dish.

“*She stops mid-word and listens outward. The room turns with her.*”

Sela’s trial.

Alaine remembered the flame. Or believed she did.

“And then?”

“I judged.”

“From that?”

“From all of it.”

The answer was ordinary. It should have closed the matter.

Alaine heard herself ask, “Have you judged wrongly?”

Tomas did not move.

There had been a better way to phrase it. There had been no need to ask it at all.

“Yes,” he said.

The word came without defence.

He put his hands flat on the table.

“There is a page five years old that ends after four words. Fen came from it.”

Alaine said nothing.

“I did not kill him. I have used that sentence often.” Tomas rubbed one thumb against the wood. “It remains true.”

The shutter struck the wall.

“There is a red page as well,” he said. “Sixteen years old.”

Alaine knew which one before he named the mother.

“Merta stands below the elbow at first snow. I watch from the rock.”

“You go there?”

“Every year.”

“Does she know?”

“No.”

“Why do you hide?”

His mouth opened. Nothing came.

Alaine had asked too many questions. She rose.

“Mother.”

She turned at the door.

“You asked what the notes said.”

“Yes.”

“They were never the difficulty.”

---

She left him in the cold.

At the griffes her hand remained at her side.

She walked past the nearest niche. One step. Two. Three.

At four she stopped.

The passage was darker here. No lamp reached the floor.

She went back and placed her palm against the stone.

It was an old griffe, the name worn shallow. She knew the office attached to it but could not bring the person to mind.

Alaine stood there until the cold entered her fingers.

Nothing changed.

She took her hand away.

The following morning she spoke the first office before the dead. Her voice held. The pauses came where they had always come.

A novice began to weep before the final response.

Alaine finished the words.

---

## Chapter Eighteen
### The Elbow

By midsummer the valley was not a valley any more.

Four thousand people lived between the house and the ford. Ansel had counted them.

He counted by cooking fires, then by water pots, then by the trenches cut above the road. None of the numbers agreed.

The camps had spread onto every flat piece of ground. Rope ran between alder trees. Wet shirts hung beside prayer strips and never dried. Three families shared a roof made from a sail carrying the faded mark of Tidebreaker. A Phoenix-made stove stood in the mud near the ford, clever enough to burn half the wood and broken in a place no one in the valley knew how to mend.

Water became the day’s first quarrel. The upper spring was clean and slow. The lower stream was fast and brown from feet, animals and rain. Ansel put novices on the spring with two barrels and a rule: one vessel per fire before noon.

By the second morning the rule had six exceptions and three people claiming to be widows of the same man.

Sela carried water because no one could stop her without making the stopping into a ceremony. She took the back path, filled two buckets and brought them to the cook line.

A man reached for one and then saw her face.

“Not for me,” he said.

“You were reaching for it.”

“I did not know.”

“Now you do. Take it.”

He backed away. The woman behind him took both buckets and poured them into the common pot before anyone could object.

Ivet heard of it by evening. In her account, Sela had refused to let the water belong to one person. By the next day, a group near the ford had begun sharing vessels in her name.

The original woman complained that she had only been in a hurry.

Meanwhile a mule died above the lower trench and had to be dragged out before the water rose. Fen found rope. Ansel found six men. Sela took the hind legs because nobody else would stand there.

For half an hour the Called of Whitehart pulled a dead mule through mud while two pilgrims argued over whether the animal had bowed its head to her before dying.

Sela lost her footing and went down on one knee.

The mule had not bowed. It had bitten her sleeve that morning.

Shelters covered the flats. Every tree below the mill was gone. Two wells had soured, and a child lay buried above the ford after dying of a flux that a dry room might have spared her.

No one had planned a town. No one was leaving it.

They had sold what they had to come here. A great many of them had nothing to go back to, and the ones who did had begun to find that when you have walked forty miles to stand below a holy mountain and wait for something, and nothing happens, you do not go home; you wait longer, because to leave is to admit that you walked forty miles for nothing.

And the house fed them, out of its stores, because the alternative was to watch them starve at the gate.

And the stores would not last the winter. Ansel had done the sums in the spring, and had gone on doing them, quietly, once a fortnight, and the numbers did not improve.

The question, when it finally came, was so small that it took Tomas a week to understand why it frightened everybody.

Nobody had ever needed to know.

For four hundred years the boundary had been *the line in the grant*, and the line in the grant was where the house’s ground began, and everyone said it was the elbow, and nobody had ever had the smallest reason to check, because there had never been anything on the elbow except a rock and a path and a woman with a coat.

Now there were nine hundred people on it.

If the elbow belonged to Whitehart, the house owed peace, water and burial to the nine hundred people camped there.

If it did not—

“Then they are the valley’s problem,” said Brother Osric, “and not ours.”

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

---

They sent to Oath-hold.

Blackthorn could advise. Blackcrest could shape the account afterwards. A disputed word required Stonebear, and Stonebear’s reading would bind both sides whether either liked it or not.

Witness Orla arrived on a wet Tuesday, alone, on a bad horse, with a satchel and a rolled copy of the grant.

She was about fifty and built like a door.

---

She would not take the guest cell.

“Guest-right,” she said, “is a great deal more than a bed, Brother, and I would rather not be under yours while I am reading your grant. It is nothing personal and it is not a slight. I will take the loft over the byre and I will pay for it.”

“You’ll pay for a *loft*.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody pays for the byre loft. It’s a *loft*.”

“Then it will be a very small payment,” said Witness Orla, “and it will take a very small amount of time to make, and it will still be worth more than my reputation.” And she went and slept in the loft, and paid Ansel four marks for it, and Ansel — who had been trying to give her the money back for three days — was still complaining about it a year later.

Tomas discovered, within about a day, that he liked her enormously.

She had a dry, flat, unhurried way of talking, and she laughed at things that were funny and did not laugh at things that were not, and she ate what she was given, and she read the grant.

She read the grant for six days.

---

She read it in the yard, in the rain, with a board across her knees, comparing the house’s copy with her own copy with a third copy she had got from somewhere and would not say where. She measured things. She went out on the path with a cord and a stick and paced it, twice, on two different days, and made a note of the difference, and went out a third time.

She talked to the shepherds. She talked to Bryn Corr for two hours and came back looking as if she had enjoyed herself.

She went and stood on the elbow for an entire afternoon and did nothing at all that anybody could see.

On the fourth evening Sela found Merta below the elbow with the coat. It was not the anniversary. Merta had begun coming more often.

Tomas, on the gallery, could not hear it.

He watched the two women stand together on the path in the rain for a quarter of an hour.

---

“You’ll want to know what I asked her,” said Orla that night.

“It’s none of my business.”

“No, it isn’t, and I shall tell you anyway, because it will be in the ruling and you may as well hear it from me first.” She had her boots off and her feet at the fire and she looked exactly like a woman who was about to ruin somebody’s life and had made her peace with it years ago. “I asked her how long she had been standing there.”

“Sixteen years.”

“Yes. And I asked her where, precisely. And she took me to the stone and put her hand on it and told me that in sixteen years she has never once put a foot above it, because your Sister Wilda told her in the first year exactly where the line was, and she has believed her ever since.” Orla turned her cup. “It is the only thing that woman has left that nobody can take off her, Brother. She has stood on the right side of a line for sixteen years out of pure spite and pure grief and pure obedience, and I have never in my life met a person who has kept a rule so exactly for so long with so little reason to.”

She drank.

“And your Sister Wilda told her wrong,” said Witness Orla.

---

Tomas sat up.

“Not far. Not knowingly.” Orla pulled the grant across the table and pressed one thick finger to the line. “The grant says the bend of the path. You have been using the stone.”

“They are the same place,” said Tomas.

“They were. The old path ran by the spring. The spring dried under Mother Ilse and the path moved. The grant did not. Your boundary is thirty paces higher.”

She sat back.

“Sixteen years she stood thirty paces below the place the grant allowed.”

---

Tomas said, carefully:

“And what does that mean?”

“The elbow is Whitehart ground. Everything above the true turn always was. Merta has been standing in the valley all these years.”

Tomas let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

“And the camps?”

Witness Orla looked at him.

And Tomas — who had spent thirty years reading the faces of frightened people, and had lately begun to wonder whether he had ever been any good at it — watched a woman of House Stonebear decide, at her leisure, not to spare him.

“The camps,” she said, “are below the true turn.”

“All of them?”

“All of them. Every soul. Nine hundred on the elbow, three thousand and more on the road and the flats.” She rolled the grant up. “They are on the valley’s ground. They are not on yours. You have no duty of care to them, no obligation to keep the peace among them, no requirement to feed them, and no right to move them.”

She tied the cord.

“And you may shut your gate,” said Witness Orla, “whenever you please, and no oath is broken.”

---

The fire cracked.

“You cannot rule that,” said Tomas.

“I have.”

“There are four thousand people down there and winter is coming.”

“Yes.”

“They will *die*.”

“A great many of them, very probably,” said Orla, “and I have thought about almost nothing else for six days, and I would rather you did not imagine that this is easy for me, because I find that insulting and I am too old for it.”

She set the grant on the table between them.

“You wanted to know what the word says,” she said. “The word says what it says. It said it four hundred years ago in a room neither of us was in, to men who could not have imagined a single thing about our lives, and it does not know about your winter and it does not care about your four thousand and it is not *for* that.”

---

“The *intent*,” said Tomas. “Surely the intent of the grant—”

And Witness Orla laughed.

Orla laughed without amusement. She had heard the same defence in other halls.

“Brother,” she said, “in twenty years of this work I have been told about intent perhaps two hundred times, and I will tell you the one thing I have learned about it, and you may keep it, and it is worth more than my fee.”

She leaned forward.

“**Intent is what a man says his word meant, after he finds out what it cost him.**”

She held up a hand before he could speak.

“I hear about intent from the losing side, after the letter has gone against them. Never before.” Orla pushed the grant back. “The strong can always hire someone to explain what dead men meant. I read what they wrote.”

The rain went across the shutter.

“The word is a wall,” said Orla. “It is a very poor wall. It is cold and it is stupid and it does not care who is standing behind it. And it is the only thing in the Banner-lands that stands between the weak and whoever is strongest, and every single time somebody knocks a hole in it for a good reason, the next man through the hole has a worse one.”

---

Tomas sat with his hands flat on the table.

Tomas searched the wording for an error. He checked terms, dates and measurements. They held.

She was right.

She was completely right, and four thousand people were going to be standing outside a shut gate in the snow because she was right, and there was no argument in the world that would not, if he made it, be used within a generation by someone worse than either of them.

“I have not shut your gate,” said Witness Orla.

She got up and put her cup on the shelf, and picked up her satchel, and stood there in the doorway with the rain behind her.

“I have told you that you *may*,” she said. “That is all a witness does. What you do with it is yours, and it will be yours in a hundred years, and it will be yours in four hundred, and I will not be there and neither will you, and the grant will still say what it says.”

She looked at him for a moment.

“Feed them,” she said. “Ruin yourselves. Open your gate and let them eat every grain in that mountain and starve with them in the spring. Nothing in that word forbids it. It does not oblige you and it does not prevent you.” Her face did not change at all. “It has only stopped being a thing you can hide behind.”

And she went out into the rain, and went and slept in the loft above the byre, which she had paid four marks for.

---

She left the next morning.

Tomas walked down with her as far as the elbow, and neither of them said very much, and at the turn — the *true* turn, thirty paces above the stone, where the path bent and the grant had put a line that nobody in the Bell of Silence had ever gone out and checked — Witness Orla stopped.

She stood there for a while and looked down.

Smoke from four thousand fires covered the flat to the ford. Their voices reached the house as one thin sound.

Merta was on the path a little way down, thirty paces below, standing where she had always stood.

Witness Orla lifted a hand to her.

Merta looked back up the hill at her for a long moment. And did not move.

“She’ll not come up,” said Tomas.

“No,” Orla agreed. “I didn’t think she would.”

She got on her bad horse.

“I gave Merta a right she may never use. I gave your Mother a gate she may close.” Orla packed the grant. “Every word is correct.”

And she rode down through the camps, past four thousand people who had no idea who she was, and none of them looked up.

---

Mother Alaine received the ruling that evening, in front of the seniors, and read it once, and folded it, and said nothing at all.

Then somebody’s mule got into the kitchen garden and there was a great deal of shouting, and the seniors of House Whitehart went out into the yard to see, because it was the most interesting thing that had happened all week, and Steward Ansel was extremely rude to a novice, and by the time it was over everyone had gone in to supper.

---

## Chapter Nineteen
### What He Left Running

He was ten months in the Bell of Silence, and when he left, the house wept.

His mule refused the first pack.

Perrin had arranged the load carefully: books in oilcloth, dry shirts, three jars from Ansel, letters sealed for Crown-watch. The mule turned her head, looked at the weight and sat down in the yard.

The house gathered to say farewell while a Blackcrest herald argued with a mule.

“She knows you are leaving,” Ivet said.

“She knows the left strap is twisted,” Fen said.

He untwisted it. The mule stood.

Perrin bowed to Ivet as if her interpretation had also been useful.

People brought gifts he could not carry. A carved cup. A packet of mountain salt. A child’s drawing of the Bell with smoke coming out of the wrong side. Perrin thanked each giver, then passed half the gifts quietly to Ansel, who returned them to the stores before the gate closed.

Tomas came last with a folded sheet.

“A list?” Perrin asked.

“Corrections.”

“To the Chronicle?”

“To your spelling of local names.”

Perrin opened it. There were four names and a note about the old count on the third row.

“You could have given me this nine months ago.”

“You did not ask correctly.”

Perrin smiled. Tomas did not.

They stood facing each other with the yard moving around them. A novice tightened the wrong strap. Fen undid it. The mule tried to bite the packet of salt.

“You will write it kindly,” Tomas said.

Perrin folded the page again. “Sometimes.”

It was not reassurance. Tomas accepted it because none was available.

Sela was not prepared for that. She stood on the gallery on the morning of it and watched the yard fill up with people who had come to say goodbye to a man from House Blackcrest — the house of the victor’s history, the house that had written the Chronicle, the house every child in the Light Heights was raised to distrust — and half of them were crying.

Steward Ansel had made him a parcel of food for the road and had got it wrong on purpose, and had put in twice what a man needs, and was standing in the doorway pretending to do sums.

The kitchen girls had made him something. He put it on immediately and it was too small and he wore it anyway and looked ridiculous and knew it.

Brother Osric shook his hand for slightly too long.

And Sister Wilda — Sister Wilda, who had called him a trick to his face at the noon meal in his first week — came out into the yard, stood in front of him, and said something short that nobody else heard, and Perrin bowed to her, properly, the full bow, and she went back inside without another word and did not come out again that day.

He found Sela on the gallery an hour before he rode.

“I’m not going to ask you for anything,” he said.

“Everyone says that.”

“Yes,” said Perrin, “and they’re all lying, and so am I, but I’m going to lie less than most.” He put his elbows on the rail beside her, and they stood there together looking down the valley at the smoke of four thousand fires. “I want to ask you one thing and I would like you to know that whatever you answer, I will write exactly the same entry. So there is nothing you can win here and nothing you can lose, and you may tell me the truth or not, as you please.”

“Ask, then.”

“Do you hear anything?”

Sela looked out at the smoke.

“No,” she said.

“No — nothing at all? Ever? Not once?”

“I don’t know,” said Sela. “That’s the true answer and nobody wants it. Something happened in the winter of the pass. Something happened at my trial. I don’t know what. It might have been the wind and three tired people. It might have been a frightened girl who learned a trick that works on rooms and doesn’t know she learned it.” Her hands were flat on the rail. “I have never once been able to make it happen and I have never once been able to make it stop, and I have gone over it every night for a year, and I am *further* from an answer than I was at the start. And I would give anything in the world to be told.”

Perrin nodded slowly.

“I know,” he said.

---

“What will you write?”

“You want to hear it?”

“I want to hear it.”

Perrin told her the entry he had begun.

A goatherd tested by Whitehart. A grave opened on the east shoulder. A warning refused. Harl dead. The words *nothing happened* preserved as humility. The nameless dead given an office in front of the house.

He had the order ready. He did not search for a sentence once.

“That’s — ” Sela’s voice had gone somewhere she could not get it back from. “Every word of that is true.”

“Yes.”

“And every word of it is *wrong*.”

“Yes,” said Perrin. “That’s what a Chronicle is.”

---

She rounded on him.

“Then don’t write it!”

“Somebody will.”

“Then let it be somebody who gets it *wrong*!” She was shaking. “Let it be the songs! Let it be some drunk in Duskport making up a story about a girl on a mountain — at least then it’d be *obviously* a story, at least then somebody in four hundred years might *doubt* it—”

“Sela.”

“—but you’ll write it and it’ll be *true*, every line of it will be true, and nobody will ever be able to argue with a word of it, and it will be a *lie*, and it will be a lie that can’t be caught—”

“Sela,” said Perrin. “That is the most intelligent thing anybody has said to me in nine years, and you are right, and I am going to do it anyway.”

---

The wind came up the valley.

“Because if I don’t,” he said, “the songs will say you cursed him. They’re already saying it, in two villages, and I have heard both, and I have written both down, and I have not decided yet what to do about them.” He turned and put his back against the rail. “That’s the choice. Not between a true version and a false one. Between the version that makes you a saint and the version that gets you burned. And I would like you to know that I have thought about it for a long time and I am not certain that I have chosen right, and I am going to do it anyway, because there is a Jubilee in four years and *somebody has to hold the pen.*”

Sela stared out at the smoke.

“You could just leave me out,” she said.

“I considered leaving it out. Four thousand people, Harl, and a girl who said nothing happened.” Perrin rubbed his eyes. “That would be honest too, for a while.”

---

She did not answer for a while.

“You understand me better than anyone in this house,” she said at last. “Don’t you.”

“Yes.”

He said it without any pleasure at all.

“I understand you better than Brother Tomas, who loves you and cannot afford to. Better than your Mother, who has other business. Better than the girl who follows you about, who understands you not at all and would die for you tomorrow.” He straightened up and pulled on his gloves. “And it is of no use to you whatever, because in three hours I am going to get on a horse, and what I understand goes in a book, and books do not help people. They only outlive them.”

---

He was at the top of the stair when she said it.

“Then tell me something useful,” said Sela. “Just once. As a person.”

Perrin stopped.

He stood there for a long moment with one hand on the rail.

“Go,” he said.

“What?”

“*Go.* Leave. Walk out of here.” He did not turn round. “You are nineteen years old and you have become an institution and they will not let you stop. Every honest word out of your mouth makes it worse — you have proved that four times over now, and you will go on proving it, and there is no version of staying in which you are still a person in ten years.”

“They’d follow me.”

“Some of them. Fewer than you think. They love a saint on a mountain; they’ll not all walk east into the cold after one.” He looked back over his shoulder at last, and his face was perfectly kind and perfectly certain. “And I will tell you the other half of it, because I said I’d lie less than most: it is also extremely useful to me if you go. A girl who stays becomes a shrine, and a shrine belongs to Whitehart. A girl who walks out into the Silent Mountains and is not heard of again belongs to *everybody*, and I will write that ending better than any man alive, and it will be the finest thing I ever do.”

He pulled his glove tight.

“Both of those are true,” said Perrin. “I have never been able to work out how to say them in an order that doesn’t make one a lie. Go anyway.”

And he went down.

---

She watched him from the gallery.

He said goodbye to the whole house, and it took him an hour, and he did it properly, one by one.

And at the end, when the horse was brought and the yard was full and he had his foot half in the stirrup, he stopped, and turned round, and looked at the passage door, where a young man in a leather apron was standing with a candle-box under his arm, watching, because he could not come out into a yard full of people and be spoken to.

Perrin walked back across the yard.

Before the whole hall, Perrin stopped at the passage door and addressed Fen by name.

Sela could not hear it. Nobody could hear it. Nobody afterwards could agree on how long it lasted or whether Fen answered.

But she saw the Herald of House Blackcrest put out his hand.

And she saw Fen — after a moment; after a long moment — take it.

And Perrin said one more thing, quite short, and let go, and turned round, and walked back across the yard through a silence you could have stood a spoon in, and got on his horse.

*“Four years,”* he said. That is what he said. Fen told nobody, ever, and it did not matter, because half the house had read it off his face.

---

The valley turned out for him.

The valley lined the road to the ford. People came from shelters and the flats to cheer the man who had found roofs, paid Corr and carried their names away.

He raised a hand to them. He did it well. He was still doing it at the bend.

Sela watched from the gallery. Perrin rode with empty saddlebags except for his papers.

He had not needed to.

---

Everything he had built was still there.

The two stories about her were still running, and they had grown, and they no longer needed him to feed them. The valley was still full of people who could not go home. The pilgrims still came. Bryn Corr still had her routes and her beds and her cousins on the road, and the mill still ran six days, and the man on the coast was still selling little bone copies of Sela at four for a mark.

The house still had a ruling in a chest that said it might shut its gate.

Below, Ivet sat on a stone beside Tam. She spoke with both hands. Tam listened in Harl’s coat.

He had stopped being angry.

At the grave he had shaken. Now he sat still and kept his eyes on the mud.

She could not hear what Ivet was saying.

Ivet was probably telling him something true.

---

Steward Ansel came up to the gallery at dusk, where the Mother was standing, and he did not know that Sela was at the far end in the dark, and he did not lower his voice, because he had nothing to hide and never had.

“Mother,” he said. “The stores.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve done it four times and I’ll not do it again, because the number does not move.” He had his tally-stick with him. He always had his tally-stick. “There’s grain in that mountain for this house until the thaw, with nothing to spare and a hard month at the end of it. That’s if we feed *ourselves*.”

“And the road?”

“There’s four thousand on the road,” said Ansel. “We’ve been feeding them since the spring out of the surplus, and there is no surplus now, and there won’t be another until the harvest, and the harvest is in the valley, and the valley is under four thousand people who have cut every tree below the mill and fouled the water in two places and eaten the seed corn.”

He let that sit.

“If we go on feeding them,” he said, “this house is empty by the third week of the cold, and then there’s four thousand of them and a hundred of us and nothing at all in the whole valley, and I have seen what that looks like once, when I was a boy, and I would rather be dead than see it twice.”

He turned the stick over in his hands.

“And if we don’t,” said Ansel, “we shut the gate. And they’re on the valley’s ground, and the Stonebear woman says we may.”

The wind came up off the valley with the smoke in it.

And Mother Alaine stood at the rail with her hands folded, and looked down at four thousand fires, and said nothing at all.

---

## Chapter Twenty
### The Gate

They argued for three days. The stores did not change.

During those three days the bread changed.

Ansel reduced each loaf by one slice, then by two. He mixed barley husks into the flour and told the kitchen to bake the crust hard so people would chew longer. The novices complained until they learned the same bread was going down the road.

The wood store fell faster than the grain. Wet people needed fires. Sick people needed hot water. The house began burning old furniture from the upper cells. A bench from the novice school went first. Then two broken chests. Nobody suggested the carved chair in the listening room.

At the gate, the hinges had sunk under constant use. Harl would have known how to lift them. Fen and Ansel tried with a bar and made the lower pin worse.

“Stop,” Wilda said.

“It is stopping on its own,” Fen answered.

The joke received nothing. Beyond the gate, people were calling for water.

Sela carried stones to brace the post. Her palms split where Harl’s knife-handle had rubbed them weeks before. A novice offered gloves and then withdrew them, uncertain whether touching the Called’s blood would mean something.

“Put them down,” Sela said.

The novice placed the gloves on the ground between them.

Sela picked them up after she left.

By the second day, sleep had gone thin inside the Bell. People argued over words they would ordinarily have let pass. Wilda corrected *refugees* to *pilgrims* in one meeting and *pilgrims* to *petitioners* in the next. Osric asked whether the distinction changed the ration. Wilda said no. They spent another hour on it.

Outside, a child struck a cup against the gate in a steady rhythm until someone took it away.

Sister Wilda said close it. A house that starved itself was not merciful, she said. Every sack sent down the path brought more people up it. Whitehart could not destroy itself and call that love.

Brother Osric said close it, and he had the sums, and the sums did not move.

Steward Ansel said nothing at all, because he had already said it, and because he had done the arithmetic four times and did not intend to be asked to do it a fifth.

And Brother Tomas stood up in front of the seniors of House Whitehart and said:

“Then we will do it with our own hands, and we will not pretend that a Stonebear ruling did it for us.”

And he lost.

Sela went to the Mother that night.

She had never once gone to Mother Alaine’s room. Nobody did. She stood in the passage for a long time with her hand up, and then she knocked, and the voice from inside said *come*, without any surprise in it at all, and Sela went in and found her sitting in the dark with no lamp lit.

“Open the gate,” said Sela.

“Sit down, child.”

“I don’t want to sit down. Open the gate. There are four thousand people out there and the frost is a month off and we have grain in this mountain and they have *nothing*—”

“Sit down,” said Alaine, “and I will show you the numbers, and then you may tell me what to do, and I will do it.”

---

Alaine lit the lamp, brought out Ansel’s boards and took Sela through them line by line. It took an hour and a half.

The valley had no grain. Seed corn was gone. Pilgrims had bought the rest at four times its old price, and the valley now bought it back at six. The fields were camps and mud. Trees below the mill had become fuel. The ford water was foul; two children had died.

Alaine showed her how long the grain would feed a hundred people, then four thousand.

Sela counted the second number twice on the board with her own finger, because she could not read but she could count, and she had counted goats and shares and stones her whole life and she was very good at it.

Nine days.

Nine days separated a full store from an empty one. After that, four thousand people and the Bell would have nothing.

---

“I saw it once,” said Alaine.

She said it quietly, and she did not look up from the board.

“In the year I came here. Not in this valley — over the wall, in the Blackthorn country, when the pass shut early and a town of six thousand was cut off from its grain.” Her hands were folded. “I was twelve. I do not intend to describe it to you and you will not ask me to.”

The lamp burned.

“That is what is on the other side of the gate,” said Alaine. “Four thousand people fed for *nine days*. Then an empty store and the same road.”

She closed the board.

“I have found no other ending. If you have one, give it to me and I will act on it tonight.”

Sela sat with her hands on her knees and looked at the board.

She could not find one.

---

“Then—” Her voice was not working properly. “Then let them *in*. Let them into the house. There’s room, there’s the cellars and the galleries and the—”

“There is room for perhaps four hundred,” said Alaine. “There are four thousand.”

“Then *four hundred!*”

“Which four hundred?”

The lamp went sideways.

“Say it,” said Alaine, and her voice was perfectly gentle. “You are the Called of House Whitehart, and this house will do what you tell it. Say which four hundred. Name them. The sick? Then a strong man will smother a sick child to take her place, and he will not be a monster; he will be a father. The children? Then the mothers stay outside and watch the door shut, and I have seen that too. The ones nearest the front? Then they will crush each other on the path to be nearest the front, and they will do it tonight, and the ones who die will be the old and the small.”

She let it sit.

“Choose,” said Mother Alaine. “I mean it. I will do it. I will stand in that yard tomorrow morning and I will open the gate to whatever four hundred you name, and I will bear it, and it will be the last thing I do in this house.”

Sela sat in the dark and could not speak.

---

“And now I will tell you the other thing,” said Alaine, “because you are owed it, and because you will find it out anyway, and I would rather you had it from me.”

She stood up.

“I am going to close the gate,” she said. “Tomorrow, at first light, formally, in front of the whole house, and I will give the order myself and I will not hide behind Ansel or Wilda or a Stonebear ruling, and it will be mine.”

She went to the door and stood with her hand on it.

“And if you tell me not to,” said Mother Alaine, “I will not.”

---

Sela’s head came up.

“You are the Called,” said Alaine. “You stood up in that hall and told me *no*, in front of a hundred people, and I gave way, and everyone in the Banner-lands knows it. If you come out into that yard tomorrow morning and tell me to open the gate, I will open it. The house will obey you. It will obey you over me and it will do it gladly, and it will do it before the sun is up.” Her face was entirely calm. “I will not fight you. I will not argue. I will stand aside and I will let you do it, and it will be your word, and not mine.”

She opened the door.

“So it is yours now,” said Mother Alaine. “It has been yours since the day you told the truth in that hall, and I am sorry, and there is nothing whatever I can do about it, and neither can you.”

And she went out and left the girl sitting in the dark with the boards.

---

Sela did not sleep.

She went out at the deepest part of the night and walked down through the sleeping house to the yard, and stood in front of the gate.

The gate was two old oak leaves, an iron bar, a hasp and a peg. Sela had seen it open every day of her life.

She stood in front of it for a long time.

Sela put her hand on the oak.

Nine days of grain. Four thousand people below. The house behind her.

She counted again and got the same answer.

If the gate opened, the stores emptied. If it stayed shut, people on the road would go hungry. She did not know how many. She knew it would begin soon.

Her mouth had opened before on its own. This time she kept it closed.

The silence came easily.

No witness records her at the gate before dawn.

---

The gate was closed at first light.

Mother Alaine gave the order in the yard in front of the whole house, in her own voice, without hiding behind anyone. She did not say it was merciful. She did not say it was necessary. She said that House Whitehart had grain for its own people and no more, and that the gate would be shut until the thaw, and that she had ordered it, and that it was hers.

And Sela stood in the third rank with the rest of them and said nothing at all.

Nobody noticed. Why would they? The Called had not spoken. The Called was famous for not speaking.

It went down the mountain within the hour and it landed on four thousand people who had been eating the house’s bread since the spring.

---

The first day, they did not believe it.

On the second day a crowd came to the gate and called until dark. The house did not answer. They went down when the cold took the path.

The third day it rained.

On the fourth morning ice covered the path. A woman left a baby at the gate and walked away. The house lifted the child over the wall in a basket. The gate stayed shut.

---

Brem came up on the fourth night.

Sela heard it from a kitchen girl at dawn — that a boy had been at the wicket in the dark and had said he was the Called’s brother, and the novices had not known what to do with him, and had put him in the byre with a blanket and gone to fetch the steward, and he was still in there.

She was in the yard before she had finished dressing.

Steward Ansel was standing at the byre door with his tally-stick under his arm and his face like a wall.

“He’s not sick,” said Ansel.

“He’s twelve.”

“He’s twelve and he’s not sick, and I have a list.” He did not raise his voice and he did not look away from her. “There are fourteen names on it and nine places, because five of them won’t survive the carrying, and I have gone through it four times and I have taken out the ones I put in for pity, and what is left is what is left.” He turned the stick over in his hands. “The boy is not on it. He is a healthy child of a family that has a house and a fire and a mother who is alive.”

Sela stood in the middle of the yard.

She did not say *let him in*. She did not say it.

She stood there. She stood there in the cold in her boots with her arms at her sides and looked at Steward Ansel, and Steward Ansel looked back at her, and somewhere behind them a novice stopped what he was doing and stood still to watch, and then another one did.

And Sela did not say anything at all.

Sela counted her breaths without meaning to. At nineteen she stopped. Ansel’s face had changed.

She watched it change.

She watched a man of fifty-five work out, standing in a byre door with a tally-stick under his arm, exactly what he was being asked and by whom and in front of how many, and what it would sound like at the ford by evening if the steward of the Bell of Silence turned the Called’s own brother out into the cold.

And something in her chest came up warm and easy, like a hand opening.

She did not stop it.

Ansel looked at her for a long moment.

Then he took the stick from under his arm and made a mark on it and said, “Right,” and went inside, and gave Brem a bowl.

---

The girl’s name was Wenna and she was twelve years old and she had the flux, and she was the tenth name on Ansel’s list, and there were nine places.

She was two hours down the road with her aunt when the list closed.

Sela did not know about her. It made no difference: the list closed at nine, and the ninth place went to a healthy boy from a house with a fire, and Wenna and her aunt sat on the path for a day and a night and then went back down to the flat, because there was nowhere else in the world for them to go.

Sela found out three weeks later, from Ivet, who told her because she thought it would comfort her — *she went easy, Sister, at the end; she was not in pain; her aunt says she asked after you* —

and Sela said, “I didn’t know she was on the list.”

She did not go down to the burial. There were reasons, and the seniors gave them to her before she had asked for any, and she took them.

For a week afterwards she was unbearable to be near. She snapped at a novice who had done nothing. She would not look at Fen. And when Brother Tomas came to her cell to ask whether she was well, she told him in a flat hard voice with the mountain in it that she was perfectly well and would he *please* stop coming.

---

And on the fifth day, four thousand people came up the mountain.

---

Sela watched it from the upper gallery.

Wilda had ordered her to remain above and out of sight. If the crowd saw the Called at the gate, they might rush the path.

So she stood at the rail with her hands on the stone and watched.

The crowd filled the path from the gate, past the elbow and down beyond sight. Their voices reached the wall as one thin roar.

Every surviving account says they were unarmed.

They were cold and hungry. Some had walked forty miles for Sela’s name and sold what they owned to remain. Children were buried above the ford. Somewhere in the middle of the crowd, one child coughed without stopping.

Above them stood a house with grain and a closed gate.

The men of the house stood inside the gate with staves.

They were novices, mostly. They were frightened. Two of them were sixteen.

---

Nobody gave an order.

Nobody has ever been able to say who threw the first stone, and a great many people have been very sure about it, and none of them were within a hundred feet.

There was no charge. The path left rock on one side and a drop on the other. Those at the front could not retreat. Those behind could not see.

The shouting at the gate changed. The gate stayed shut. People went over the side.

---

It lasted, they think, a little under a quarter of an hour.

A woman of the coast, questioned three winters later, said the mud had gone to grease. A carter in front of her fell. She stepped on him because the people behind kept moving. A novice had his fingers between the gate leaves and shouted a name she knew; the boy outside was his cousin. When the press reached the gate, the shouting changed. A stave struck a shoulder near her ear. An old man in a grey coat sat down and disappeared under the crowd. By then the gate could not have opened inward. Bodies held it shut.

*Nobody struck first.* That is the one thing the accounts agree on, and the Chronicle says otherwise.

A man named Ordin Vass, of the ford, who had been at the front and came out of it with a broken collarbone, went back up the path twice that night in the dark, alone, and brought two people down off the slope. One of them was a woman he had been at law with for six years over a wall.

He told nobody. She told nobody. It is in one account, from a man who watched it from the rocks and did not go down himself, and who is not otherwise reliable about anything.

Vass was compensated, because his ground was above the true turn. He took the grain. He went on cursing the house at the ford every market day, and he is on record doing it.

The house counted nine dead.

The valley counted twelve.

Both counts were made carefully. The difference is three.

The house entered nine. The valley songs kept twelve. Neither record explains the missing number.

Most of them died of the press. Two went over the edge. One was an old man who simply stopped, in the middle of it, and sat down, and was still sitting there when they came to lift him.

None of them were killed by anybody.

---

The novice on the inside of the gate was called Wick, and he was sixteen, and he had his fingers in the gap of the leaves when the press came on, and he lost two of them.

The house said he had held the gate.

The flat by the ford said he had broken a woman’s arm with a stave and laughed while he did it, and the woman was named, and she had a broken arm, and she said it herself, twice, and then a third time she said she was not sure, and by then it did not matter.

Wick was sixteen. He had a stave, two fingers in the hinge and no view beyond the first bodies. He did not run.

One song calls him saint. Another calls him butcher. Both are sung in the Light Heights.

---

The Ordins would not have Whitehart bury their son.

They carried him down the mountain themselves, on the second day, in the rain, past the house’s own diggers who had come out to help and who stood back and let them go by — and they buried him in the low pasture in ground that had never held anybody, and there was no rite of any kind, and Brother Tomas asked to come and was told, politely, that he was not wanted.

The Chronicle records that the dead of the gate were buried with the full office of House Whitehart.

That is what the Chronicle says, and it is what the house believed it had done, and there is a grave in the low pasture that the house has never had a good answer for.

---

Whitehart paid for three of the dead.

They had fallen above the true turn, inside the boundary Orla had measured. Their families received grain to the last measure.

The other six had died below it.

Below the turn is the valley’s ground. The house has no duty of care there and no obligation and no liability, and a Stonebear ruling in a chest said so in a hand nobody could argue with, and Brother Osric read it out to the assembled seniors and to two families who had come up to ask, and he read it kindly, and he was correct.

They got nothing.

Four feet separated one paid body from one unpaid body.

The boundary ran between them.

---

Osric wrote the account that went down the mountain. Tomas allowed it.

He had watched Perrin work for ten months and kept the notes. The account gave the stores, the lack of any order to strike and the press on the frozen path. No witness could name a beginning.

It omitted the two days of calling below the gate.

It omitted the baby lifted over the wall.

Tomas read it in the Ledger room with the shutter open.

He read it twice, and put it down, and sat with his hands flat on the table for a long while.

Osric expected the account to leave the camps no order, name or single man to punish.

And the fuller account would give them a boy with two fingers gone, and a Mother, and two days of a house that had heard them calling and had not answered — and there were four thousand of them and a hundred in the house, and there was a mill in the middle of the valley with Bryn Corr’s family living in it.

Nobody burned anything that winter. The mill is still standing.

Whether the account is why, no source can say — and no source can say whether the crowd on the flat was in any condition by then to burn a mill at all. Two of the surviving camp songs put it down to the carts from Duskport. Neither mentions the account. Neither mentions the house.

“It’s a good account,” said Tomas.

“It’s true,” said Osric.

“Yes,” said Tomas. “That’s what makes it good.”

And he signed it, and it went down the mountain.

Tomas, who had burned every working note of his office for sixteen years, put his name to an arranged account and let it stand. Every written line was true.

He read it again in spring, found no falsehood and signed the register a second time.

There is a letter in the Crown-watch registers, written from an inn three days down the road, in a hand the registrars knew well, recommending an account of the gate in terms not far from Osric’s.

It is undated. It may have been written before the house’s account came down the mountain, or after, or in the same week. Nobody has ever been able to establish which, and Brother Tomas did not know it existed.

---

They brought them up at dusk, on hurdles, through the wicket in the left-hand leaf, which was opened for exactly as long as it took and then shut again.

And they carried them into the cold room off the listening hall, and set them down, and went out.

There were four cloths for nine bodies. Fen boiled them between each use on the small fire. The work lasted past midnight.

---

Sela came down at full dark.

She came down the passage with her hands in her sleeves and she stood in the doorway of the cold room, and there was one lamp, and a bucket, and a young man in a leather apron with his sleeves rolled up, working his way along the row.

He did not look up.

He had washed four bodies in that room in the spring and he had done it alone. There were nine of them now and there was still only him, because the dead of House Whitehart go to the man who keeps the niches, and because nobody in that house was permitted to speak to him, and you cannot ask a man for help if you cannot say his name.

Sela stood in the doorway.

She had promised to help him.

She did not say his name.

She took off her cloak and hung it on the peg. She rolled up her sleeves. She picked up the second cloth from the shelf, and she went to the far end of the row, and she started at the other end and worked towards him.

Fen did not look up.

He did not stop, and he did not speak, and he did not send her away.

They worked from opposite ends of the row. After midnight they met at the last body.

Fen rinsed the cloth. Sela put out the lamp.

---

## Chapter Twenty-One
### The Keeper

They buried them on the third day, and the valley did not come.

Merta came alone with Col’s coat over her arm.

A novice met her below the elbow and said, “At least they have names.”

The girl meant Harl and the others. She meant that the graves would be marked, that prayers would be said, that no mother would stand sixteen winters with a coat and no place to put it.

Merta stopped.

“Col had a name,” she said.

The novice went white. “I meant in the book.”

“I know what you meant.”

Merta continued up the path. The girl remained by the boundary, crying into both hands.

At the burial ground, someone had set a stool for Merta. She moved it aside and stood. During the first prayer she folded Col’s coat once, then unfolded it because the sleeve had gone under. During the second she folded it again.

Sela stood near her but not close enough to touch.

“He hated boiled turnip,” Merta said.

Sela looked at her.

“Col. He would hide it under the bread and think I could not see. Seventeen years old and hiding turnip like a six-year-old.”

The priest began Harl’s name.

Merta held the coat against her chest. “Everyone remembers the goats. They always tell the goat story. He hated turnip too.”

Sela nodded.

There was nothing useful to say. For once, no one tried.

After the prayers, Merta carried the coat back down.

At the elbow she discovered that someone had placed a small loaf inside it. The bread had flattened against the lining and left flour on the sleeve.

She turned around. The burial party was already breaking apart above her. A novice carried the stool away. Wilda spoke to the priest. Sela stood beside Harl’s grave with her hands empty.

Merta could have taken the loaf back. She could have asked who had put it there and refused the kindness properly.

Instead she sat on the boundary stone and ate half. The bread was dry and had too much salt. She wrapped the rest in the coat.

A man coming up from the valley stopped when he saw her.

“Is that his?” he asked.

Merta looked down at the worn elbow patch.

“Yes.”

The man removed his cap. He did not know whether she meant Col or the coat. He stood there until she told him to move because he was blocking the path.

Merta walked home with the bread still inside the sleeve. She found it that evening when she hung the coat beside the door.

She ate the rest cold.

The deaths did not split the house. The bodies did. Nine dead lay in the cold room while the valley counted twelve.

The valley did not come.

Nine people — twelve; the number was already a war of its own — had died on that path in a quarter of an hour, and their people were four hundred feet below in the wet, and they did not come up.

Fen knew why both numbers held. He had washed the bodies the house received.

They did not come up because to come up was to stand on the path where it happened. And to stand on the path was to be at the gate again. And nobody, on either side of that wall, was ever going to be able to stand at that gate again for as long as they lived.

So House Whitehart buried the valley’s dead, alone, in the rain, and said the office over them, and nobody watched.

He had not been spoken to in five months.

It should be put plainly, and there is no record of it anywhere.

Not by Ansel, who left bread on the table. Not by Sinnet in the kitchen, who used to call him a lazy article every morning for five years and now looked at the pot. Not by the novices, who had been fifteen when he was fifteen. Not by Sela, who had promised, and who kept every promise she ever made, and who came into the cold room in the night once and washed nine bodies beside him from the other end and never said a word.

Within three weeks the jokes had returned. Some were old. Two failed and he used them again anyway. He worked, ate and slept when sleep came.

He was twenty-four years old and he did not mind at all.

He minded so much that he had begun, in the last month, to talk to the wall.

He worked the rows and said each name inwardly. At the gap in the third row he once added his own. Then he stood with the rag in his hand.

---

Sister Wilda came into the listening room on the fourth night after the burial, at the hour when nobody came anywhere, and she stood in the arch and watched him work for a long time before she said anything.

He knew she was there. He had known for a quarter of an hour. He went on working, because there was nothing else to do and because he had learned, in five months, that being looked at was not the same as being seen and it was safer to assume the first.

Then she said:

“Fen.”

---

He dropped the rag.

He picked it up. His hands did not work properly. He got it, and lost it, and got it, and did not look round, and his heart was going in a way that had nothing to do with fright and he could not have named it if you had held a knife on him.

“You’ll want to be careful, Sister,” he said. “There’s a rule.”

“I know the rule. I wrote half of it.”

“Then—”

“Turn round,” said Wilda. “I am going to say a thing to you, and I have been sixteen years deciding to say it, and I will not say it to a man’s back.”

---

He turned round.

Wilda looked smaller than she had at the gate. Her sleeves hung loose at the wrists.

She did not look like that now.

“Sit down,” she said, and sat down herself, on the flags, in her habit, on the cold stone, with her back against the wall of the dead.

Fen sat.

---

“Twenty-four years old,” said Wilda.

“Twenty-five in the spring.”

“You had the lung-fever at twenty. I sat with you two nights.” She was looking at her hands. “I have never mentioned it and you have never mentioned it and I am mentioning it now because I want you to understand that I am not a monster, and that I know I am about to sound like one, and I would rather you had the two facts at the same time and did what you liked with them.”

“Sister—”

“I walked Col up the mountain,” said Sister Wilda.

---

The wind came in through the arch.

“I know,” said Fen carefully. “Everyone knows. You’re the keeper of the rite. The keeper walks them. That’s the—”

“He fell,” said Wilda.

---

Wilda’s eyes were dry. Her hands stayed flat on her knees.

“Halfway up. Above the second bend.” Wilda looked at the floor. “He had not eaten in two days. He went to his knees and would not rise. I said the strengthening office.”

Her thumb moved once across the seam of her habit.

“He looked at me and said, *I don’t want to.*”

She stopped.

“He did not beg. He said, *I don’t want to.* Like a child refusing bed. Then he put his face in the snow.”

---

“So I picked him up,” said Sister Wilda.

Fen did not move.

“I lifted him.” Wilda’s hands remained on her knees. “He was light. I carried him across the shoulder instead of up. There is a path beyond the neck, down through the eastern trees.”

“You—”

“I left him beneath the last trees with my cloak and two days’ bread. I told the house the cloak was lost.” She paused. “I told Col never to return. If he did, I would hang and his mother’s house would answer for it.”

She looked up.

“I told him to go. I watched until the snow hid him. I do not know whether he lived a week or has grandchildren somewhere east.”

---

Fen sat on the flags with his hands on his knees.

He could hear his own blood.

“And then you came down,” he said, “and told the house he walked into the snow.”

“Yes.”

“And Merta—”

“Yes,” said Wilda.

“*Sixteen years.*”

“Sixteen years,” Wilda agreed. “Sixteen winters standing below the elbow with his coat over her arm, and I have come down that path every one of those years and told her that she may not say his name, and I have said it in a voice with no kindness in it, on purpose, because if I had ever once been kind to her she would have looked at me properly and she would have *seen it*.”

---

“You could have told her.” Fen was shaking. He heard it in his own voice and could not stop it. “Sister, you could have told her *once*, in sixteen years, on any night, in the dark—”

“And what would she have done?”

“She’d have been *happy!*”

“She would have gone looking for him,” said Sister Wilda.

The candle in the nearest niche went sideways and steadied.

“Merta would have followed. She would have asked in every village. Others would have followed her questions.”

She let it sit.

“And then, Fen, tell me what happens to the next one.”

---

He did not answer.

“The rite holds because no one returns. A Tester who writes red believes the result will be death. If return becomes possible, red becomes exile. That is another judgment.”

She leaned forward.

“Now make it survivable,” she said. “Make it a thing where a merciful keeper might carry you over the shoulder and set you down under the trees. What happens? The Tester’s hand gets *lighter*. Of course it does. He is a good man and he loves the child and he tells himself: *well, and Wilda carried the last one, didn’t she.* And so he writes red where he would once have written nothing, because the red has stopped being a killing.” Her voice did not rise. “And then a Mother comes who is not fond of the poor, and she appoints keepers who do not carry. And now you have a rite that kills whoever the keeper decides, and there is no ledger for it and no page and no witness, and it will be done in the snow by one person, alone, for four hundred years.”

She sat back.

“I have searched for an honest answer every winter.” Wilda looked at her hands. “I have not found one.”

---

“You let her *grieve*,” said Fen.

“Yes.”

“You let a woman grieve a living son for sixteen years.”

“He may not be living.”

“You let her grieve a *maybe* for sixteen years!”

“Yes,” said Sister Wilda. “I did. And I chose it in the first year, and then I chose it again in the second, and then in the third, and I have chosen it sixteen separate times, on sixteen separate nights, standing on that path in the snow four feet from her face.”

She looked at him steadily.

“One sin?” Wilda said. “No. I did it sixteen times. The first winter I could hardly speak. The last time I finished before she did.”

---

Fen got up.

He walked to the arch and stood in it with his back to her and the wind coming in off the valley, and he was twenty-four years old and he did not know what to do with his hands.

“Why are you telling *me*?”

“You know why.”

“Say it.”

“Because you cannot be believed,” said Sister Wilda.

---

She said it flatly, without cruelty, in the voice she used for law.

“You cannot swear or stand witness. No court, house or griffe must hear you.” Wilda stood slowly. “If you repeat this, the law gives your words nowhere to land.”

She smoothed her habit.

“I needed to say it once before I died. You were the person I could use.”

---

Fen laughed.

It came out of him wrong, and hard, and he heard it hit the stone and come back.

“That’s *beautiful*,” he said. “That is genuinely beautiful. You’ve been sitting on the biggest thing in this house for sixteen years and you’ve been waiting for a man with no legal existence to grow up so you could put it down on him.” He turned round. “I’m a *hole*, Sister. That’s what you came for. You wanted to say it into a hole.”

“Yes,” said Sister Wilda.

“And you’re not even ashamed.”

“I am *deeply* ashamed,” she said, “and it changed nothing, and I came anyway, and that is what shame is worth, and you will find that out yourself one day if you have not already.”

---

She went to the door.

And then she stopped, with her hand on the stone, and stood there for a long moment with her back to him.

“He went east,” said Wilda. “Under the last trees on the far side of the neck, where the path drops into the pines. There is a stream about a mile down and after that I do not know, and I have never gone one step past those trees in sixteen years, and I could walk you to the spot in the dark.”

She turned her head.

“And I have just given it to you,” she said, “which means it is no longer mine, and I find that I am not able to tell you what to do with it, which is a thing I have never once failed to do in my life.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“Do what you like,” said Sister Wilda.

And she went out.

---

Fen stood in the listening room for a long time.

The wall of the dead was in front of him. Three hundred-odd names, every one of which he knew, and not one of which had ever been permitted to know his.

He had spent his whole life wanting to be worth telling something to.

And a woman had finally, finally come to him with the greatest secret in House Whitehart and had laid it in his hands — and she had done it *precisely because he was nothing*, because he was the one place in the Banner-lands where a truth could be set down and be certain never to be picked up.

He was the hole they had made. And now they were using it.

He waited for himself to want it.

He stood in the listening room, at the wall, in the dark, with the biggest thing he had ever held in his two hands, and he waited to want it —

— and found, with a sort of exhausted astonishment, that he did not.

There was only a woman standing on a path in the snow with a coat over her arm.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Two
### I Don’t Want To

He got out through the wicket, and nobody stopped him.

The guard on the wicket was a novice who had once borrowed Fen’s knife and returned it sharpened badly.

The novice lifted the bar. He looked at the list, then at Fen.

“You are not—”

“I know. It saves ink.”

The novice did not smile. He had been on the gate during the deaths and had not slept well since.

Fen ducked through. The candle-box struck the frame.

“You are taking that?” the novice asked.

Fen looked down at the box. He had brought it without deciding to. Inside were six candles, two rags and the bone knife.

“The valley is dark,” he said.

It sounded almost noble. Fen disliked it at once.

“I forgot to put it down,” he added.

Outside the wall, cold found the sweat under his shirt. The road smelled of ash, wet wool and the trenches. He had eaten nothing since morning. At the first camp a woman offered him broth, then asked his name for the prayer list.

Fen told her he was called Harl.

The lie came out before he could make it funny.

She wrote it on a strip of cloth and tied it above the fire.

Fen carried the bowl away. He drank behind a cart where he could not see the cloth move.

Nobody stopped him because there was no rule about him. The gate was shut by the Mother’s order and the wicket was barred and there were two novices on it day and night, and they had a list of who might pass and on whose authority, and Fen was not on the list, and Fen was not on any list, and had never been on any list.

So when he came down to the wicket at first light with his coat on and a bag over his shoulder, the two novices looked at him, and looked at each other, and did not know what to do.

They could not ask him where he was going, because they were not permitted to speak to him.

And in the end one of them — a boy of sixteen who had known him all his life and had stopped saying his name in the spring — lifted the bar and stood back, and looked at the ground.

Fen went out.

At the gate no one stopped him. He carried that fact down the mountain and found no joke for it.

The valley was worse at ground level.

From the gallery, four thousand people had looked like fires and roofs. On the road they were coughs, wet blankets, bad shoes and the smell of boiled bark.

He had not.

From the gallery they looked merely still. Down on the flat, they were dying. Nobody was screaming; nobody was rioting; the fight at the gate had gone out of them the day they carried their dead away and the house did not open, and what was left was four thousand people sitting in the wet, slowly, in an orderly fashion, running out.

They did not look at him.

He walked through the camps with his bag. No one looked at him. For once this was useful.

---

Merta’s kitchen was as cold and clean and poor as it had been in the spring.

The fire was very small. There was one chair with a cushion and nobody sat in it.

She opened the door and looked at him and she knew him at once.

“You’re the one that brought the Herald,” said Merta.

“Yes.”

“Come in, then,” she said. “You’ll be the first.”

---

He did not sit down.

He stood in the middle of her floor with his cap in his hands, exactly as he had stood in the spring, and he could not begin, and he stood there so long that she went and put the kettle on the fire to give him something to do with the silence.

“Say it,” said Merta, with her back to him.

“I told the Herald that Sela was frightened.”

She did not turn round.

“I told him she didn’t believe her own words. I told him what she’d said in the hall and what she’d said to me at the wall and I told him she wasn’t sure of anything and had never been sure of anything, and I told him because he asked me, and because he’d used my name.” The words came out flat and fast and he did not stop for breath because if he stopped he would not start again. “And he let two stories go about the valley. Hers is law, and hers is nothing. He let them both run, because he wanted to see which one people would argue with. And I gave him the second one.”

The kettle ticked.

“And Corr priced the route,” said Fen. “And Harl went up. And you know the rest.”

---

Merta turned round.

She looked at him for a long moment with her arms folded.

“Nobody killed him,” said Fen. “I’ve been over it every night for four months. I can lay it out for you like a length of rope. Sela said the ground was wet and she was *right*. Ivet made it holy and she meant *well*. Corr took the bookings and she’d have starved without them. Harl was a proud man and he’d taken money. The spring came early. There is not one person in that chain who did an evil thing.” He looked up. “And I’m in it. I’m the third link. And I did it for a seat at a table.”

“Why are you telling me this?”

“Because I’m about to tell you something else,” said Fen, “and you’re going to want to know what sort of man is telling it to you.”

---

He told her about Col.

He told it plainly and in order and he did not decorate it, and he watched a woman of fifty-two stand in the middle of her own kitchen and go completely still.

The rite. The high line. The second bend, above where it steepens. The boy going down on his knees in the snow and not getting up. Sister Wilda standing over him, saying the strengthening words.

And Sister Wilda picking him up.

And carrying him — not up, but *across*, over the shoulder, over the neck, down into the pines on the far side where the path drops and there is a stream a mile down, and setting him under the last trees with her own cloak and two days’ bread, and telling him that if he ever came back she would hang and his mother’s house would be pulled down.

And watching him go east.

And coming down alone. And saying he walked into the snow.

And doing it again the next winter, on the path, four feet from Merta’s face.

And the winter after that.

Sixteen times.

---

Merta did not move for a long time.

Then she said, in a voice with nothing in it at all:

“And why should I believe you?”

---

“You shouldn’t,” said Fen.

She looked up.

“I mean it. Don’t.” He turned his cap over in his hands. “There is no reason on this earth to believe me and I have thought about it the whole way down and there is nothing — nothing at all — that I can give you. I’m not in the book. I can’t swear. I can’t stand witness. If you go up that mountain and say the candle-tender told you this, they will say that a nameless man told you a story, and they will be *right*, and it will be entered nowhere and heard by no one.”

The fire moved.

“And Sister Wilda will deny it,” said Fen. “She’ll do it in front of the whole house, in that flat voice, and she’ll do it well, and nobody will doubt her for a heartbeat, because she is the keeper of the rite and I am a hole in the floor. And she chose me for exactly that reason. She *said* so. She sat on the flags in the listening room and told me to my face that the only reason she could say it out loud was that I was the one place in the Banner-lands where a truth could be set down and be certain never to be picked up.”

He stopped.

“So no,” he said. “You shouldn’t believe me. I’ve told you what I am. I sold a frightened girl to a man in a good coat for the sound of my own name. I’m the worst person you’ll speak to this year and I’ve just proved it to you myself so that you’d have it in front of you.”

He looked at her.

“I came anyway,” said Fen.

---

“What do you want?” said Merta.

“Nothing.”

“Everybody wants something.”

“Yes,” Fen agreed. “They do. And I’ve wanted things my whole life and I’ve never once been able to get them without selling somebody, and I sold everyone I had, and here I am.” He put his cap on. “And I would like you to understand that I know exactly how this looks, and I have no way of making it look any different, and I would not believe me either.”

---

Merta came across the kitchen.

She stopped in front of him and she looked up into his face — she was a head shorter than him; he had not noticed that in the spring — and she said, very quietly:

“What did he say?”

“What?”

“When she stood over him,” said Merta. “In the snow. Above the second bend. When he went down on his knees and would not get up and she said the strengthening words to him.” Her hands had come up and she had taken hold of the front of his coat and she did not seem to know she had done it. “You said he said something. What did he say?”

---

And Fen — who could have said anything, who could have made something beautiful, who had a tongue on him that could have taken that woman apart and put her back together in whatever shape he liked —

— told her.

“He said, *I don’t want to.*”

---

The kettle went on ticking.

“Wilda said he did not beg. He did not ask for you. He said, *I don’t want to.* Then he put his face in the snow.”

---

Merta let go of his coat.

She went across the kitchen and put her hands flat on the table and stood there, bent over it, with her head down.

She was not making any sound at all.

“That’s how he said it,” she said.

“I know.”

“That’s *exactly* how he said it. That is how that boy said no to me for seventeen years.” Her hands were flat on the wood. “*I don’t want to.* Not *I won’t.* Never *I won’t.* He hadn’t the spine for it and I used to be ashamed of it and I used to tell him a man says *I won’t* —”

She stopped.

---

Fen stood in the middle of the kitchen and did not go to her, because he was a man who was not permitted to be spoken to, and because he did not know how, and because there was nothing in the world he could have done that would not have made it worse.

He stood there for what felt like a very long time.

And then Merta straightened up.

Merta wiped her face once with her palm.

And she said:

“You’ve taken it off me.”

---

“What?”

“My grief.” She turned round. Her eyes were red and her voice was steady. “Do you understand what you have done? I have had one thing for sixteen years. *One.* I have had a dead son, and I have known where he died, and I have known who killed him, and I have stood on a stone in the snow every winter of my life and held his coat and been *certain*.”

She was not shouting.

“And you have walked into my kitchen,” said Merta, “and you have taken my certainty away, and you have given me a *maybe*, and you cannot prove it, and nobody will ever be able to prove it, and I will be dead in twenty years and I will never know.”

She stood in the middle of her cold clean kitchen with her hands at her sides.

“I can hold a grief,” she said. “I’ve held one for sixteen years and I am *good* at it. Nobody in the Light Heights is better.” Her voice cracked, once, and she put it back. “You cannot hold a maybe. There’s nothing to hold. It just goes on and on and on.”

---

Fen shut his eyes.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“I know. You were sorry in the spring too.” Merta took the kettle off the fire. “Sit down.”

---

He sat down.

She put a cup in front of him because that is what you do, and she sat down opposite him in a hard chair, not the one with the cushion, and they sat in her kitchen for a while with the fire going down and the smoke of four thousand cooking fires coming in under the door.

“Will you go east?” said Fen.

“I don’t know.”

“She could take you to the spot. She said she could walk it in the dark—”

“I don’t *know*,” said Merta.

She turned the cup around.

“I’m fifty-two,” she said. “I have a house that leaks and no money and four thousand people camped on the road between me and everything else in the world. And if I go east, and I look, and I find nothing — and I will find nothing, boy, there is no version of this where a woman of fifty-two goes over a mountain and finds a man who left sixteen years ago — then I will have spent the rest of my life *looking*, and I will die on a road somewhere, and it will have been for a story told to me by a man who cannot be believed.”

She drank.

“And if I don’t go,” she said, “then I sit in this house until I die, knowing that he might have been alive, and that I didn’t.”

She set the cup down.

“That’s what you’ve given me,” said Merta. “That’s the gift. Thank you.”

---

He got up to go, eventually.

He stood in the doorway with the cold coming in and the road below full of people who were not going to see the spring, and he could not think of one single thing to say, and he had been able to think of something to say every day of his life since he was nine years old.

“Boy,” said Merta.

He stopped.

She was sitting at the table with her hands around a cold cup and she was looking at the fire.

“You had nothing to gain by it,” she said.

“No.”

“Nothing at all. It costs you everything and it buys you nothing and I can’t even *tell* anyone, because if I tell anyone they’ll ask who told me and I’ll say the candle-tender and they’ll laugh.” She did not look up. “You came down a mountain through four thousand starving people to hand a woman a truth that no one will ever believe, and you knew it before you set out, and you did it anyway.”

The fire settled.

“Fen,” said Merta.

---

He stood in the doorway of a cold kitchen in a dying valley and did not move.

Nobody had said it in five months.

He had gone along the wall in the dark saying three hundred names that were not his. He had eaten bread put down on a table by a woman who looked at the table. He had rebuilt himself out of nothing three separate times and had been very funny about it and had told himself that he did not mind.

Merta looked up.

“Fen,” she said again. “That’s your name, isn’t it.”

“Yes,” he said. “It is.”

“Right,” said Merta. “Go home, Fen. It’s getting dark.”

---

He went up the mountain in the dark, through the camps, past four thousand people who did not look at him, and up the path, and past the elbow, and past the stone.

And in through the wicket, which a boy of sixteen opened for him without a word and closed behind him.

And he went to the listening room, and lit a candle, and did the north row, which did not need doing.

He did not sell it then.

He was offered a good deal for it in the end, and there is no record of what he did, because there was no one to keep one.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Three
### The Carts from Duskport

The carts came on the fourteenth day after the gate.

Nine good carts came first, escorted by six riders. Four more followed, then a fifth string gathered somewhere on the road.

They were full of grain.

The first cart carried coastal barley in blue-stitched sacks. The next held pale Ironvale rye sealed with wax marks no one in the valley could read. One load smelled of salt and ship rope. Another had crossed the Ashbourne roads and arrived with red dust under the axles despite twelve days of rain.

The drivers spoke in three accents. Two wore short Stormrider coats cut for saddles. A woman from the western road had a Stonebear oath-ring tied to her belt rather than worn on her hand. No one explained why.

Unloading took all day.

Ansel weighed the first ten sacks and accused Duskport of counting the stitching as grain. Nella ordered every sack reweighed. By the fourth, Ansel admitted the weights were honest. He continued weighing them.

Sela joined the line from cart to store. The sacks were heavier than feed sacks and cut the shoulder in a new place. A Duskport driver tried to take one from her.

“I can carry it.”

“I know. I need the line to move.”

He shifted the sack to the next person without waiting for reverence. Sela liked him immediately and did not know his name.

A wheel sank near the gate. Six people pushed. Ottar brought boards. Nella took off her coat, put one shoulder against the cart and ruined a good sleeve in the mud.

When the wheel rose, the crowd made no sign. They were already watching the grain.

By dusk the yard held broken cord, spilled kernels and three foreign coins nobody could spend. Ivet found one stamped with a ship and asked whether it meant the sea had sent food.

Nella heard her.

“It means someone charged for crossing it,” she said.

From the gallery Sela watched grain move through the camps. The sound that followed the carts was low and wide. She had never heard four thousand people make it.

No one cheered.

It was the sound people make when they have been promised something.

The house came out onto the wall to look, and the house did its sums.

“Twelve days from Duskport,” said Steward Ansel, “with loaded carts, at that time of year, over the ford.”

“Twelve,” Osric agreed.

“And we shut the gate fourteen days ago.” Ansel turned his tally-stick over in his hands. “So she was on the road with a loaded string before we shut it.”

Nobody said anything.

“She was on the road *before*,” said Ansel, “which means somebody in this valley or somebody on that mountain sat down two or three weeks before the gate was shut, and worked out that it was going to be shut, and got word to Duskport.”

He put the stick away.

“And that is a very great deal of money,” said Steward Ansel, “for a piece of arithmetic that any one of us could have done.”

---

Merta said it was him.

She said it in the open, at the ford, standing on a cart, to about a hundred and fifty people, and she was not shouting and she did not weep and she was entirely calm, which is what made it survive.

She said: *who did the sums? Who has done the sums in this valley for thirty years? Who told the Mother the number that shut that gate, and then a trader came up the road with twelve days of loaded carts behind her and a fortnight of warning in her pocket, and there is one man on that mountain who knew the number before anybody, and he is the one man on that mountain who has never gone short.*

And she said: *ask him what he was paid.*

None of it was untrue. Every fact in it was a fact. Somebody in the Bell of Silence had sold Duskport the arithmetic three weeks before the gate closed, and it was worth a very great deal of money, and the house never found out who, and no account of that year has ever been able to say.

Ansel had not arranged the carts.

Ansel had never been paid for anything but work. He fed Fen from house stores and kept no private purse. He could move grain inside the Bell, not summon it across half a realm.

The valley believed her.

Of course it did. She was Merta. She had stood on a stone in the snow for sixteen years and never once been bought, and she was the only person in the Light Heights who had never told anybody a comfortable thing, and when a woman like that stands on a cart and names a man, the naming *sticks* — and it stuck, and it is still stuck, and it is in two songs.

Ansel did not defend himself. He was asked, once, in the yard, by Brother Osric, and he said, “No,” and went back to his stores.

And then he stopped.

That is the part nobody in the valley ever put together, and it is the only part that killed anybody: Steward Ansel stopped taking bread out of the house’s stores for the four families in the low pasture, because a man who has been named for a thief cannot be seen carrying grain out of a gate.

He stopped in the third week of the cold.

Two of those families got through the winter. The Ordins did not lose anybody, because they had a cousin at the mill.

The Wicks lost the old woman in the second month, and they lost her of the cold and not of hunger, and the physician said so, and the physician was right, and she had been eating Steward Ansel’s bread for nine years and had never known it.

Nobody carried any of this up to Merta.

No account records anyone carrying the correction to Merta.

She went on saying the old woman’s name at the ford, in spring and at a neighbour’s burial. No source records her taking it back.

The songs kept the name.
---

Her name was Nella, a trade-widow of Duskport. During the whole winter she never said *Ravenshade*.

She was forty-six and extremely pleasant. She wore good plain clothes and she had a laugh you wanted to hear again, and within two days she knew the name of every family on the flat by the ford, and within four she knew which of them had a boy in Duskport and which of them had a grudge against Bryn Corr and which of them had buried a child above the water.

And she sold the grain.

The loads had crossed half the realm. Some sacks came through Ironvale, some by the Ashbourne roads, and the last three had been bought from a Phoenix factor. Nella’s board kept the prices separate. The people in line did not.

One driver said Ashen Crown men had been asking grain prices at the southern crossings. Nella told him to unload.

---

Nella charged Duskport price, carriage and a margin she wrote on the cart-board where anyone could see it.

“Check the figures when the next trader comes,” she called from the mud. “I hope one does. I have no wish to be the only cart on this road.”

And four thousand people looked at a woman who could have robbed them and did not, and something happened in that valley that Sela did not have a word for at the time and has never really found one for since.

They did not just buy from her.

---

“It’s the cleverest thing I have ever seen done,” said Brother Osric, and he was pale, and he meant it. “She has spent perhaps a third of what she could have taken, and she has bought the whole valley.”

“She’s *fed* them,” said Sela.

“Yes,” said Osric. “She has. That’s what makes it work. That is the entire art of it and I do not think you understand yet how frightened you should be.”

---

Because there were a great many people in that valley who could not pay.

Most could not pay. They had sold beasts and tools during the summer and spent the money on bread.

Nella sat on the tailboard for six days. She gave out grain until dark and entered every name in her book.

Sela went and stood at the back of the line and watched her do it, in a plain cloak, with her hood up, and nobody knew her.

*“I’ve nothing,”* said a man from the coast, with three children behind him.

And the man looked at the book, and at his three children, and at the woman who had just saved their lives.

*“I’d not say no,”* he said.

---

The woman who came after him was called Halla, and she was about thirty, and she had a burn up the inside of her right forearm from a kitchen she had worked in before she walked forty miles to look at a mountain.

She stood at the tailboard with her hands behind her back and said she had nothing.

“I know,” said Nella.

“I have nothing to trade.” Halla spoke too loudly; the sentence had been refused elsewhere. “No beast, no tool, no coin. Only a blanket.”

Nella did not look up from the book.

“Sit down,” she said.

“I’m not—”

“Sit *down*, Halla.” She wrote something. “You’ll want to be sitting, because I’m going to give you grain for four months and I’m going to charge you nothing for it, and you’re going to want to argue with me about it, and I have thirty more behind you and I’d rather do it seated.”

Halla sat down on the tailboard.

Halla began to cry. Nella kept writing until she had finished the line.

“Your name in the book,” said Nella at last. “That’s all. No interest. No day. No sum.”

“And what do you want?”

“Nothing. One day I may want something.” Nella turned the book round and showed her the line, in a clear steady hand. “And you may say no.”

Halla looked at it.

She could not read. Almost nobody in that valley could. She looked at the shape of her own name in another woman’s writing and put her finger on it, and she looked at it for a long time.

“I’d not say no,” she said.

“No,” said Nella. “Well. There it is.”

Halla went back to her shelter with four months of grain, and she did not go to the barn behind the mill, and she did not go to Ordin at the ford, and neither of them ever saw a mark of what they had been owed.

She was still on the flat nine years later, and when a woman from Duskport came up that road in a bad year and asked her for something, she gave it, and she did not ask what it was for.

---

Nobody said no.

Not that year, and there is no entry anywhere in Nella’s book, in any hand, showing that anybody ever did.

Sela stood at the back of the line with her hood up and watched Nella work.

Children ate. The old survived the flat. Nella paid to mend the water.

Her book also held every unpaid name.

Whitehart had no clean place to put both entries.

---

A Stormrider rider came up with the fourth string. He belonged to none of Nella’s companies, and nobody knew why he was there.

Roan was twenty-nine, three months out of the Expanses, and spoke of the journey as a delay on the road to somewhere else.

He said *we* almost all the time.

“We came up by the low ford,” he said, in the kitchen, with a bowl in his hands. “We’d have come the high road but we’d word the water was bad. We’ve been at Crown-watch and we’ve been at the Hub and we’re going west, and we’ll be at the sea before the ice, if we’re lucky, which we won’t be.”

“You keep saying *we*,” said one of the kitchen girls.

Roan looked genuinely puzzled.

“There’s a good many of us,” he said.

There was not. He was alone. He had been alone for three months and had ridden the length of the Banner-lands by himself, and he did not appear to have noticed.

---

He carried news without knowing anyone in the room needed it.

He mentioned it without emphasis.

“The Binder’s dying,” said Roan.

The kitchen did not stop. Somebody went on chopping. Sela, sitting in the corner where she sat now, went very still.

“Garron?”

“Mm.”

“He’s been dying for two years,” said Ansel, from the door. “He was dying when the wool came up in the spring.”

“Aye, and he’s better at it now.” Roan wiped his bowl out with his bread. “We heard it three ways coming west, and I’ll give you all three and you can have whichever you like, for I’ve no notion which is true and I’ve stopped caring.”

He held up a finger.

“First: he’s dead already and they’ve not said, because there’s no man to say it to. Second: he’s alive and he’s stopped speaking, and he’s been silent since the spring, and they carry him out in the mornings and set him where he can see the horses.” Another finger. “Third: he’s alive and he’s talking a great deal, and none of it makes sense, and the ones who hear it write it down.”

He mopped the bowl.

“And they’re all true,” said Roan, “because that’s how it is with him.”

---

“That’s not — ” Sela heard herself say it, and stopped, and then went on, because she could not help it. “They can’t all be true. He’s either dead or he isn’t.”

Roan looked at her for the first time.

He did not know who Sela was. She nearly wept from the ease of it.

“You have not left this valley,” he said.

“No.”

“There is no single word for it. No crown, no writ. Garron binds disputes. People obey because the next dispute may be theirs.”

He picked at a tooth.

“There’s no clerk waiting to close a book on him,” said Roan. “People take Garron their quarrels. While they keep doing that, he may as well be alive.”

He set the clean bowl down.

“When somebody rides in with enough men, we’ll learn what they call it.”

---

Steward Ansel had gone very still by the door.

Brother Osric, who had come in for something and stopped in the passage, did not move at all.

Tomas sat at the end of the table with untouched food and both hands flat on the board.

“Every dispute in the realm.”

“Aye.”

“Boundaries. Debts. Marriages used as treaties.”

“Aye.”

“And there is no court.”

Roan shrugged.

“There’s him,” he said. “There’s always been him. That’s why nobody built one.”

---

Nella of Duskport came up to the house on the eighth day, and she came to see Sela, and she did not pretend otherwise.

They sat in the yard, because Sela would not sit anywhere else, and it was cold.

“I’ll not ask you for an account,” said Nella. “You’ve had a herald at you for ten months and I’d not insult you with a worse one.”

Sela said nothing.

“I’ve a proposition instead.” Nella folded her hands. She had a good, warm, entirely unhurried way of sitting. “Come to Duskport. I’ll take you down with the empty carts. You’d have a room in my house and nobody in Duskport gives a damn about a Called; we’ve had four of them in my lifetime and two were frauds and one married a fishmonger.”

“And what do you want?”

“Nothing.”

“There’s a book,” said Sela. “There’s a book on your cart with four thousand names in it.”

Nella smiled.

“There is,” she said. “And I’d not put you in it. No name, no sum, no day, no future favour. Nothing at all.” She spread her hands. “You’d owe me *nothing*, and I would never ask you for anything, and I would say so in front of your Mother and your Tester and anyone else you liked, and I would mean it, and I would keep it.”

The wind came across the yard.

Sela looked at the woman from Duskport, and at the book on the cart with four thousand names in it, and thought that a debt you cannot see is still a debt.

She thought about saying so.

She thought about how it would sound, and what would be built on it, and who would repeat it, and how it would be in three villages by the thaw.

And she closed her mouth.

---

Nella waited. She did not fill the silence for Sela.

Sela sat in the cold with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the mountain and said nothing at all.

And after a while, Nella began to laugh.

“Ah,” she said. “*Ah.* Somebody’s taught you.”

She got up.

“Good,” she said. “Keep that.”

She dusted her palms against her skirts.

“The offer stands. I won’t ask twice. Your Mother would have filled that silence for me.”

And she went off across the yard, whistling.

---

Sela sat in the yard for a long while after that.

A man from Nella’s party came out carrying a basin of dirty water. Nella had called him Ottar once, when she wanted a crate moved. He was thirty-eight. He drank from his own cup and waited until everyone else had been served. No one asked why.

“Are you cold?” Sela said.

Ottar stopped.

“If it please you — I am not cold.”

“You can just say it.”

His mouth opened. He stood with the basin in both hands until the water trembled against the rim.

“I am not cold,” he said at last.

At his belt hung a short braid of horsehair, worn smooth and mended twice. Sela had seen a hundred like it among riders and traders.

Ottar crossed the yard, emptied the basin and went inside.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Four
### That’s How You Know

On the flat they had begun calling themselves the ones who wait.

No one knew who first said it. They kept a fire below the elbow and took turns feeding it through the night. They were not waiting for the gate, or grain, or a sign. Nella’s carts had given them food.

They waited because they had already waited a year.

Ivet went down twice a week to pray with them.

The people at the fire kept a list of turns on a board split from a flour crate. Most could not read it. A boy marked each name with a different notch and remembered which notch belonged to whom.

They argued about wood every evening. The nearest slopes had been stripped. Green branches smoked and made the children cough. Someone began walking three miles for fallen pine, and within a week the walk had become a duty with rules, substitutes and accusations of theft.

Corrin went on the third day.

He returned dragging a crooked branch behind him, his bad leg stiff in the cold. Nobody thanked him. He spent an hour cutting the branch into lengths and another hour complaining that the fire had been laid badly.

“You say she is false,” a woman told him. “Why do you stay?”

“Because my leg is broken and the road home is forty miles.”

His leg was not broken then. It was only bad.

He rebuilt the edge of the fire with flat stones so the pot would stop tipping. The next morning Ivet found him patching the shelter above a family that had called him a liar the night before.

“You are kinder than you speak,” she said.

Corrin spat a nail into his palm. “No. Their roof drips on my blanket.”

Ivet repeated the exchange at the Bell. In her telling, even the doubter served the waiting without knowing why.

Corrin heard about that version and swore for most of an afternoon.

The shelter held through the next rain.

A man called Corrin also went to the fire. He had a bad leg, a coast voice and a habit of saying Sela was a fraud.

Sometimes he said she had killed Harl with a curse. Sometimes he said Whitehart had invented her. People had stopped answering.

One Tuesday night three young men beat him beside the fire.

They broke his good leg and his jaw. They left him breathing.

One wore Harl’s coat.

---

Ivet came to Sela the next morning.

“They say Tam was there.”

Sela was already tying her boots.

“He would not do that,” Ivet said. “He carried water all summer. He sat with the Ottard child when she was sick.”

“What have you told him?”

Ivet stopped.

“The truth.”

“Which part?”

“That Harl was warned.”

Sela pulled the knot too tight and had to start again.

“You told him I warned his father with a voice.”

“I told him you climbed through the rain and said not to go.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.” Ivet’s eyes filled. “It is better.”

Sela looked at her.

“He thought his father died for four marks a head and a horse,” Ivet said. “He could not carry that.”

“And now?”

“Now his father was warned and chose.”

Ivet said it with relief.

“He can carry that.”

Sela left before she struck her.

---

Tam sat at the waiting fire in Harl’s coat.

The sleeves covered part of his hands. He stood when Sela approached.

“Sister.”

“Don’t.”

He lowered his head.

“Sit down.”

He sat at once.

Sela remained standing until she saw what that looked like. Then she took the stone opposite him.

“Corrin.”

“He lies.”

“Did you break his leg?”

Tam watched the fire.

“Did you?”

“He said you killed my father.”

“That is not an answer.”

“Yes.”

The calm in his voice frightened her.

“I asked him to stop,” Tam said. “Many times.”

“So you broke his jaw.”

“It will mend.”

“His leg?”

Tam rubbed one thumb across the other.

“I did not want him saying it anymore.”

---

Sela had rehearsed what came next on the way down.

“I heard nothing on that hill.”

Tam raised his eyes.

“Nothing I could call a voice,” she said. “I saw wet ground. The thaw was early. Any herder could have seen it.”

He listened without interrupting.

“Your father went around the neck. I watched him. He took the safer ground and the mountain gave way under that too.”

The fire shifted between them.

“He did not die because he disobeyed me,” Sela said. “He died because the slope went.”

Tam’s face did not change.

“It would have happened if I had never spoken. It might have happened if I had never been born.”

She waited for anger.

Tam said, “You would say that.”

“What?”

“Of course you would.”

He leaned forward. His eyes were wet.

“You could have told him the dead spoke. He would have turned back.”

“That would have been a lie.”

“I know.”

“No, Tam. Listen to me.”

“I am listening.”

“You are making this into proof again.”

He shook his head with something close to wonder.

“You came down here to tell me my father died for nothing, because you think it is true. You would rather I hate you than let me believe a lie.”

“That is not—”

“That’s how you know,” Tam said.

---

A song drifted from higher on the flat.

It was one of the bad ones. Sela’s name sat awkwardly in the verse. The final line carried clearly through the cold:

*Go where you are not certain. That is where the truth begins.*

Sela had never said it. The words did not sound like her. Years later Blackcrest placed them at the head of her Chronicle entry. No surviving account names the singer who made them.

Tam was still watching her.

Anything she said would go back to the fire.

She stood.

“Don’t hurt anyone else.”

“Sister—”

“Promise me.”

“I promise.”

He took the words with both hands, as if they were something she had given him.

Sela left before he could thank her.

---

At the top of the traverse she stopped.

The Silent Mountains filled the eastern sky. Beyond the neck, Wilda’s path entered the pines. A stream ran somewhere below them.

Sela measured ground, water, shelter and days.

By the time she reached the house, she had begun to count.

---

## Chapter Twenty-Five
### The Blank Page

For four days Sela planned and told no one.

She worked. She ate with the empty space around her. In the hall she sat with her back to the arch and kept it there. At night, when the passages were clear, she went to the mouth of the listening room and faced the valley.

Harl’s knife came out of its cloth.

She counted food, weather and distance. The middle route over the neck was the easiest and would be watched. The northern cut held snow too long. The third way went past the old slip and the covered grave, then over bad rock to the east face.

She chose the third.

She needed food that would keep. The kitchen had oat cake, hard cheese and two onions beginning to soften. Sela took only what fit beneath her cloak and left coin on the shelf, though the coin had come from the house.

Ansel found it before noon.

He came to the yard carrying the two onions.

“This is poor travelling food.”

Sela kept mending the strap on her bundle.

“They are food.”

“They are water in skins. Take salt meat.”

“I did not ask.”

“No.”

He set down a wrapped piece of meat, a needle, waxed thread and a small lump of salt. None appeared on a tally.

“How much?” Sela asked.

“Nothing.”

She looked up.

Ansel frowned. “Do not make that face. Nothing is not a price. It is an absence of entry.”

He picked up the coin from the bench and pushed it into the seam of her bundle where it would not fall out.

“The third path has no shelter before the pines,” he said. “Rain tomorrow night.”

Then he went back to the stores before she could ask how he knew which path she had chosen.

The counting steadied her. That was new.

On the fifth day Tomas sent for her.

The Ledger-room shutter stood open. Cold air moved the loose corner of a page, though no paper lay in front of him. He watched her come through the door.

“You have taken Harl’s knife out of the cloth,” he said.

Sela kept her hands in her sleeves.

“You asked Ansel about the weather over the neck. Twice. You have stopped arguing.” Tomas turned his empty hands over. “Wilda calls that wisdom. I do not.”

The shutter knocked once.

“Sit down.”

---

“I’m going east,” Sela said.

“I know.”

“I’m not asking.”

“No.”

“You could tell the house. They would guard the gate and the path. I would not get four miles.”

“Yes.”

Tomas unlocked the chest and lifted out the Ledger. Its corners shone where generations of hands had avoided the middle.

He set it between them.

“Come here.”

---

Black entries passed under his hand, then red. Sela had imagined more red. On the page it looked ordinary: a name, a date, a line in the same cramped hand as all the rest.

Near the working end Tomas stopped.

There was a page with nothing on it.

A date stood at the top. The ruled line beneath it began with the clean space where a name should have been. A small brown fleck marked the lower corner, trapped in the paper when it was made.

“That is yours,” Tomas said. “The book does not know it. I do.”

Sela put both hands on the table.

“You tested me.”

“Yes.”

“You wrote something.”

“I wrote what I saw and burned it: you stopped speaking, listened outward, and the room followed. I could prove nothing more.”

His hand rested beside the empty line.

“Other events overtook the testing. Osric will ask before thaw.”

“He will want a verdict.”

“He will be right to ask. A house cannot keep an unconcluded Called forever.”

Sela looked at the empty name-space.

“Can he finish it?”

“No. A sealed page cannot be reopened. Osric cannot add your name or complete my line. If he wants a verdict, he must find you and test you again from the beginning.”

“Which he will.”

“If he finds you.”

---

“What will you write?”

“Nothing.”

Tomas closed the Ledger.

“Next month makes a year and a day. I will seal the page blank. A date. No name. The forty-second.”

Sela looked at him.

“You’re making me Unwritten.”

“Yes.”

Wind pushed through the shutter. Tomas rose to fasten it, then left it as it was.

“I know what blank does,” he said. “Fen cannot marry, inherit, swear or be addressed in the house’s voice. I called that mercy once.”

“And now?”

“Now I call it what it is.”

His thumb pressed the edge of the table.

“The first blank page was tiredness. The second was mercy. This one is deliberate.”

“Why?”

“Because either verdict becomes a weapon.”

He spoke more quickly now, as if the categories might hold if he set them out in order.

“Black, and Whitehart owns a confirmed Called. Your words become doctrine. Tam breaks a man’s jaw with the Ledger behind him. Every house that needs a saint will hunt for you.”

He moved one finger across the closed board.

“Red, and I send you to the high line on evidence I do not have.”

A second finger.

“Blank, and the Ledger remains silent. Songs will speak. Blackcrest will write. But no one can point to Whitehart’s book and say the house judged you.”

“Fen is still in the book as nothing.”

“Yes.”

“So this is poor protection.”

“I found none better.”

---

Sela sat on the stool.

Outside, someone carried a bucket along the passage. Water struck the sides in time with the steps.

“They will take your office.”

“Almost certainly.”

He spoke as if reporting weather.

“Osric will be careful, quick and honest. He believes the craft can hold.” Tomas looked at his hands. “I no longer know what that sentence means.”

“I’m not worth your office.”

“No one is. That is why this is not a bargain.”

He returned the Ledger to the chest.

“When they ask what you were, I will say I do not know. I trained my whole life to tell and failed in the case that mattered.”

“That is the right answer.”

“I think.”

The last two words came after the chest was locked.

---

Fen was at the wall.

Sela stood in the arch with her bundle and waited. He cleaned two cups before speaking.

“You will want the third way.”

“How did you know?”

“You have spent four days behaving like a person who has made a decision and would prefer the rest of us not improve it. Also, Ansel talks in his sleep.”

He scraped the cup again. The joke found nowhere to land.

“Do not take the middle path,” he said. “They will search it. Go past the slip. Keep the drop on your left and cross the bad ground.”

“How bad?”

“Bad enough that nobody sensible uses it. You should be comfortable.”

Sela did not smile.

Fen missed a strip of wax along the rim and went back for it.

“On the neck, take the east face. Find the last pines. A track runs beneath them. The stream is a mile down. Follow it.”

“And after the stream?”

“Wilda did not know. Nobody here does.”

He set the cup back into the niche.

A candle guttered two niches down. Fen went to it at once, though it had enough wax for another hour. He trimmed the wick too short and the flame shrank.

Sela watched him replace the cup.

“You could ask Tomas,” she said. “About the road after the stream.”

“Tomas knows roads in books. They do not get muddy.”

“You could ask someone from the camps.”

“And explain why?”

Fen opened the candle-box, moved three candles and closed it again.

“There was a trader from Tidebreaker who came over the east face in spring,” he said. “He complained about a broken bridge. He also complained about the soup, the beds and a woman in the valley who would not marry him. I do not know which part was true.”

“Did he say where the bridge was?”

“After the last pines. Before a village whose name he could not keep straight. He had been drinking.”

Sela tightened the strap on her bundle.

“That is something.”

“It is a drunk man’s bridge near a village he could not name.”

“Still something.”

Fen looked at the shortened flame. “You always were greedy.”

The old shape of the joke was there. It did not quite stand.

---

“Come with me,” Sela said.

Fen looked at the rag in his hand.

“No.”

“I’m asking.”

“I heard you.”

He turned with wax on his knuckles. He looked thinner than he had in spring.

“If I go, you take me. I have no papers, portion or oath anyone can enter. I become something you carry.”

“I wouldn’t treat you like that.”

“You do not know that.”

“I know myself.”

“That must be restful.”

The words came too quickly. Fen looked away.

“One day you tell me something,” he said. “Someone offers a chair and uses my name. I know what that can buy.”

Sela waited.

“You would not do that to me,” she said.

“You do not know that either.”

He put the rag down and picked it up again.

“Besides. Someone has to do the wall.”

“Someone else can clean stone.”

“No one else knows the order. If I leave, the names remain and the wall goes blind.”

He touched the nearest cup with one finger.

“I stay. I do the wall. In four years Blackcrest comes back, and we see.”

---

Sela stood in the arch with her bundle and the cold at her back.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“I know.”

“I ruined things. Harl. You. All of it.”

“Yes.”

Fen looked at her.

He could have told her where Perrin learned her fear. He could have described the table, the wine and how quickly he had answered. Sela waited, thinking he might say more.

Fen bent for the candle-box instead. One hinge had loosened. He tested it with his thumb before shouldering the weight.

“You have apologised enough. It has not improved either of us.”

The joke was almost one he had used before. Neither of them smiled.

He went past her to the door.

At the arch he stopped without turning.

“Go on, then.”

Sela passed the griffes, the listening room and the long passage. Harl’s knife hung beneath her cloak.

Then she went out into the dark.

## Chapter Twenty-Six
### Interlude: The Order She Did Not Give

Alaine heard the wicket before the bell.

She stood on the gallery in the dark with both hands on the rail. Below, the hinge lifted with the small scrape it made when someone knew how to bear its weight. The wicket opened only far enough for a body and closed again.

No lamp appeared.

No guard called out.

Alaine listened to Sela leave.

One word would have stopped her.

She did not say it.

---

The order had already formed.

Wake Wilda. Send men to the neck. Bring the girl back before the camps learned she was gone.

It was a proper order.

Three thousand people still lived on the flat. Tam and his companions kept their fire below the elbow. The songs had begun changing Sela faster than any house could answer. A vanished Called could be made into anything.

Bring her back. Keep her where she could be watched.

Alaine remained at the rail.

The cold entered through her sleeves.

---

The path disappeared behind the shoulder. Mist lay over the lower bends.

There was also this: Sela could not remain and continue as herself. Every refusal had become a teaching. Every doubt had become proof. The girl had tried silence and the silence had gathered followers.

East offered no safety. It only offered distance.

Alaine knew how useful distance could be.

She tightened her fingers on the stone.

The order to stop Sela would protect the house. Letting her go might protect it better.

One choice might also be mercy.

Alaine did not try to name which.

---

The sky paled.

The novice at the gate was looking up at her.

He had seen the wicket open. He stood with the bar half raised, waiting. Seventeen, perhaps. His face was white in the first light.

Alaine looked down.

She lowered one hand, slowly.

The boy replaced the bar and returned to his post.

He spoke of that gesture only near the end of his life. His granddaughter wrote the account. The house later disputed her dates.

---

Alaine stayed on the gallery.

The bell rope moved below her before the novice pulled it. Wind, perhaps. The first note came weak and had to be struck again.

A kitchen door opened. Someone carried out a pail of ash and left a grey line across the yard where the bottom leaked. Two novices crossed it without noticing. A third stopped to wipe the stones with her shoe and made the mark wider.

The guard at the wicket changed. The new boy yawned while receiving the bar, then looked ashamed when he saw Alaine above him.

She had not slept. Her left hand had gone numb against the rail. She shifted it once and pins of pain ran to the elbow.

No one below knew which order had not been given. They saw only the Mother keeping watch before dawn.

The distinction could not be corrected from the gallery.

She could no longer see the path beyond the shoulder, but she watched it.

Novices entered the yard one by one. They saw their Mother above them, still and wakeful in the dawn. One bowed. Another began the morning response before the bell.

Alaine did not correct him.

Her hands remained on the cold stone until the light reached the gate.

## Chapter Twenty-Seven
### East

Sela took the third way.

The old slip showed pale in the dark. New turf covered the grave, laid square and already browning at the edges.

She stopped beside it.

Nothing came. Wind moved over the exposed rock.

Sela tightened the strap on her bundle and climbed.

---

Merta sat at the elbow.

She wore her good coat. Col’s coat was not with her.

Sela stopped below the stone. Mist crossed the path between them.

“Third way,” Merta said. “Not the middle.”

“Who told you?”

Merta looked down the valley. After a while she moved a little to one side on the stone.

Sela could not sit. If she bent her knees, she might not stand again.

“Merta—”

“Go on.”

Sela went.

She did not look back.

The valley accounts place Merta in her house through the following spring. Some keep her there another year. All agree that when she left, Col’s coat remained behind.

---

The neck was worse than Fen had said.

Ice filled the cracks in the rock. Twice Sela went to her knees. The second time she stayed there until feeling returned to her fingers.

A goat would have found better ground. She had no goat to follow.

She kept the drop on her left and tested each hold before trusting it. At first light she reached the spine.

More mountains lay beyond.

No city. No light. No answer waiting on the ridge. Blue ranges faded into grey until the sky swallowed them.

Sela stood there until the cold forced her down.

---

The east face looked impassable.

She found a break in the scree and descended sitting, boots ahead of her, stones sliding under both heels. At the bottom the wind dropped.

Pines grew in a fold of the mountain, black and bent. Beneath the last trees was a track.

Deer might have made it. People might have. It was narrow enough that the difference did not matter.

Sela put one hand on a trunk.

She had not eaten since the previous evening. Behind her lay the only valley she knew. Ahead, the track vanished under branches.

Nothing had come on the neck. Nothing came now.

She went down anyway.

---

The Whitehart accounts end her journey there.

They agree that she was nineteen, carried Harl’s knife and had no verdict in the Ledger. They do not agree on what she heard before she crossed the mountains.

No account follows her beneath the pines.

---

An hour later the bell sounded behind her.

Sela had reached the stream. She stopped with one foot in the water.

The Bell of Silence had no clapper. Two men struck it from outside with a shod beam. It did not mark hours or call prayers. It was used for the dead.

Three strokes for a novice. Five for a Brother. Seven for a Mother.

The bell passed seven.

It continued.

Sela climbed back to where she could see a strip of sky through the branches. Each stroke crossed the mountain alone.

No Whitehart rite named a count for the Binder.

So the bell went on.

---

After it stopped, another sound rose from the valley.

Too far away for words. Thousands of voices, the house, the road and the camps reaching the mountain together.

Sela held the pine and listened.

She did not know Garron had died. She would hear it later in a village from someone who had the details wrong.

Whatever had happened behind her was larger than the Bell. It had already begun.

Sela did not turn around.

---

She followed the stream east.

The trees closed over it. Light went green, then dim.

Tidebreaker’s maps showed every ridge of the Silent Mountains. Beyond the last ridge, the paper ended.

The map ended.

She kept walking.
